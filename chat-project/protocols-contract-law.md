# Agency Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-AGENCY
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Special Contracts - Agency
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 182-238)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers the **contract of agency** - a relationship where one person (agent) acts on behalf of another (principal) with authority to affect the principal's legal relations with third parties.

**Key Principle**: "Qui facit per alium facit per se" (He who acts through another acts himself) - Principal bound by agent's authorized acts as if principal did them personally.

---

## Section 182: Agent and Principal Defined

```
"An 'agent' is a person employed to do any act for another, or to represent another in dealings with third persons. The person for whom such act is done, or who is so represented, is called the 'principal'."
```

**Essential Elements**:
1. **Two parties**: Principal and agent
2. **Authority**: Agent acts with principal's authority
3. **On behalf**: Agent acts for principal (not for himself)
4. **Legal relations**: Agent creates legal rights/obligations for principal

**Parties**:
1. **Principal**: Person on whose behalf agent acts
2. **Agent**: Person who acts on principal's behalf
3. **Third Party**: Person with whom agent deals on principal's behalf

---

## Creation of Agency (Sections 183-187)

**Agency created by**:

### 1. Express Agreement (Section 183)

**Express Authority**: Principal expressly appoints agent (oral or written)

**Example**: A appoints B as sales agent (written contract) → Express agency

**No formality required**: Need not be in writing (except power of attorney for immovable property under Registration Act)

### 2. Implied Agreement (Section 187)

**Implied Agency**: Conduct of parties indicates agency relationship

**Example**: 
- Wife purchases household necessaries on credit
- Shopkeeper assumes wife acts as husband's agent
- Husband liable (implied agency)

**Case: Suraj Narain Dube v. Raghunath Prasad (1954)**:
- Manager of joint family property
- Court held: Implied agency from conduct and circumstances

### 3. Necessity (Section 189)

**Agency of Necessity**: In emergencies, person may act as agent to protect another's property

**Requirements**:
1. **Emergency** (impossible to communicate with principal)
2. **Bona fide** (for principal's benefit)
3. **Necessary** (circumstances require immediate action)

**Examples**:
- Ship's captain sells cargo to prevent perishing
- Bailee incurs expense to preserve bailed goods

**Case: Great Northern Railway v. Swaffield (1874)**:
- Railway company fed horse (consignee didn't collect)
- Court held: Agency of necessity; entitled to reimbursement

### 4. Estoppel (Section 237)

**Holding Out**: If principal represents that person is his agent (or allows such representation) → Principal estopped from denying agency

**Requirements**:
1. **Representation** by principal (or principal's conduct allows representation)
2. **Third party relies** on representation
3. **Third party alters position** in good faith

**Example**:
- A (owner) allows B to act as if B is A's agent
- C (third party) contracts with B believing B is agent
- A bound (estopped from denying B's agency)

**Case: Pickering v. Busk (1812)**:
- Broker habitually acted for principal
- Third party relied on apparent authority
- Court held: Principal bound (estoppel)

### 5. Ratification (Sections 196-200)

**Ratification**: Principal retrospectively approves agent's unauthorized act

**Effect**: Unauthorized act becomes authorized from beginning (relates back)

**Section 196**: Ratification of unauthorized act = same effect as if authorized initially

**Requirements**:
1. **Act done on behalf** of principal (agent disclosed he acts for principal)
2. **Principal in existence** at time of act (company not yet incorporated cannot ratify)
3. **Principal competent** at time of act (principal must have capacity when act done)
4. **Full knowledge** of facts
5. **Entire transaction** ratified (cannot ratify part and reject part)
6. **Within reasonable time**
7. **Not illegal** act

**Section 200**: Ratification cannot harm third party who has acted on assumption act was unauthorized

**Example**:
- B (without authority) sells A's car to C for Rs. 5 lakhs
- A later ratifies sale (approves)
- Sale valid from time B sold (relates back)

**Case: Bolton Partners v. Lambert (1889)**:
- Agent accepted offer without authority
- Principal ratified after offer withdrawn
- Court held: Ratification relates back; contract binding from acceptance

---

## Extent of Agent's Authority (Sections 186-188)

### 1. Express Authority

**Stated in contract** (written or oral)

**Scope**: Agent can do what expressly authorized

### 2. Implied Authority (Section 188)

**Section 188**: "An agent having an authority to do an act has authority to do every lawful thing which is necessary in order to do such act."

**Includes**:
- **Incidental acts**: Necessary for performing express authority
- **Usual and customary acts**: In trade/business (unless principal restricts and third party knows)

**Example**: 
- Agent authorized to sell goods
- **Implied**: Authority to receive payment, give delivery, issue receipt (necessary/incidental)

**Case: Hely-Hutchinson v. Brayhead Ltd. (1968)**:
- Chairman had implied authority from conduct (usual authority for that position)
- Court held: Implied actual authority existed

### 3. Apparent/Ostensible Authority (Section 237)

**Apparent Authority**: Third party reasonably believes agent has authority (based on principal's conduct)

**Principal bound** even if agent lacks actual authority (if third party reasonably relied)

**Example**:
- A (principal) allows B to act as if B is sales manager
- C (customer) orders from B (believing B has authority)
- A bound (apparent authority)

**Case: Freeman & Lockyer v. Buckhurst Park Properties (1964)**:
- De facto director (not formally appointed)
- Company allowed him to act as managing director
- Court held: Apparent authority existed; company bound

### 4. Emergency Authority (Section 189)

**Agent can exceed authority in emergency** (if necessary to protect principal from loss)

**Requirements**:
- Cannot communicate with principal
- Bona fide for principal's benefit

## Duties of Agent to Principal

### 1. Duty to Follow Instructions (Section 211)

**Agent must conduct business** according to principal's directions

**Breach**: Liable for loss from non-compliance

**Example**: Principal instructs agent to sell at minimum Rs. 10 lakhs; agent sells at Rs. 8 lakhs → Agent liable for Rs. 2 lakhs loss

**Exception**: Emergency (Section 189)

### 2. Duty of Skill and Diligence (Section 212)

**Section 212**: "An agent is bound to conduct the business of his principal with as much skill as is generally possessed by persons engaged in similar business, unless the principal has notice of his want of skill."

**Standard**: Reasonable skill and care (as would possess by persons in that business)

**Professional Agent**: Higher standard (professional skill expected)

**Example**: Lawyer agent must exercise lawyer's skill; architect must exercise architect's skill

**Case: Turpin v. Bilton (1843)**:
- Stockbroker agent failed to obtain proper security
- Court held: Liable (breach of duty to exercise skill)

### 3. Duty to Render Accounts (Section 213)

**Agent must maintain** proper accounts; render on demand

**Duty**: Keep principal's money/property separate from own

**Example**: Sales agent must account for all collections; provide statements

### 4. Duty to Communicate (Section 214)

**Agent must communicate** material information acquired in course of agency

**"Material"**: Information affecting principal's decision

**Example**: Agent buying property for principal learns of defect → Must inform principal

**Case: Greenwood v. Martins Bank (1933)**:
- Bank (agent) knew of forgeries by customer's wife
- Failed to inform customer (principal)
- Court held: Breach of duty to communicate

### 5. Duty Not to Deal on Own Account (Section 215)

**Agent cannot deal on own account** (without principal's consent + full disclosure)

**Reason**: Conflict of interest

**Example**: Agent authorized to buy land; buys from self → Must disclose and get consent

**Breach**: Principal can:
- Rescind transaction, OR
- Claim profit from agent

**Case: Armstrong v. Jackson (1917)**:
- Stockbroker sold own shares to principal (disguised as market purchase)
- Court held: Breach of fiduciary duty; contract voidable

### 6. Duty Not to Make Secret Profit (Section 216)

**Agent must account** for all profits/benefits arising from agency

**Secret Profit**: Profit not disclosed to principal

**Examples**:
- Commission from third party (without principal's knowledge)
- Discount obtained (not shared with principal)

**Breach**: Principal can claim secret profit

**Case: Mahesan v. Malaysia Government Officers' Co-op Housing Society (1979)** (Privy Council):
- Director received bribe to award contract at inflated price
- Court held: Must account for bribe; principal can claim

### 7. Duty Not to Delegate (Section 190-194)

**Section 190**: "An agent cannot lawfully employ another to perform acts which he has expressly or impliedly undertaken to perform personally, unless by the ordinary custom of trade a sub-agent may, or from the nature of the agency, a sub-agent must, be employed."

**General Rule**: "Delegatus non potest delegare" (A delegate cannot delegate)

**Agent must act personally** (unless authorized to delegate)

**Exceptions** (when delegation allowed):
1. **Express authority** to delegate
2. **Trade custom** permits
3. **Nature of business** requires (ministerial acts)
4. **Emergency**

**Sub-agent**: Person appointed by agent (with authority)

**Liability**:
- **For sub-agent**: Agent liable if unauthorized delegation
- **Sub-agent's acts**: Principal bound if delegation authorized; otherwise agent liable (Section 191-192)

### 8. Duty Not to Put Himself in Position of Conflict (Fiduciary Duty)

**Fiduciary Relationship**: Agent owes loyalty to principal

**Cannot**:
- Act for opposite party (without disclosure + consent)
- Compete with principal
- Use confidential information for own benefit

---

## Rights of Agent

### 1. Right to Remuneration (Section 219)

**Agent entitled to agreed remuneration** (commission, fees)

**If no rate fixed**: Reasonable remuneration (quantum meruit)

**When Payable**:
- After completion of work (unless agreed otherwise)
- Effective cause: Agent's efforts caused result

**Section 220**: Agent not entitled if misconduct (breach of duty)

**Example**: Sales agent entitled to commission when sale completed (contract executed)

**Case: Luxor Ltd. v. Cooper (1941)**:
- Estate agent introduced buyer; sale fell through (not agent's fault)
- Contract said "commission on completion"
- Court held: No commission (sale didn't complete)

### 2. Right to Retainer (Section 217)

**Agent can retain** from principal's money:
- All sums due to agent (remuneration, advances, expenses)

**Retainer = Right to deduct** from principal's funds in agent's hands

**Example**: Agent collects Rs. 10 lakhs for principal; entitled to Rs. 1 lakh commission → Can retain Rs. 1 lakh; remit Rs. 9 lakhs

### 3. Right of Lien (Section 221)

**Particular Lien**: Agent can retain principal's goods/property until dues paid

**General Lien**: Certain agents (bankers, factors, wharfingers, attorneys) have general lien (for all dues, not just in respect of those goods)

**Lost if**: Agent parts with possession

**Example**: Commission agent (factor) can retain goods until all commissions/charges paid

### 4. Right to Indemnity (Section 222-225)

**Section 222**: "The employer of an agent is bound to indemnify him against the consequences of all lawful acts done by such agent in exercise of the authority conferred upon him."

**Indemnity covers**:
- Lawful acts within authority
- Acts in emergencies (exceeding authority for principal's benefit)
- Loss from following principal's instructions (even if acts result in liability)

**Example**:
- Principal instructs agent to sell goods
- Goods belong to third party (agent doesn't know)
- True owner sues agent
- Principal must indemnify agent

**Section 223**: Agent acting without authority (but principal ratifies) → Entitled to indemnity

**No Indemnity**: If agent's criminal/illegal act (Section 225)

### 5. Right to Compensation (Section 225)

**Agent entitled to compensation** for:
- Principal's breach of contract (wrongful termination)
- Principal's neglect/misconduct

**Example**: Principal terminates fixed-term agency before expiry (without cause) → Agent can claim damages

---

## Duties of Principal to Agent

### 1. Duty to Indemnify (Section 222-225)

As above (agent's right to indemnity)

### 2. Duty to Pay Remuneration (Section 219)

**Pay agreed remuneration** (or reasonable remuneration if no agreement)

### 3. Duty to Compensate for Injury (Section 225)

**Compensate agent** for injury caused by principal's:
- Neglect
- Want of skill

**Example**: Principal gives wrong information; agent acts on it and suffers loss → Principal must compensate

---

## Rights of Principal

### 1. Against Agent

**Principal can**:
- Sue for breach of duty (negligence, fraud, unauthorized acts)
- Claim secret profits
- Rescind contract (if agent dealt on own account)
- Claim damages for loss

### 2. Against Third Party

**Principal can sue/be sued** by third party (for contracts made by agent within authority)

**Exception**: Undisclosed principal (see below)

---

## Liability of Principal and Agent to Third Parties

### 1. Disclosed Principal

**Agent acts for known principal** (third party knows agent acts for someone)

**Liability**: **Principal liable** to third party (not agent)

**Example**: B (agent) contracts with C "on behalf of A (principal)" → A liable; B not liable

**Section 230**: Representative properly acting (within authority) not personally liable

### 2. Undisclosed Principal

**Agent acts for principal** but **doesn't disclose** principal's existence (third party thinks agent is principal)

**Liability**: 
- **Agent liable** (third party can sue agent)
- **Principal also liable** (third party can sue principal when discovered)

**Third party's election**: Can sue agent OR principal (not both)

**Section 232**: Third party can hold either agent or undisclosed principal liable

**Exception**: If third party specifically contracted with agent personally (no substitution of principal allowed)

**Example**:
- B buys goods from C (C thinks B is principal)
- B actually buying for A (undisclosed principal)
- C can sue B or A (when A discovered)

**Case: Sims v. Bond (1833)**:
- Agent contracted without disclosing principal
- Principal discovered later
- Court held: Third party can elect to sue agent or principal

### 3. Agent Exceeding Authority (Section 235)

**Agent exceeds authority** (or acts without authority) → **Agent personally liable**

**Principal not liable** (unless ratifies)

**Agent's liability**: Breach of warranty of authority

**Example**: Agent sells principal's car (without authority); principal refuses to honor → Agent liable to buyer (breach of warranty)

### 4. Foreign Principal (Section 234)

**Agent for foreign principal** → **Agent personally liable** (unless contract provides otherwise)

**Reason**: Difficult to enforce against foreign principal

### 5. Agent Acting for Principal with No Capacity (Section 235)

**If principal lacks capacity** (minor, unsound mind) → **Agent personally liable**

**Example**: Agent contracts on behalf of minor → Agent liable (minor's contract void)

---

## Termination of Agency (Sections 201-210)

**Agency terminates by**:

### 1. Agreement (Section 202)

**Mutual agreement** to terminate

**Revocation by principal** (Section 203):
- Can revoke anytime (if agency at will)
- Must compensate if revocation before end of fixed term

**Renunciation by agent** (Section 206):
- Can renounce anytime (if agency at will)
- Must give reasonable notice
- Liable for damages if renounces without notice or before fixed term

### 2. Performance (Section 202)

**Completion of business** for which agency created

**Example**: Agent hired to sell property; property sold → Agency ends

### 3. Lapse of Time (Section 202)

**Expiry of period** (if agency for fixed term)

**Example**: Agency for 1 year; 1 year passes → Agency ends

### 4. Death or Insanity (Section 209)

**Death or insanity** of principal or agent → Agency terminates

**Reason**: Personal nature of relationship

**Exception**: Coupled with interest (see below)

### 5. Insolvency of Principal (Section 209)

**Principal becomes insolvent** → Agency terminates

**Reason**: Principal's property vests in official receiver

### 6. Destruction of Subject Matter (Section 202)

**Subject matter** of agency destroyed → Agency terminates

**Example**: Agency to sell specific car; car destroyed → Agency ends

### 7. Principal Becoming Alien Enemy (Section 210)

**War breaks out** → Agency with alien enemy terminates

**Reason**: Illegal to contract with enemy

---

## Irrevocable Agency (Section 202 Proviso, Section 203)

**General Rule**: Principal can revoke agency anytime

**Exception**: **Agency coupled with interest** = Irrevocable

**"Coupled with Interest"**: Agent has interest in subject matter (not just interest in remuneration)

**Requirements**:
1. **Agent has proprietary interest** in subject matter
2. **Interest is to secure debt** or pre-existing obligation

**Example**:
- A borrows Rs. 10 lakhs from B
- A gives B power to sell A's property to recover loan
- B has interest in property (security for loan)
- Agency irrevocable

**NOT Coupled with Interest**:
- Mere right to commission (interest in remuneration only)

**Case: Smart v. Sandars (1848)**:
- Agent had interest in subject matter to secure debt
- Court held: Agency irrevocable (coupled with interest)

**Section 202 Proviso**: Agency cannot be terminated to agent's prejudice if coupled with interest

**Section 203**: Principal cannot revoke if agency coupled with interest (unless agent consents)

---

## Del Credere Agent (Section 204)

**Del Credere Agent**: Agent who guarantees that third parties (customers) will pay

**For Guarantee**: Gets extra commission (del credere commission)

**Liability**: If customer defaults → Del credere agent pays principal

**Example**: Sales agent guarantees customer will pay for goods sold on credit; customer defaults → Agent pays

**Nature**: Agent + Surety (guarantee)

**Section 204**: Del credere agent liable to principal if third party defaults

---

## Sub-Agent (Sections 190-195)

**Sub-Agent**: Person appointed by agent (with authority)

### Named Sub-Agent (Section 194)

**Agent nominates** person to act under principal's direction → **Principal and nominated person = principal-agent** (original agent drops out)

**Example**: A appoints B as agent; B nominates C (with A's consent); C acts under A's direction → A-C is principal-agent

### Substituted Agent (Section 191)

**Agent substitutes** another person (with authority) → **Principal and substituted agent = principal-agent** (original agent responsible for selection)

**Original agent's liability**: For selection (if incompetent/dishonest); not for substituted agent's acts (if properly selected)

### Sub-Agent Proper (Section 190-192)

**Agent delegates** with authority → **Sub-agent is agent's agent** (not principal's agent directly)

**Liability**:
- **Agent liable** to principal for sub-agent's acts
- **Sub-agent liable** to agent (not principal) - Section 193

**Exception**: Section 192 - Sub-agent represents principal for acts in ordinary course (in respect of third parties)

**Example**: Lawyer appoints junior lawyer (sub-agent) to draft document → Lawyer liable to principal for sub-agent's negligence

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Ratification

**Facts**:
- B (without authority) sells A's property to C for Rs. 50 lakhs
- Market value Rs. 55 lakhs (good price)
- A approves sale (ratifies) after 2 days
- C tries to withdraw (claims no contract)

**Analysis**:
- **Section 196**: Ratification relates back to time of act
- **B acted on behalf of A**: Yes (disclosed he acted for A)
- **A's knowledge**: Full knowledge (knew terms)
- **Entire transaction**: A ratified entire sale
- **Reasonable time**: 2 days reasonable
- **Result**: Contract valid from time B sold (C bound; cannot withdraw)

### Example 2: Agent Exceeding Authority

**Facts**:
- A authorizes B to sell car for minimum Rs. 5 lakhs
- B sells to C for Rs. 4.5 lakhs (exceeded authority)
- A refuses to deliver car
- C sues

**Analysis**:
- **B exceeded authority**: Sold below minimum
- **A not bound**: No ratification; transaction outside authority (Section 235)
- **B's liability**: Personally liable to C (breach of warranty of authority)
- **C's remedy**: Sue B for damages (cannot enforce against A)

### Example 3: Secret Profit

**Facts**:
- A (principal) instructs B (agent) to buy property
- B negotiates with C (seller); obtains 10% discount
- B buys at full price (keeps 10% discount as secret profit)

**Analysis**:
- **Section 216**: Agent must account for all benefits
- **Secret profit**: 10% discount not disclosed
- **Breach**: Fiduciary duty violated
- **A's remedies**:
  - Claim 10% discount from B
  - Dismiss B (terminate agency)
  - Sue for damages
- **Result**: B must pay A the secret profit

### Example 4: Undisclosed Principal

**Facts**:
- A instructs B to buy goods from C (don't disclose A's identity)
- B buys from C (C thinks B is principal)
- A later revealed as true principal
- Goods defective; who can sue C?

**Analysis**:
- **Undisclosed principal**: C didn't know A existed
- **Section 232**: Third party can sue agent or undisclosed principal (when discovered)
- **A's right**: Can sue C (even though undisclosed)
- **B's right**: Also can sue C (as apparent principal)
- **C's right**: Elect to sue A or B (not both)

### Example 5: Irrevocable Agency

**Facts**:
- A borrows Rs. 20 lakhs from B (bank)
- A gives B power of attorney to sell A's property (if A defaults)
- Loan secured by property
- A tries to revoke power of attorney before loan repaid

**Analysis**:
- **Agency coupled with interest**: Yes (B has security interest in property)
- **Section 203**: Cannot revoke if coupled with interest
- **Result**: A cannot revoke (power of attorney irrevocable until loan repaid)

### Example 6: Del Credere Agent

**Facts**:
- A (manufacturer) appoints B (sales agent)
- B guarantees customer payments (del credere agent)
- Extra 5% commission for guarantee
- B sells Rs. 10 lakhs goods to C (customer)
- C defaults; doesn't pay

**Analysis**:
- **Del credere agent**: B guaranteed C's payment
- **Section 204**: B liable to pay A (if C defaults)
- **Result**: B must pay A Rs. 10 lakhs (then B can sue C)

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Assuming Agent Always Personally Liable

**Mistake**: Thinking agent liable to third party for contracts made on principal's behalf

**Correct**: 
- **Disclosed principal**: Agent NOT liable (principal liable)
- **Undisclosed principal**: Agent liable (third party's election)
- **Exceeded authority**: Agent liable

### 2. Delegating Without Authority

**Mistake**: Agent delegates to sub-agent without principal's consent

**Correct**: Cannot delegate unless authorized (Section 190); agent liable for unauthorized sub-agent's acts

### 3. Not Disclosing Secret Profits

**Mistake**: Agent thinks can keep commission from third party (if helps principal get good deal)

**Correct**: Must disclose ALL benefits/profits to principal (Section 216); secret profit = breach

### 4. Principal Revoking Agency Coupled with Interest

**Mistake**: Principal tries to revoke power of attorney given as security

**Correct**: Cannot revoke if coupled with interest (Section 203) unless agent consents or debt paid

### 5. Confusing Del Credere with Surety

**Mistake**: Treating del credere agent same as guarantor

**Correct**: 
- **Del credere**: Agent + guarantor (one person; one contract)
- **Guarantee**: Three parties (creditor, debtor, surety; separate contract)

# Bailment and Pledge Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-BAILMENT-PLEDGE
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Special Contracts - Bailment & Pledge
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 148-181)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers two related special contracts:
1. **Bailment** (Sections 148-171): Temporary transfer of possession of goods for a purpose
2. **Pledge** (Sections 172-181): Special type of bailment as security for payment of debt/performance of promise

**Key Principle**: Bailment involves **possession transfer** (not ownership); ownership remains with bailor.

---

## PART A: CONTRACT OF BAILMENT

### Section 148: Bailment Defined

```
"A 'bailment' is the delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall, when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them. The person delivering the goods is called the 'bailor'. The person to whom they are delivered is called the 'bailee'."
```

**Essential Elements**:
1. **Delivery of possession**: Goods transferred from bailor to bailee
2. **Purpose**: Specific purpose for bailment
3. **Contract**: Agreement between parties (express or implied)
4. **Return**: Goods to be returned when purpose accomplished (or disposed per bailor's direction)
5. **Same goods**: Identical goods returned (not equivalent)

**Parties**:
1. **Bailor**: Person delivering goods (owner or authorized person)
2. **Bailee**: Person receiving goods (takes possession, not ownership)

---

### Types of Delivery

#### 1. Actual Delivery

**Physical transfer** of possession

**Example**: A hands car keys to B (valet parking) → Actual delivery

#### 2. Constructive Delivery

**Symbolic transfer** without physical handing over

**Examples**:
- Transfer of keys (when goods not movable)
- Transfer of documents of title (warehouse receipt, bill of lading)
- Attornment: Third party holding goods acknowledges holds for bailee

**Section 149**: "The delivery to the bailee may be made by doing anything which has the effect of putting the goods in the possession of the intended bailee or of any person authorized to hold them on his behalf."

---

### Types of Bailment

#### 1. Based on Consideration

##### a) Gratuitous Bailment

**No consideration** (free service)

**Example**: A lends book to friend B (no charge) → Gratuitous bailment

**Bailee's Duty**: Take care as would of own goods (Section 151)

##### b) Bailment for Reward (Non-Gratuitous)

**Consideration present** (payment/benefit)

**Example**: A parks car at paid parking (B) → Bailment for reward

**Bailee's Duty**: Higher standard of care (Section 152)

#### 2. Based on Benefit

##### a) Sole Benefit of Bailor

**Example**: Gratuitous custody (A deposits goods with B for safekeeping; no charge)

**Bailee's liability**: Minimal (only gross negligence)

##### b) Sole Benefit of Bailee

**Example**: Gratuitous loan (A lends car to B free)

**Bailee's liability**: Higher (must take extraordinary care)

##### c) Mutual Benefit

**Example**: Paid storage, rental, transport

**Bailee's liability**: Reasonable care (as prudent person)

#### 3. Based on Purpose

**Six Classes** (Section 148 Explanation):

1. **Deposit** (safekeeping): A deposits jewelry with B (bank locker)
2. **Commodatum** (gratuitous loan): A lends book to B
3. **Hire** (paid use): A rents car from B
4. **Pawn/Pledge** (security): A pledges gold to B (for loan)
5. **Delivery for work** (services): A gives watch to B (for repair)
6. **Gratuitous services**: A delivers goods to B for free transport

---

### Duties of Bailee

#### 1. Duty to Take Reasonable Care (Sections 151-152)

**Section 151**: Gratuitous bailment
```
"In all cases of bailment the bailee is bound to take as much care of the goods bailed to him as a man of ordinary prudence would, under similar circumstances, take of his own goods of the same bulk, quality and value as the goods bailed."
```

**Section 152**: Bailment for reward
```
"The bailee, in the absence of any special contract, is not responsible for the loss, destruction or deterioration of the thing bailed, if he has taken the amount of care of it described in section 151."
```

**Standard**: **Reasonable care** (man of ordinary prudence)

**NOT insurer**: Bailee not liable for loss despite care (force majeure, etc.)

**Exception**: Liability even with care if:
- Special contract (bailee guaranteed safety)
- Bailee's deliberate act/negligence

**Case: Chaudhary Dharampal v. Allahabad Bank (1957)**:
- Bank locker; theft by breaking bank wall
- Court held: Bank not liable (took reasonable care; theft by extraordinary means)

**But**: If inadequate security → Bank liable

#### 2. Not to Make Unauthorized Use (Section 154)

**Bailee must use goods only for agreed purpose**

**Breach**: Unauthorized use → Bailee liable for all loss (even without negligence)

**Example**: 
- A hires car to B for city use
- B drives to mountains (unauthorized)
- Car damaged (no negligence)
- B liable (unauthorized use)

**Case: Lilley v. Doubleday (1881)**:
- Horse hired for ride; bailee used for racing
- Horse injured during race
- Court held: Bailee liable (unauthorized use)

#### 3. Not to Mix Goods (Section 155-157)

**Section 155**: **Without consent**
- If bailee mixes goods with own goods → Liable for loss
- If goods separable → Bailee bears separation cost + compensates loss

**Section 156**: **With consent**
- If mixed with consent → Both have proportionate share in mixture
- Loss borne proportionately

**Section 157**: Difficulty in separation
- Bailee must compensate bailor for loss from separation difficulty

#### 4. Return Goods (Section 160)

**Bailee must return goods** when:
- Purpose accomplished, OR
- Time period expired, OR
- Bailor demands (if gratuitous bailment)

**Identical Goods**: Must return same goods (not equivalent - except fungible goods with consent)

**Section 161**: If bailee doesn't return on demand → Liable for loss even by unavoidable accident

#### 5. Return to Right Person (Section 160)

**Return to bailor** OR person authorized by bailor

**Section 164**: Bailee's liability if delivers to wrong person (even if in good faith)

**Exception**: Delivery to person with better title (bailee protected)

#### 6. Return Accretion/Profit (Section 163)

**Any increase/profit** from goods belongs to bailor

**Example**: Cow bailed; calf born → Calf belongs to bailor

---

### Rights of Bailee

#### 1. Right to Expenses and Remuneration (Section 158)

**Bailment for reward**: Bailee entitled to remuneration agreed

**Extraordinary expenses** (preserving goods, defending bailor's title): Bailee can claim from bailor

**Example**: Goods deposited with B; third party claims ownership; B incurs legal costs defending A's title → B can claim costs from A

#### 2. Right of Lien (Section 170)

**Particular Lien**: Bailee can retain goods until:
- Remuneration paid, AND
- Expenses incurred reimbursed

**Only for amount due** in respect of those goods (not other debts)

**Example**: A repairs B's car; B doesn't pay → A can retain car (lien for repair charges)

**Lost if**: 
- Bailee parts with possession (voluntarily)
- Payment/tender of dues

#### 3. Right to Sue Third Party (Section 180)

**If third party wrongfully deprives bailee of use/damages goods** → Bailee can sue

**Bailee's right**: Sue in own name (as if bailee were owner)

**Example**: Car bailed to A; C negligently damages car → A (bailee) can sue C for damages

#### 4. Right to Compensation for Breach (Section 168)

**If bailor breaches contract** (e.g., terminates bailment wrongfully) → Bailee can claim damages

**Example**: Goods hired for 3 months; bailor demands return after 1 month → Bailee can claim damages for remaining 2 months

---

### Duties of Bailor

#### 1. Disclosure of Known Defects (Section 150)

**Gratuitous bailment**: Bailor must disclose faults known to bailor (causing injury to bailee)

**Bailment for reward**: Bailor must disclose faults **whether known or not** (should have known with ordinary diligence)

**Liability**: Damages for loss arising from non-disclosure

**Example**:
- A lends defective car to B (gratuitous)
- A knows brakes defective; doesn't disclose
- B injured due to brake failure
- A liable (non-disclosure of known defect)

**For Reward**:
- A rents car to B
- Brakes defective (A didn't check; reasonable inspection would reveal)
- B injured
- A liable (should have known)

#### 2. Bear Extraordinary Expenses (Section 158)

**Bailor must reimburse** bailee for extraordinary expenses incurred for preservation of goods

#### 3. Indemnify Bailee (Section 164)

**If bailor has no right to bail goods** OR gives wrong instructions → Must indemnify bailee for loss

**Example**: 
- A bails B's goods to C (A not authorized)
- B (true owner) sues C
- A must indemnify C for loss

#### 4. Receive Back Goods (Gratuitous Bailment)

**Gratuitous bailment**: Bailor must take back goods when bailment terminates

**If bailor doesn't**: Liable for loss to bailee

---

### Rights of Bailor

#### 1. Enforce Bailee's Duties

**Sue bailee** for breach of duty (negligence, unauthorized use, etc.)

#### 2. Terminate Gratuitous Bailment (Section 159)

**Bailor can demand return** anytime (if gratuitous bailment)

**But**: Liable for damages if termination causes loss to bailee

**Example**: A lends B machine (gratuitous); B using for important project; A demands return midway → A liable for B's project loss

#### 3. Claim Damages from Third Party (Section 180)

**If third party wrongfully damages bailed goods** → Bailor can sue for damages (to ownership interest)

---

### Termination of Bailment

**Bailment ends when**:

1. **Purpose accomplished**: Task completed
2. **Time expired**: Period ended
3. **Destruction of goods**: Subject matter perished
4. **Act inconsistent with bailment**: Bailee acts as owner (sells goods)
5. **Death** (if personal skill/confidence essential - Section 162)
6. **Bailor's demand** (gratuitous bailment - Section 159)

**Section 162**: Death terminates if contract personal; otherwise, legal representatives succeed

---

### Finder of Goods (Sections 168-169)

**Finder of lost goods** = Bailee

**Section 168**: Finder has:
- **Duty**: Same as bailee (take reasonable care; restore to true owner)
- **Right**: Particular lien for expenses + compensation for preserving/finding owner

**Section 169**: Finder can sell if:
1. **Goods perishable**, OR
2. **Owner cannot be found with reasonable effort**, OR
3. **Owner refuses to pay** finder's lawful charges (and charges ≥ 2/3 of goods' value)

**Sale Procedure**: Public notice required (if possible)

**Case: Parker v. British Airways Board (1982)**:
- Finder of lost bracelet at airport
- Court held: Finder has rights against all (except true owner)
- Better title than possessor of premises (airline)

---

## PART B: CONTRACT OF PLEDGE

### Section 172: Pledge Defined

```
"The bailment of goods as security for payment of a debt or performance of a promise is called 'pledge'. The bailor is in this case called the 'pawnor', and the bailee is called the 'pawnee'."
```

**Pledge = Special bailment** (security purpose)

**Parties**:
1. **Pawnor**: Person delivering goods (borrower)
2. **Pawnee**: Person receiving goods as security (lender)

**Purpose**: Security for debt/promise

**Example**: A borrows Rs. 1 lakh from B; pledges gold ornaments as security → A is pawnor; B is pawnee

---

### Essential Elements of Pledge

1. **Delivery of possession**: Pawnor to pawnee (actual or constructive)
2. **Security**: For debt or performance of promise
3. **Return**: Goods returned when debt paid/promise performed
4. **Same goods**: Specific goods pledged (not fungible substitutes)

**Section 173**: Constructive delivery sufficient

**Case: Official Assignee of Bombay v. Shri Suraj Mal Ram Nath (1961)** (Supreme Court):
- Pledge by delivery of document of title valid
- Constructive possession sufficient

---

### Who Can Pledge?

**General Rule**: **Owner** or person with authority

**Section 178**: **Non-owner can pledge** if:

#### 1. Mercantile Agent (Section 178)

**Mercantile agent** in possession with owner's consent → Can pledge (if in ordinary course of business)

**Pawnee's protection**: If pawnee acts in good faith + no notice of agent's lack of authority

**Example**: A (owner) gives goods to B (agent) for sale; B pledges to C (lender) → C gets good title (if in good faith)

#### 2. Person with Limited Interest (Section 179)

**Person with limited ownership** (co-owner, joint owner) → Can pledge (to extent of his interest)

**Example**: A and B co-own goods; A pledges his 50% share → Valid pledge of A's interest

#### 3. Seller/Buyer in Possession (Section 178A)

**Seller in possession after sale** OR **buyer in possession before property passes** → Can pledge

**Pawnee protected** if bona fide and no notice

**Case: Lallan Prasad v. Rahmat Ali (1967)** (Supreme Court):
- Buyer obtained possession before paying
- Buyer pledged goods
- Court held: Pledge valid (buyer in possession with seller's consent)

---

### Duties of Pawnee

#### 1. Take Reasonable Care (Section 150-152)

**Same as bailee**: Reasonable care of pledged goods

**Liable for loss** due to negligence

#### 2. Not Make Unauthorized Use (Section 154)

**No use of pledged goods** (unless authorized)

**Example**: Pawnee pledges car; cannot drive car (unless pawnor permits)

#### 3. Return on Payment (Section 177)

**Pawnee must return goods** when:
- Debt paid, OR
- Promise performed

**If pawnee refuses** after payment → Liable for damages

#### 4. Account for Surplus on Sale (Section 176)

**If pawnee sells pledged goods** (on default) → Must account for surplus (after deducting debt + expenses)

**Example**:
- Debt Rs. 1 lakh
- Pledged goods sold for Rs. 1.5 lakhs
- Pawnee deducts Rs. 1 lakh (debt) + Rs. 10,000 (sale expenses)
- Must return Rs. 40,000 (surplus) to pawnor

#### 5. Give Reasonable Notice Before Sale (Section 176)

**Pawnee must give reasonable notice** before selling pledged goods

**Purpose**: Allow pawnor to redeem

---

### Rights of Pawnee

#### 1. Right of Retainer (Section 174)

**Pawnee can retain goods** until:
- Debt paid, AND
- Interest paid, AND
- Expenses incurred reimbursed

**Particular lien**: For amount due in respect of pledged goods

#### 2. Right of Retainer for Subsequent Advances (Section 175)

**If pawnor makes subsequent borrowings** from same pawnee → Pawnee can retain for all debts (if agreed)

**Example**: 
- A pledges goods for Rs. 1 lakh loan (Loan 1)
- Later borrows Rs. 50,000 more (Loan 2) from same pawnee
- If agreed, pawnee can retain until Rs. 1.5 lakhs paid

#### 3. Right to Extraordinary Expenses (Section 175A)

**Pawnee entitled to reimbursement** for extraordinary expenses (preserving pledged goods)

#### 4. Right of Sale (Section 176)

**On pawnor's default**, pawnee can:
1. **Sue for debt** (retain goods as collateral), OR
2. **Sell goods** (after reasonable notice)

**Sale Procedure**:
- Reasonable notice to pawnor
- Public sale (unless contract provides otherwise)
- Account for surplus

**Pawnee's right**: Choose between suit or sale (cannot do both for same debt)

**Example**:
- Debt Rs. 1 lakh; pawnor defaults
- Pawnee can:
  - (a) Sue for Rs. 1 lakh (keep goods as security), OR
  - (b) Sell goods (after notice); apply proceeds to debt

#### 5. Sue Third Party (Section 180)

**If third party wrongfully damages pledged goods** → Pawnee can sue (as bailee)

---

### Rights of Pawnor

#### 1. Right to Redeem (Section 177)

**Pawnor can redeem** pledged goods by:
- Paying debt, AND
- Interest, AND
- Expenses

**Right survives default** (as long as goods not sold)

**Example**: 
- Loan due June 1; pawnor defaults
- Pawnee gives notice of sale (to be sold July 1)
- Pawnor can redeem anytime before sale (by paying all dues)

#### 2. Right to Surplus (Section 176)

**After sale**, pawnor entitled to surplus (sale proceeds - debt - expenses)

#### 3. Pawnor's Right to Limit Pawnee's Sale (Section 176 Proviso)

**If default is only part payment** → Pawnee cannot sell entire goods (only proportionate part)

**Example**:
- Debt Rs. 1 lakh; pledged goods worth Rs. 2 lakhs
- Pawnor pays Rs. 50,000; defaults on Rs. 50,000
- Pawnee cannot sell entire goods (worth Rs. 2 lakhs); can sell only proportionate part (worth Rs. 1 lakh after accounting for Rs. 50,000 paid)

**Interpretation**: Controversial; some courts allow full sale with accounting to pawnor

---

### Pledge vs. Mortgage

| Aspect | Pledge | Mortgage |
|--------|--------|----------|
| **Subject** | Movable goods only | Immovable property (land, buildings) |
| **Possession** | Pawnee gets possession | Mortgagee usually doesn't get possession |
| **Transfer of Ownership** | No (only possession) | Limited ownership transferred |
| **Right to Use** | Pawnee cannot use (unless authorized) | Mortgagee cannot use (unless possession mortgage) |
| **Sale on Default** | Can sell after notice (Section 176) | Must file suit for foreclosure/sale (unless document permits) |
| **Statute** | Contract Act (Sections 172-181) | Transfer of Property Act 1882 |

---

### Pledge vs. Hypothecation

| Aspect | Pledge | Hypothecation |
|--------|--------|---------------|
| **Possession** | Pawnee gets possession | Borrower retains possession |
| **Nature** | Bailment (actual security) | Equitable charge (no bailment) |
| **Sale Rights** | Pawnee can sell (Section 176) | Creditor cannot sell directly (must take possession first) |
| **Example** | Gold loan (gold deposited with bank) | Vehicle loan (vehicle with borrower) |

---

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Bailment - Unauthorized Use

**Facts**:
- A rents car to B for city use (within 50 km radius)
- B drives to hill station (200 km away; unauthorized use)
- Car damaged in accident (no negligence of B)
- Repair cost Rs. 1 lakh

**Analysis**:
- **Section 154**: Unauthorized use → Bailee liable for all loss (even without negligence)
- **Result**: B liable for Rs. 1 lakh (even though accident not due to B's fault)

### Example 2: Bailment - Mixing Goods

**Facts**:
- A deposits 100 kg rice (premium quality) with B for storage
- B mixes A's rice with own rice (ordinary quality) without A's consent
- Mixture impossible to separate

**Analysis**:
- **Section 155**: Mixing without consent → Bailee liable for loss
- **Inseparable mixture**: A can claim compensation for premium rice value
- **Result**: B must compensate A (difference between premium and ordinary rice value)

### Example 3: Finder of Goods

**Facts**:
- A finds lost mobile phone at railway station
- A spends Rs. 5,000 (advertising, storage, attempting to find owner)
- True owner C comes forward after 3 months
- C refuses to pay A's expenses

**Analysis**:
- **Section 168**: Finder = bailee; has particular lien for expenses
- **A's rights**: 
  - Retain phone until C pays Rs. 5,000 (expenses)
  - If C refuses + expenses ≥ 2/3 of phone value → A can sell (Section 169)
- **Result**: A has lien; can retain phone or sell if conditions met

### Example 4: Pledge - Sale on Default

**Facts**:
- A pledges gold ornaments (value Rs. 5 lakhs) to B for loan of Rs. 2 lakhs
- Loan due June 1; A defaults
- B gives notice (30 days) of intention to sell
- A doesn't pay; B sells ornaments for Rs. 4.8 lakhs (after public auction)
- Sale expenses: Rs. 20,000

**Analysis**:
- **Section 176**: Pawnee can sell after notice
- **Notice given**: 30 days (reasonable)
- **Sale proceeds**: Rs. 4.8 lakhs
- **Deductions**: Rs. 2 lakhs (debt) + Rs. 20,000 (expenses) = Rs. 2.2 lakhs
- **Surplus**: Rs. 4.8 lakhs - Rs. 2.2 lakhs = Rs. 2.6 lakhs
- **Result**: B must pay Rs. 2.6 lakhs to A (pawnor)

### Example 5: Pledge by Non-Owner

**Facts**:
- A (owner) gives goods to B (mercantile agent) for sale
- B pledges goods to C (lender) for personal loan (without A's knowledge)
- C acts in good faith; no notice of B's lack of authority

**Analysis**:
- **Section 178**: Mercantile agent in possession with owner's consent can pledge
- **C's protection**: If (1) B is mercantile agent, (2) in possession with A's consent, (3) C in good faith, (4) ordinary course of business
- **Result**: C gets valid pledge (good title); A cannot reclaim goods from C (but can sue B for breach)

### Example 6: Bailment for Reward - Reasonable Care

**Facts**:
- A deposits jewelry in B's bank locker (paid service)
- Thieves break into bank; cut locker wall with gas cutter; steal jewelry
- Bank had: (1) Security guards, (2) CCTV, (3) Alarm system
- Theft highly sophisticated (gas cutter; avoided alarms)

**Analysis**:
- **Section 152**: Bailee for reward must take reasonable care
- **Not insurer**: Not liable for loss despite care
- **Reasonable care**: Bank had adequate security (guards, CCTV, alarm)
- **Extraordinary means**: Thieves used sophisticated method
- **Result**: Bank NOT liable (took reasonable care; loss due to extraordinary means beyond prevention)

**But**: If inadequate security (e.g., no guard, broken locks) → Bank liable (negligence)

---

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Confusing Bailment with Sale

**Mistake**: Treating transfer of possession as transfer of ownership

**Correct**: 
- **Bailment**: Possession transfers (ownership remains with bailor)
- **Sale**: Ownership transfers (Section 4 Sale of Goods Act)

### 2. Assuming Bailee is Insurer

**Mistake**: Expecting bailee liable for all loss (even if bailee took care)

**Correct**: Bailee liable only for negligence (Section 152); not liable for unavoidable loss despite care

### 3. Using Bailed Goods Without Permission

**Mistake**: Bailee uses goods for different purpose (thinking minor change acceptable)

**Correct**: Unauthorized use = liable for all loss (even without negligence - Section 154)

### 4. Pawnee Selling Without Notice

**Mistake**: Pawnee sells pledged goods immediately on default (without notice to pawnor)

**Correct**: Must give reasonable notice (Section 176); pawnor has right to redeem until sale

### 5. Pawnor Assuming Cannot Redeem After Default

**Mistake**: Thinking once defaulted, pledge is lost (no redemption possible)

**Correct**: Pawnor can redeem anytime before sale (by paying all dues - Section 177)

# Contract Breach Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-BREACH
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Contract Performance - Breach
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Section 39, 73-75), Sale of Goods Act 1930 (Section 11-13)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **breach of contract** - the failure to perform contractual obligations without lawful excuse, and the rights and remedies available to the aggrieved party.

**Key Principle**: Breach entitles innocent party to damages and may allow termination of contract.

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 39: Effect of Refusal to Accept Offer of Performance

```
"When a party to a contract has refused to perform, or disabled himself from performing, his promise in its entirety, the promisee may put an end to the contract, unless he has signified, by words or conduct, his acquiescence in its continuance."
```

**Breach → Right to Terminate** (if material breach)

---

## Types of Breach

### 1. Actual Breach

**Definition**: Failure to perform when performance is due

**Elements**:
1. **Performance due**: Time for performance arrived
2. **Failure to perform**: Party does not perform (wholly or partially)
3. **No lawful excuse**: No valid excuse (impossibility, force majeure)

**Example**: Seller fails to deliver goods on agreed delivery date → Actual breach

### 2. Anticipatory Breach

**Definition**: Repudiation of contract before time for performance

**Elements**:
1. **Before performance due**: Repudiation occurs before time for performance
2. **Express or Implied repudiation**:
   - **Express**: Party states they will not perform
   - **Implied**: Party's conduct makes performance impossible

**Landmark Case: Hochster v. De La Tour (1853)**:
- Defendant hired plaintiff as courier for European tour (June 1)
- On May 11, defendant wrote refusing plaintiff's services
- Plaintiff sued on May 22 (before June 1)
- Court held: Plaintiff can sue immediately upon repudiation (anticipatory breach)

**Example - Express**:
- A contracts to deliver goods on Dec 31
- On Dec 1, A writes: "I will not deliver goods"
- Anticipatory breach

**Example - Implied**:
- A contracts to sell specific car to B (delivery Jan 15)
- On Jan 5, A sells same car to C
- A disabled from performing → Anticipatory breach

---

## Rights of Innocent Party (Anticipatory Breach)

**Case: Frost v. Knight (1872)** - Leading case on anticipatory breach

**Innocent Party's Options**:

### Option 1: Treat Contract as Discharged (Accept Repudiation)

**Immediate Action**:
- Treat contract as ended
- Sue immediately for damages
- No need to wait for performance date

**Advantages**:
- Immediate remedy
- Mitigation of losses (can make alternative arrangements)

**Example**: A repudiates on Dec 1; B can sue immediately (no need to wait till Dec 31)

### Option 2: Affirm Contract (Reject Repudiation)

**Wait and See**:
- Keep contract alive
- Wait till performance date
- If party still doesn't perform → Sue then

**Advantages**:
- Party may change mind and perform
- Innocent party can still earn consideration (if needs to complete own performance)

**Risks**:
- Supervening impossibility may occur (frustrating contract)
- Contract may become void before performance date

**Case: Avery v. Bowden (1855)**:
- Charterer repudiated charter party (ship hire)
- Shipowner waited
- Before performance date, Crimean War broke out (charter illegal)
- Court held: Contract frustrated; shipowner cannot sue (should have accepted repudiation immediately)

**Indian Position**: Both options available (affirm or accept repudiation)

---

## Materiality of Breach

**Not all breaches allow termination**

### Breach of Condition vs Breach of Warranty

**Sale of Goods Act 1930 (Sections 11-13)**: Distinguishes conditions and warranties

**Condition**: **Essential term** of contract
- Breach → Right to terminate + damages

**Warranty**: **Non-essential term** (collateral)
- Breach → Damages only (no right to terminate)

**Section 12 Sale of Goods Act**:

```
"A stipulation in a contract of sale with reference to goods which are the subject thereof may be a condition or a warranty.

A condition is a stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a right to treat the contract as repudiated.

A warranty is a stipulation collateral to the main purpose of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a claim for damages but not to a right to reject the goods and treat the contract as repudiated."
```

**Test**: Is term essential to main purpose?
- YES → Condition (breach allows termination)
- NO → Warranty (breach allows damages only)

**Example**:
- **Condition**: Sale of "2024 model car"; delivered 2020 model → Breach of condition (buyer can reject)
- **Warranty**: "Air conditioning works perfectly"; minor AC issue → Breach of warranty (damages only; cannot reject car)

**Case: Bettini v. Gye (1876)**:
- Opera singer contracted to arrive 6 days before performance for rehearsals
- Arrived 4 days before (2 days late for rehearsals)
- Court held: Breach of warranty (rehearsal time not essential; mere collateral); employer cannot terminate

**Case: Poussard v. Spiers (1876)**:
- Opera singer contracted to perform from opening night
- Fell ill; missed opening week
- Court held: Breach of condition (opening performances essential); employer can terminate

### Fundamental / Material Breach

**Fundamental Breach**: Breach going to root of contract

**Effect**: Repudiatory breach (innocent party can terminate)

**Test**: Does breach deprive innocent party of substantially entire benefit of contract?

**Case: Hong Kong Fir Shipping Co. v. Kawasaki Kisen Kaisha (1962)**:
- Ship charter for 24 months
- Ship unseaworthy for 20 weeks (delay)
- Court held: Not fundamental breach (charterer still got substantial benefit for remaining period)

**Innominate Terms**: Terms that can be condition or warranty depending on consequences of breach
- If breach deprives substantial benefit → Condition
- If breach minor → Warranty

---

## Consequences of Breach

### 1. Damages (Section 73)

**Section 73**: "When a contract has been broken, the party who suffers by such breach is entitled to receive, from the party who has broken the contract, compensation for any loss or damage caused to him thereby, which naturally arose in the usual course of things from such breach, or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach of it."

**Damages = Compensation** for loss

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-DAMAGES protocol (detailed coverage)

### 2. Specific Performance

**Specific Relief Act 1963**: Court can order specific performance (actual performance of contract)

**When Granted**: If damages inadequate remedy (e.g., sale of unique property, land)

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-SPECIFIC-PERFORMANCE protocol

### 3. Injunction

**Prohibitory Injunction**: Order restraining party from breaching negative covenant

**Example**: Contract not to compete; party competes → Court can grant injunction

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-INJUNCTION protocol

### 4. Quantum Meruit

**Quantum Meruit**: "As much as merited/earned"

**When Applicable**: If contract breached after party has partially performed → Can claim reasonable value of work done

**Example**: Builder contracted to build house for Rs. 50 lakhs; completed 50% when owner breaches → Builder can claim quantum meruit (reasonable value of 50% work)

### 5. Rescission

**Rescission**: Cancellation of contract + restoration to original position

**When Available**: Voidable contracts (fraud, misrepresentation, undue influence)

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-RESCISSION protocol

---

## Measure of Damages (Section 73)

### General Principle

**Compensatory Damages**: Put innocent party in position they would have been if contract performed

**Not Punitive**: Cannot punish breaching party (unlike criminal law)

### Rule in Hadley v. Baxendale (1854)

**Two-Limb Test** (incorporated in Section 73):

#### Limb 1: Natural Consequences

**Damages that "naturally arose in the usual course of things from such breach"**

**Example**: Contract to deliver goods on Jan 1; seller fails to deliver
- Buyer's loss of profit from resale (foreseeable) → Recoverable
- Buyer's extraordinary loss (unforeseeable) → NOT recoverable

#### Limb 2: Special Damages (Contemplated by Parties)

**Damages "which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach"**

**Example**: Buyer informs seller that goods urgently needed for specific contract; seller fails to deliver
- Buyer's loss from specific contract (seller knew) → Recoverable

**Case: Hadley v. Baxendale (1854)**:
- Mill owner's crankshaft broken; sent for repair
- Carrier delayed delivery of crankshaft to repair shop
- Mill stopped (loss of profit)
- Court held: Loss of profit NOT recoverable (carrier didn't know crankshaft was only one; mill would stop)

**Indian Application**:

**Case: Murlidhar v. Harishchandra (1962)**:
- Contract to supply jaggery (raw sugar)
- Supplier failed
- Buyer claimed loss of profit from reselling jaggery
- Court held: Loss of profit foreseeable; recoverable under Section 73

### Remoteness of Damages

**Proximate Cause**: Damages must flow directly from breach (not too remote)

**Case: Victoria Laundry v. Newman Industries (1949)**:
- Contract to sell boiler to laundry
- Delivery delayed
- Laundry claimed: (1) Normal loss of profit, (2) Loss of lucrative government contract
- Court held:
  - (1) Recoverable (foreseeable)
  - (2) NOT recoverable (seller didn't know about special government contract)

---

## Duty to Mitigate (Section 73 Proviso)

**Section 73 Proviso**: "Such compensation is not to be given for any remote and indirect loss or damage sustained by reason of the breach. Compensation for breach of contract is subject to the rule that the party complaining of the breach must take reasonable steps to mitigate the loss."

**Duty to Mitigate**: Innocent party must take reasonable steps to minimize loss

**Not Required**: Unreasonable steps or steps involving substantial cost/risk

**Example**: Contract to deliver goods on Jan 1; seller fails
- Buyer should buy goods from alternative source (reasonable mitigation)
- Buyer cannot claim excessive damages if unreasonably failed to mitigate

**Case: British Westinghouse v. Underground Electric Railways (1912)**:
- Defective turbines supplied
- Buyer replaced with more efficient turbines (which saved more than they cost)
- Court held: Damages reduced by savings from new turbines (duty to mitigate)

**Mitigation Examples**:

**Reasonable**:
- Finding alternative supplier
- Accepting similar performance (if offered)
- Re-letting property (if tenant breaches lease)

**Unreasonable** (Not required):
- Accepting substantially different performance
- Incurring substantial costs
- Taking unreasonable risks

---

## Liquidated Damages vs Penalty (Section 74)

**Section 74**: "When a contract has been broken, if a sum is named in the contract as the amount to be paid in case of such breach, or if the contract contains any other stipulation by way of penalty, the party complaining of the breach is entitled, whether or not actual damage or loss is proved to have been caused thereby, to receive from the party who has broken the contract reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount so named or, as the case may be, the penalty stipulated for."

**Two Concepts**:
1. **Liquidated Damages**: Genuine pre-estimate of loss
2. **Penalty**: Amount intended to penalize (not genuine estimate)

**English Law**: Penalty unenforceable; only liquidated damages enforceable

**Indian Law (Section 74)**: Both enforceable, but court awards **reasonable compensation** (not exceeding stipulated amount)

**Effect**:
- Liquidated damages clause → Ceiling (court can award up to that amount)
- Penalty clause → Ceiling (court can award reasonable compensation up to penalty amount)

**Case: Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Dass (1963)** (Supreme Court):
- Contract stipulated Rs. 10 per day for delay
- Actual loss minimal
- Court held: Can award reasonable compensation (not necessarily full Rs. 10/day if excessive)

**Case: Kailash Nath Associates v. DDA (2015)** (Supreme Court):
- Construction contract with liquidated damages clause
- Contractor delayed
- Supreme Court held: Liquidated damages are compensation (not penalty); court must determine if genuine pre-estimate

**Distinction**:
- **Liquidated damages**: Reasonable estimate of probable loss → Enforceable
- **Penalty**: Extravagant sum (in terrorem) → Enforceable but court reduces to reasonable compensation

---

## Exclusion / Limitation Clauses

**Exclusion Clause**: Clause excluding or limiting liability for breach

**Example**: "Seller not liable for any consequential damages"

**Indian Position**:
- **Generally valid** (freedom of contract)
- **Exceptions**:
  - **Fundamental breach**: Exclusion clause may not protect if fundamental breach
  - **Unconscionable**: If grossly unfair, court may disregard
  - **Statutory violations**: Cannot exclude liability imposed by statute

**Case: Suisse Atlantique v. NV Rotterdamsche (1967)**:
- Limitation clause in charter party
- Fundamental breach occurred
- Court held: Limitation clause may not apply to fundamental breach

**Indian Approach**: Courts interpret exclusion clauses strictly (against party relying on it)

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Actual Breach

**Facts**: Seller contracts to deliver 1000 units on March 1; fails to deliver

**Analysis**:
- **Type**: Actual breach (performance due; not performed)
- **Buyer's rights**: Terminate + damages
- **Damages**: Loss of profit from resale (if foreseeable)

### Example 2: Anticipatory Breach

**Facts**:
- A contracts to deliver goods to B on Dec 31
- On Dec 1, A states: "I will not deliver"

**Analysis**:
- **Type**: Anticipatory breach (repudiation before due date)
- **B's options**:
  1. Accept repudiation → Sue immediately (Dec 1)
  2. Affirm contract → Wait till Dec 31; sue then if A still doesn't deliver

### Example 3: Breach of Condition vs Warranty

**Facts**:
- Contract to sell "2024 Honda City with leather seats"
- Delivered: 2024 Honda City with cloth seats

**Analysis**:
- **Model (2024 Honda City)**: Condition (essential)
- **Leather seats**: Warranty (collateral)
- **Breach**: Warranty only (seats)
- **Buyer's remedy**: Cannot reject car (model correct); can claim damages for cloth vs leather difference

### Example 4: Liquidated Damages

**Facts**:
- Construction contract: Rs. 50 lakhs; completion by June 1
- Liquidated damages: Rs. 10,000/day for delay
- Contractor completes on Aug 1 (60 days late)
- Actual loss to owner: Rs. 2 lakhs

**Analysis**:
- **Stipulated amount**: Rs. 10,000 × 60 = Rs. 6 lakhs
- **Section 74**: Court awards reasonable compensation (not exceeding Rs. 6 lakhs)
- **Likely award**: Rs. 2 lakhs (actual loss) OR higher if court finds genuine pre-estimate

### Example 5: Duty to Mitigate

**Facts**:
- A contracts to rent office to B for Rs. 1 lakh/month (1-year lease)
- B breaches after 3 months
- A could re-let office immediately for Rs. 90,000/month but doesn't
- A sues for Rs. 9 lakhs (remaining 9 months @ Rs. 1 lakh)

**Analysis**:
- **Duty to mitigate**: A should have re-let office
- **Reasonable mitigation**: Accept Rs. 90,000/month tenant
- **Damages**: Rs. 10,000/month × 9 = Rs. 90,000 (difference) + 3 months vacancy (if reasonable)
- **Cannot claim**: Full Rs. 9 lakhs (failed to mitigate)

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Confusing Condition with Warranty

**Mistake**: Terminating contract for minor breach (warranty)

**Correct**: Only material breach (condition) allows termination; minor breach (warranty) → damages only

### 2. Failing to Mitigate

**Mistake**: Sitting idle after breach, letting losses accumulate

**Correct**: Take reasonable steps to minimize loss (duty to mitigate)

### 3. Assuming Penalty Unenforceable

**Mistake**: Thinking penalty clauses void (English law position)

**Correct**: India (Section 74) - Penalty enforceable as ceiling; court awards reasonable compensation

### 4. Ignoring Anticipatory Breach

**Mistake**: Waiting till performance date when party clearly repudiated

**Correct**: Can accept repudiation and sue immediately (or affirm and wait)

### 5. Remote Damages

**Mistake**: Claiming all losses (even remote, unforeseeable)

**Correct**: Only natural consequences or special damages (known to parties) recoverable (Hadley v. Baxendale)

# Capacity to Contract Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-CAPACITY
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Contract Formation - Capacity
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 10-12, 68)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **competency to contract** - who can and cannot enter into legally binding contracts under Indian law.

**Key Principle**: Only persons competent to contract can create valid contracts. Contracts by incompetent persons are either **void** or **voidable**.

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 10: Essential of Valid Contract

```
"All agreements are contracts if they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract..."
```

**Competency is ESSENTIAL** element of valid contract

### Section 11: Who are Competent to Contract

```
"Every person is competent to contract who is of the age of majority according to the law to which he is subject, and who is of sound mind and is not disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject."
```

**THREE Categories of Incompetent Persons**:
1. **Minors** (Below 18 years)
2. **Persons of Unsound Mind**
3. **Persons Disqualified by Law** (Alien enemies, insolvents, convicts, etc.)

---

## Category 1: Minors (Section 11)

### Definition of Minor

**Age of Majority**: Governed by **Indian Majority Act 1875**

**Section 3 Indian Majority Act**:
- **General Rule**: 18 years
- **Exception**: If court/guardian appointed → 21 years

**Example**:
- Child born on Jan 1, 2006 → Attains majority on Jan 1, 2024 (18th birthday)
- If guardian appointed by court → Attains majority on Jan 1, 2027 (21st birthday)

### Legal Position: Minor's Agreement is VOID AB INITIO

**Landmark Case: Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903)** (Privy Council - Binding on India)

**Facts**:
- Minor (Dharmodas Ghose, 17 years) executed mortgage deed for Rs. 20,000
- Lender (Brahmo Dutt) advanced Rs. 8,000
- Minor's mother sued to set aside mortgage (son still minor)
- Defendant argued: Minor's contract voidable (can be ratified after majority)

**Privy Council Held**:
- **Minor's agreement is VOID AB INITIO** (void from the beginning)
- Not merely voidable
- **Cannot be ratified** after attaining majority
- Minor not liable to return money or property received

**Effect**:
- No contract exists at all
- No obligation on minor
- No question of enforcement or ratification

### Consequences of Mohori Bibee Principle

#### 1. Minor Cannot Be Sued on Contract

**Rule**: Minor cannot be sued for breach of contract or to enforce contractual obligations

**Reason**: Contract void; no legal obligation exists

**Example**: Minor borrows Rs. 1 lakh, spends it, refuses to repay → Cannot be sued (contract void)

#### 2. Minor Cannot Sue on Contract (General Rule)

**Rule**: Since contract void, minor also generally cannot sue

**Exception**: **If minor partly performed** and wants to recover property/money
- **Minority as Shield, Not Sword**: Minor can use minority to escape liability, but cannot enforce void agreement

#### 3. Goods Supplied to Minor - No Liability

**General Rule**: Minor not liable to pay for goods supplied

**Exception: Necessaries** (Section 68)

**Section 68**: "If a person, incapable of entering into a contract, or any one whom he is legally bound to support, is supplied by another person with necessaries suited to his condition in life, the person who has furnished such supplies is entitled to be reimbursed from the property of such incapable person."

**Key Points**:
- **Reimbursement from property**: Can claim from minor's property (not personal liability)
- **Necessaries**: Essential goods (food, clothing, shelter, education, medical)
- **Suited to condition in life**: Luxury car NOT necessity for poor minor

**Landmark Case: Nash v. Inman (1908)** (English case, persuasive):
- Minor (Cambridge undergraduate) bought 11 fancy waistcoats
- Tailor sued for price
- Court held: NOT necessaries (minor already sufficiently supplied with clothes)

**Indian Position** (Section 68):
- Supplier can recover from **minor's property** (not personal obligation)
- Must prove: (1) Necessaries, (2) Suited to minor's condition, (3) Minor has property

#### 4. Money Advanced to Minor - No Recovery

**General Rule**: Money lent to minor cannot be recovered

**Case: Mohori Bibee**: Minor borrowed Rs. 8,000; lender could not recover

**Exception: Necessaries** (Section 68):
- If money advanced for necessaries (food, medicine) and used for that purpose → Can recover from minor's property

#### 5. Minor's Property - No Specific Performance Against Minor

**Specific Performance Act, Section 14(d)**: Court will not enforce specific performance against minor

**Example**: Minor agrees to sell land; later refuses → Court will not order specific performance (minor protected)

#### 6. Minor as Partner

**Indian Partnership Act, Section 30**: Minor cannot be partner in firm

**But**: Minor can be **admitted to benefits of partnership** (shares profits but not liable for losses)

#### 7. Minor's Liability in Tort (Wrongful Act)

**General Rule**: Minor liable for torts (civil wrongs like assault, trespass)

**Exception**: **Cannot convert contract into tort** to make minor liable

**Case: Burnard v. Haggis (1863)**:
- Minor hired horse for riding (contract)
- Minor jumped horse, horse injured
- Owner sued in tort (negligence)
- Court held: Liable (tort separate from contract)

**Case: Jennings v. Rundall (1799)** (Counter):
- Minor hired horse for riding
- Lent horse to friend (breach of contract)
- Owner sued in tort
- Court held: NOT liable (would be enforcing contract indirectly)

**Rule**: Minor liable for tort ONLY if tort independent of contract (not mere breach of contract)

#### 8. Estoppel Against Minor

**Estoppel**: Preventing person from denying previous representation

**General Rule**: Minor can plead minority EVEN IF minor lied about age

**Case: Mohori Bibee**: Minor falsely represented he was major; still could avoid contract

**Rationale**: Estoppel cannot create contract where none exists (contract void)

**BUT**: Minor liable in tort for **deceit/fraud** (independent tort)

#### 9. Restitution (Return of Benefit)

**General Rule**: Minor not obliged to return benefit received under void contract

**Case: Mohori Bibee**: Minor not obliged to return Rs. 8,000

**Equity Doctrine** (Some courts apply):
- If property/money still identifiable in minor's hands → Court may order return on equitable grounds
- **But**: If money spent or property consumed → Cannot be recovered

**Section 64 Indian Contract Act**: When agreement discovered to be void, person receiving advantage must restore it

**Debate**: Whether Section 64 applies to minors (generally NO; Mohori Bibee prevails)

### Ratification After Attaining Majority

**Rule**: Minor's agreement is **VOID**, not voidable

**Consequence**: **Cannot be ratified** after attaining majority

**Case: Mohori Bibee**: Expressly held that minor cannot ratify after majority

**Fresh Contract Needed**: After majority, parties can enter into **fresh contract** (not ratification of old void contract)

---

## Category 2: Persons of Unsound Mind (Section 12)

### Definition of Sound Mind

**Section 12**: "A person is said to be of sound mind for the purpose of making a contract, if, at the time when he makes it, he is capable of understanding it and of forming a rational judgment as to its effect upon his interests."

**Test**: Capacity to understand AND form rational judgment

**Time Relevant**: At the time of making contract (not before or after)

### Types of Mental Incapacity

#### 1. Permanent Insanity

**Total incapacity**: Person always of unsound mind
- **Effect**: All contracts VOID

**Guardian**: Guardian can contract on behalf of insane person (for necessaries)

#### 2. Temporary Insanity (Lucid Intervals)

**Intermittent insanity**: Person alternates between sanity and insanity

**Section 12 Explanation**: "A person who is usually of unsound mind, but occasionally of sound mind, may make a contract when he is of sound mind."

**Rule**: Contract during lucid interval (sound mind period) → VALID

**Burden of Proof**: Party alleging unsoundness must prove person was of unsound mind at time of contract

**Case: Imperial Loan Co. v. Stone (1892)**:
- Defendant signed guarantee during lucid interval
- Later claimed he was insane
- Court held: Valid contract (signed during lucid interval)

#### 3. Drunkenness / Intoxication

**Section 12 Illustration**: "A patient in a lunatic asylum, who is at intervals of sound mind, may contract during those intervals."

**Intoxication**: Person under influence of alcohol/drugs

**Test**: Was person capable of understanding contract?

**Rule**:
- If so intoxicated as to be incapable of understanding → Contract VOIDABLE
- If capable of understanding despite intoxication → Contract VALID

**Case: Gore v. Gibson (1845)**:
- Defendant signed contract while drunk
- Court held: Voidable if defendant proves (1) so drunk as incapable of understanding, (2) other party knew of drunkenness

**Indian Position**: Contracts by intoxicated persons voidable if incapacity proven

#### 4. Mental Deficiency (Not Total Insanity)

**Low intelligence**: Person not insane but limited mental capacity

**Test**: Could person understand nature and effect of contract?

**Example**: Illiterate person signs complex legal document without explanation
- Not necessarily void (unless fraud/undue influence)
- Court looks at whether person understood

### Effect of Unsoundness of Mind

#### If Unsound at Time of Contract:

**Contract VOID** (no legal effect)

**Exception: Necessaries** (Section 68):
- Like minors, insane person's property liable for necessaries supplied
- **Reimbursement from property**, not personal liability

**Case: In re Rhodes (1890)**:
- Lunatic supplied with necessaries (food, clothing)
- Supplier entitled to reasonable payment from lunatic's estate

#### If Sound at Time of Contract:

**Contract VALID** (no infirmity)

### Burden of Proof

**General Presumption**: Every person is of sound mind

**Burden**: Party alleging unsoundness must prove:
1. Person was of unsound mind at time of contract
2. Incapacity was such that person could not understand contract

**Case: Maneck Cowasjee Vakharia v. Bai Sakina (1910)**:
- Plaintiffs claimed deceased was of unsound mind when executing will
- Burden on plaintiffs to prove unsoundness
- Could not prove → Will upheld

## Category 3: Persons Disqualified by Law

**Section 11**: "...and is not disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject."

### Types of Disqualified Persons

#### 1. Alien Enemies (During War)

**Alien Enemy**: Citizen of country at war with India

**Rule**: Contracts with alien enemies during war are **VOID**

**Rationale**: Trading with enemy aids enemy war effort (against public policy)

**Exception**: Contracts made **before war** are merely **suspended** (revived after war ends)

#### 2. Foreign Sovereigns and Ambassadors

**Diplomatic Immunity**: Cannot be sued in Indian courts without consent

**Effect**: Can contract, but cannot be sued for breach (practical bar)

#### 3. Insolvents (Undischarged)

**Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016**

**Undischarged Insolvent**: Person declared insolvent but not yet discharged

**Rule**: Insolvent **CAN contract**, but:
- **Pre-insolvency debts**: Vest in Official Assignee/Trustee
- **Post-insolvency contracts**: Valid, but creditors cannot seize assets (assets vest in Official Assignee)

**After Discharge**: Full capacity restored

#### 4. Convicts (During Sentence)

**Conviction**: Person sentenced to imprisonment

**Rule**: Convict **CAN contract** (no legal bar), but practical difficulties (imprisoned)

**Civil Disabilities**:
- Cannot sue during imprisonment (civil rights suspended)
- After release, rights restored

#### 5. Corporations (Beyond Memorandum Powers)

**Corporation**: Legal person created by law (companies, statutory bodies)

**Doctrine of Ultra Vires**: Corporation cannot contract beyond powers in Memorandum of Association

**Effect**: Ultra vires contracts VOID

**Companies Act 2013**: Object clause broad; ultra vires less common now

#### 6. Married Women (Historical)

**Historical Position**: Married women could not contract (Hindu Law, Muslim Law)

**Current Position**: Married women have **FULL capacity** to contract
- Hindu Women's Rights Act 1937
- General emancipation laws

**No Disability**: Married women fully competent

## Practical Implications

### Contracts with Minors

**Best Practices**:
1. **Verify Age**: Ask for proof (birth certificate, Aadhaar)
2. **Avoid Contracting**: Do not contract with minors (unenforceable)
3. **Guardian's Contract**: Contract with parent/guardian instead
4. **Joint Contract**: If minor + adult jointly sign, adult liable (but minor not)

**Risk**: Entering contract with minor = Complete loss if minor repudiates

### Contracts with Persons of Unsound Mind

**Best Practices**:
1. **Assess Capacity**: Does person understand transaction?
2. **Medical Certificate**: For large transactions, obtain medical fitness certificate
3. **Witnesses**: Have witnesses observe person's demeanor and understanding
4. **Guardian Approval**: For major transactions, involve guardian

### Necessaries (Section 68)

**What Qualifies as Necessaries?**

**Factors**:
1. **Essential for living**: Food, clothing, shelter, medical care
2. **Education**: School fees, books
3. **Suited to condition in life**: What's necessary for rich person may differ from poor
4. **Not already supplied**: If minor already has sufficient clothing, more clothes not necessaries

**Examples**:
- **Necessaries**: School tuition, food, basic clothing, medicine, rent
- **NOT Necessaries**: Luxury car, expensive jewelry, non-essential gadgets

**Practical Tip**: If supplying to minor/insane person, document that:
- Goods are necessaries
- Suited to their condition
- Not already adequately supplied

### 1. Assuming Minor's Contract is Voidable (Not Void)

**Mistake**: Thinking minor can ratify contract after majority

**Correct**: Minor's contract VOID ab initio; cannot be ratified (Mohori Bibee)

**Mistake**: Assuming person occasionally insane is never competent

# Contract Damages Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-DAMAGES
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Remedies - Damages
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 73-75)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **damages** for breach of contract - the monetary compensation awarded to the innocent party for loss suffered due to breach.

**Key Principle**: Damages aim to compensate, not punish. The goal is to put the innocent party in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed.

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 73: Compensation for Loss or Damage

```
"When a contract has been broken, the party who suffers by such breach is entitled to receive, from the party who has broken the contract, compensation for any loss or damage caused to him thereby, which naturally arose in the usual course of things from such breach, or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach of it.

Such compensation is not to be given for any remote and indirect loss or damage sustained by reason of the breach.

When a contract has been broken, the party complaining of the breach must take reasonable steps to mitigate the loss consequent on the breach."
```

**Key Elements**:
1. **Compensation** for loss/damage
2. **Natural consequences** OR **contemplated consequences**
3. **NOT remote/indirect** damages
4. **Duty to mitigate** losses

### Section 74: Liquidated Damages and Penalty

```
"When a contract has been broken, if a sum is named in the contract as the amount to be paid in case of such breach, or if the contract contains any other stipulation by way of penalty, the party complaining of the breach is entitled, whether or not actual damage or loss is proved to have been caused thereby, to receive from the party who has broken the contract reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount so named or, as the case may be, the penalty stipulated for."
```

**Liquidated damages clause** = Ceiling on compensation

### Section 75: Compensation for Breach of Contract to Indemnify

```
"A person who rightfully rescinds a contract is entitled to compensation for any damage which he has sustained through the non-fulfillment of the contract."
```

**Rescission + Damages** available

---

## Types of Damages

### 1. Compensatory Damages (General Damages)

**Purpose**: Compensate for actual loss suffered

**Rule**: Put innocent party in position they would have been if contract performed

**Measure**: Difference between contract value and actual position

**Example**: Contract to deliver goods worth Rs. 10 lakhs; seller breaches; buyer buys from market for Rs. 12 lakhs
- **Damages**: Rs. 2 lakhs (additional cost incurred)

### 2. Nominal Damages

**Purpose**: Vindicate legal right (when breach proved but no actual loss)

**Award**: Token amount (Rs. 1 or nominal sum)

**Example**: Seller breaches delivery contract; buyer already bought same goods elsewhere at same price (no actual loss)
- **Damages**: Nominal (legal right vindicated)

**Indian Position**: Generally awarded in breach cases even if no actual loss

### 3. Special / Consequential Damages

**Purpose**: Compensate for foreseeable consequential losses

**Section 73**: "...or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach..."

**Requirement**: Loss must be in **contemplation of parties** at time of contract

**Example**: Buyer informs seller that goods needed for specific contract; seller breaches
- **Special damages**: Loss from buyer's specific contract (seller knew purpose)

**See**: Hadley v. Baxendale rule (below)

### 4. Reliance Damages / Wasted Expenditure

**Purpose**: Reimburse expenses incurred in reliance on contract

**Example**: Contract to supply machines; buyer spent Rs. 1 lakh preparing factory; seller breaches
- **Reliance damages**: Rs. 1 lakh (wasted preparation costs)

**Alternative to Expectation Damages**: When expectation loss difficult to prove

### 5. Liquidated Damages

**Purpose**: Pre-agreed compensation amount

**Section 74**: Amount named in contract as compensation for breach

**Effect**: Ceiling (court awards reasonable compensation not exceeding stipulated amount)

**See**: Liquidated Damages vs Penalty (below)

---

## Measure of Damages: Hadley v. Baxendale Rule

**Landmark Case: Hadley v. Baxendale (1854)** (English case, incorporated in Section 73)

**Facts**:
- Mill owner's crankshaft broke; mill stopped
- Owner sent crankshaft to carrier for delivery to repair shop
- Carrier delayed delivery
- Mill remained idle (loss of profit)

**Court Held**: Loss of profit NOT recoverable

**Reasoning**: Carrier didn't know crankshaft was only one; mill would stop entirely

**Two-Limb Test** for Recoverable Damages:

#### Limb 1: Natural Consequences

**Damages "which naturally arose in the usual course of things from such breach"**

**Test**: Would reasonable person foresee this loss as probable consequence?

**Example**: Seller fails to deliver goods
- **Natural consequence**: Buyer's loss from inability to fulfill resale contract (foreseeable)

#### Limb 2: Special Damages (Contemplated)

**Damages "which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach"**

**Test**: Did both parties have **actual knowledge** of special circumstances?

**Example**: Buyer tells seller "I need goods urgently for government contract worth Rs. 1 crore"
- Seller knows buyer will lose Rs. 1 crore if breach
- **Special damages**: Loss from government contract (within contemplation)

**Indian Application**:

**Case: Murlidhar Chiranjilal v. Harishchandra (1962)** (Supreme Court):
- Contract to supply jaggery for resale
- Supplier breached
- Court held: Loss of profit from resale recoverable (naturally foreseeable)

---

## Remoteness of Damages

**Section 73 Proviso**: "Such compensation is not to be given for any remote and indirect loss or damage sustained by reason of the breach."

**Remote Damages**: Loss too far removed from breach (indirect consequence)

**NOT Recoverable**: If loss not reasonably foreseeable or not in contemplation

**Proximate Cause Test**: Loss must flow directly from breach

**Case: Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd. v. Newman Industries Ltd. (1949)**:
- Contract to sell boiler to laundry; delivery delayed
- Laundry claimed: (1) Normal loss of profit, (2) Loss of lucrative dyeing contract with government
- Court held:
  - (1) **Recoverable** (foreseeable - laundry needs boiler to operate)
  - (2) **NOT recoverable** (seller didn't know about special government contract)

---

## Duty to Mitigate (Section 73 Proviso)

**Section 73**: "...the party complaining of the breach must take reasonable steps to mitigate the loss consequent on the breach."

**Rule**: Innocent party must minimize loss after breach

### What is Reasonable Mitigation?

**Reasonable Steps**:
- Finding alternative supplier
- Re-letting property (if tenant breaches)
- Selling goods at best available price
- Accepting substitute performance (if reasonable)

**NOT Required**:
- Unreasonable expense or risk
- Substantially different performance
- Litigation or complicated action

**Example**: Seller breaches; buyer must buy from alternative source (reasonable)
- Buyer cannot sit idle and claim damages for extended period

**Case: British Westinghouse Electric v. Underground Electric Railways (1912)**:
- Defective turbines supplied
- Buyer replaced with more efficient turbines
- New turbines saved more than cost
- Court held: Savings offset damages (duty to mitigate)

**Indian Application**:

**Case: Sohanlal v. Charan Das (1970)**:
- Buyer breached property purchase; seller kept property idle
- Court held: Seller should have sold property to mitigate loss

---

## Liquidated Damages vs Penalty (Section 74)

### Distinction

**Liquidated Damages**: Genuine pre-estimate of loss

**Penalty**: Amount intended to compel performance (punitive; not genuine estimate)

### English Law Position

**Penalty**: Unenforceable; only liquidated damages enforceable

**Test (Dunlop v. Selfridge)**: Is amount genuine pre-estimate? OR Extravagant sum (in terrorem)?

### Indian Law Position (Section 74)

**Both Liquidated Damages and Penalty Enforceable** - But as ceiling

**Section 74**: Court awards **reasonable compensation** not exceeding stipulated amount

**Effect**:
- Stipulated amount = Ceiling
- Court can award less (if reasonable compensation less than stipulated)
- Court can award full amount (if reasonable compensation equals or exceeds stipulated)

**Difference from English Law**:
- **English**: Penalty not enforceable at all
- **Indian**: Penalty enforceable as ceiling

**Case: Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Dass (1963)** (Supreme Court):
- Contract stipulated Rs. 200/day liquidated damages for delay
- Actual loss minimal
- Court held: Award reasonable compensation (can be less than stipulated)

**Case: Oil and Natural Gas Corporation v. Saw Pipes (2003)** (Supreme Court):
- Large liquidated damages clause in contract
- Court held: Section 74 treats liquidated damages and penalty alike; court awards reasonable compensation up to stipulated ceiling

**Case: Kailash Nath Associates v. DDA (2015)** (Supreme Court):
- Construction contract; liquidated damages for delay
- Supreme Court clarified: If liquidated damages are genuine pre-estimate, court should generally award stipulated amount (not reduce arbitrarily)
- Burden on party claiming less to show stipulated amount unreasonable

### Proof of Actual Loss (Section 74 Proviso)

**Section 74**: "...whether or not actual damage or loss is proved to have been caused thereby..."

**Rule**: Need NOT prove actual loss if liquidated damages clause exists

**Benefit**: Saves innocent party from proving exact quantum of loss

**Example**: Delay in construction; liquidated damages Rs. 10,000/day
- Innocent party need not prove exact Rs. 10,000 loss per day
- Court awards reasonable compensation up to Rs. 10,000/day

---

## Damages in Specific Contracts

### A. Sale of Goods Act 1930 (Sections 55-61)

**Buyer's Damages** (Seller's Breach):

**Section 57**: Non-delivery by seller
- **Measure**: Difference between contract price and market price (at time of breach)
- **Example**: Contract price Rs. 1 lakh; market price Rs. 1.2 lakh → Damages Rs. 20,000

**Section 58**: Breach of warranty by seller
- **Measure**: Difference between value as warranted and actual value
- **Plus**: Consequential damages (if foreseeable)

**Seller's Damages** (Buyer's Breach):

**Section 61**: Non-acceptance by buyer
- **Measure**: Difference between contract price and market price (or resale price)
- **Example**: Contract price Rs. 1 lakh; seller sells in market for Rs. 80,000 → Damages Rs. 20,000

### B. Specific Performance Contracts (Specific Relief Act 1963)

**Alternative Remedy**: Specific performance (actual performance) instead of damages

**When Damages Inadequate**:
- Sale of unique property (land, artwork)
- Specific goods with special value

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-SPECIFIC-PERFORMANCE protocol

### C. Indemnity Contracts (Section 124-125)

**Section 125**: Indemnity-holder can recover:
- Damages paid in suit
- Costs incurred
- Sums paid under compromise

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-INDEMNITY-GUARANTEE protocol

---

## Damages NOT Recoverable

### 1. Remote / Indirect Damages

**Example**: Seller breaches delivery; buyer misses meeting and loses job (too remote)

### 2. Speculative / Uncertain Damages

**Example**: Claim for "potential future profits" with no basis

### 3. Damages Due to Failure to Mitigate

**Example**: Buyer doesn't buy from alternative source; claims extended losses

### 4. Punitive / Exemplary Damages

**General Rule**: Punitive damages NOT awarded in contract breach (only compensatory)

**Exception**: Tort damages (fraud, deceit) may include punitive component

**Indian Position**: Generally no punitive damages for pure contract breach

---

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Hadley v. Baxendale Application

**Facts**:
- A contracts to supply machine part to B for Rs. 50,000 (delivery Jan 1)
- A breaches
- B's factory stops; loses Rs. 5 lakhs profit

**Analysis**:
- **Direct loss**: Cost of buying from market (if higher than Rs. 50,000) → Recoverable
- **Loss of profit**: Foreseeable? Did A know factory would stop?
  - If YES (A knew) → Recoverable (special damages)
  - If NO (A didn't know) → NOT recoverable (remote)

### Example 2: Duty to Mitigate

**Facts**:
- A leases office to B for Rs. 1 lakh/month (1 year)
- B breaches after 3 months
- A could re-let for Rs. 90,000/month but doesn't
- A sues for Rs. 9 lakhs (9 months × Rs. 1 lakh)

**Analysis**:
- **Duty to mitigate**: A should re-let
- **Reasonable mitigation**: Accept Rs. 90,000 tenant
- **Damages**: Rs. 10,000/month × 9 = Rs. 90,000 + reasonable vacancy period

### Example 3: Liquidated Damages

**Facts**:
- Construction contract: Rs. 50 lakhs; completion June 1
- Liquidated damages: Rs. 10,000/day delay
- Contractor completes Aug 1 (60 days late)
- Actual loss: Rs. 3 lakhs

**Analysis**:
- **Stipulated amount**: Rs. 10,000 × 60 = Rs. 6 lakhs
- **Section 74**: Court awards reasonable compensation (not exceeding Rs. 6 lakhs)
- **Likely award**: Rs. 3 lakhs (actual loss) to Rs. 6 lakhs (depending on whether genuine pre-estimate)

### Example 4: Sale of Goods (Non-Delivery)

**Facts**:
- Contract to buy 100 units at Rs. 1,000/unit (total Rs. 1 lakh)
- Seller doesn't deliver
- Market price at breach: Rs. 1,200/unit

**Analysis**:
- **Section 57 Sale of Goods Act**: Difference between contract and market price
- **Damages**: (Rs. 1,200 - Rs. 1,000) × 100 = Rs. 20,000

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Expecting Profit as Damages (When Remote)

**Mistake**: Claiming lost profits without proving foreseeability

**Correct**: Lost profits recoverable only if foreseeable or in parties' contemplation

### 2. Failing to Mitigate

**Mistake**: Sitting idle after breach, letting losses accumulate

**Correct**: Take reasonable steps to minimize loss

### 3. Confusing Penalty with Liquidated Damages

**Mistake** (English law mindset): Thinking penalty unenforceable

**Correct** (Indian law): Both enforceable as ceiling under Section 74

### 4. Claiming Punitive Damages

**Mistake**: Requesting punitive damages for contract breach

**Correct**: Contract damages are compensatory (not punitive)

### 5. Remote Damages

**Mistake**: Claiming all losses (even indirectly connected)

**Correct**: Only proximate, foreseeable damages recoverable

# Contract Discharge Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-DISCHARGE
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Contract Performance - Discharge
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 62-67)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **discharge of contracts** - the ways in which contractual obligations come to an end, releasing parties from further performance.

**Key Principle**: Contracts end when obligations are fulfilled, mutually cancelled, or excused by law.

---

## Methods of Discharge

**Six Methods**:
1. **Performance** (Section 37)
2. **Agreement** (Sections 62-63)
3. **Impossibility** (Section 56)
4. **Breach** (Section 39)
5. **Lapse of Time** (Limitation Act)
6. **Operation of Law** (Death, insolvency, merger)

---

## 1. Discharge by Performance

### Actual Performance (Section 37)

**Rule**: Contract discharged when parties perform all promises

**Requirements**:
- **Complete performance** (not partial)
- **Exact performance** (as per contract terms)
- **By proper person** (promisor or agent if allowed)
- **At proper time and place**

**Example**: Seller delivers 100 units; buyer pays Rs. 10,000 → Contract fully performed and discharged

### Attempted Performance / Tender (Section 38)

**Section 38**: "Where a promisor has made an offer of performance to the promisee, and the offer has not been accepted, the promisor is not responsible for non-performance, nor does he thereby lose his rights under the contract."

**Tender of Performance**: Unconditional offer to perform

**Effect**: Discharges promisor's obligation (even if not accepted)

**Example**: Seller tenders delivery on due date; buyer refuses → Seller discharged from obligation; can sue buyer

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-PERFORMANCE protocol (Section 41)

---

## 2. Discharge by Agreement

**Maxim**: "As parties can make contract, they can unmake it"

**Four Types**:
- Novation (Section 62)
- Rescission (Section 62)
- Alteration (Section 62)
- Remission (Section 63)
- Waiver

### A. Novation (Section 62)

**Section 62**: "If the parties to a contract agree to substitute a new contract for it, or to rescind or alter it, the original contract need not be performed."

**Novation**: Substitution of new contract for old contract

**Elements**:
1. **Mutual agreement** of all parties
2. **New contract** with different terms OR different parties
3. **Consideration**: New contract itself is consideration

**Types of Novation**:

#### 1. Change of Parties

**Example**: A owes Rs. 10,000 to B; B, A, and C agree that C will pay Rs. 10,000 to B (A released)
- Old contract: A → B
- New contract: C → B
- A discharged from obligation

#### 2. Change of Terms

**Example**: A agrees to deliver goods on Jan 1; parties agree to change to Jan 15
- Old contract: Delivery Jan 1
- New contract: Delivery Jan 15
- Original obligation discharged

**Case: Scarf v. Jardine (1882)**:
- Original contract to deliver wheat
- Parties agreed to substitute barley
- Court held: Novation; original contract discharged

### B. Rescission (Section 62)

**Rescission**: Mutual cancellation of contract

**Elements**:
1. **Mutual agreement** to cancel
2. **Before performance** (or partial performance with agreement)
3. **Consideration**: Mutual release is consideration

**Example**: A and B agree to cancel contract before delivery
- Both released from obligations
- No further performance required

**Bilateral Rescission**: Both parties release each other

**Unilateral Rescission**: One party releases other (requires consideration)

### C. Alteration (Section 62)

**Alteration**: Material change in one or more terms

**Requirements**:
1. **Mutual consent** to alter terms
2. **Material change** (substantial, not minor)
3. **Consideration**: Mutual promises for alteration

**Example**: Contract for Rs. 10,000; parties agree to change to Rs. 12,000
- Original contract discharged
- New contract for Rs. 12,000 formed

**Minor vs Material Change**:
- **Minor**: Change of payment mode (cash → cheque) → Amendment (not discharge)
- **Material**: Change of price, quantity, delivery date → Alteration (original contract discharged)

### D. Remission (Section 63)

**Section 63**: "The promisee may dispense with or remit, wholly or in part, the performance of the promise made to him, or extend the time for such performance, or accept instead of it any satisfaction which he thinks fit."

**Remission**: Acceptance of less than agreed performance

**Types**:

#### 1. Remission of Part Performance

**Example**: A owes Rs. 10,000 to B; B accepts Rs. 8,000 in full settlement
- B **remits** Rs. 2,000
- A discharged from Rs. 10,000 debt

**No Consideration Required**: Section 63 exception (promisee voluntarily accepts less)

#### 2. Extension of Time

**Example**: Payment due Jan 1; promisee extends to Jan 15
- Original obligation (Jan 1) discharged
- New obligation (Jan 15) created

#### 3. Acceptance of Different Satisfaction

**Example**: A owes Rs. 10,000 cash to B; B accepts car in settlement
- B accepts car **instead of** cash
- A's cash obligation discharged

**Case: Pinnel's Case (1602)** (English law):
- General rule: Part payment not satisfaction of whole debt (creditor can sue for balance)
- Exception: If part payment **plus something additional** (e.g., early payment, different goods) → Satisfaction

**Indian Law (Section 63)**: Remission valid even without consideration (promisee's voluntary acceptance sufficient)

### E. Waiver

**Waiver**: Voluntary relinquishment of right

**Example**: Seller has right to sue for delay; seller waives right
- Seller cannot later sue for delay damages

**Requirements**:
- **Voluntary**: Must be intentional
- **With knowledge**: Party knows of right being waived
- **Unequivocal**: Clear intention to waive

**No Consideration Required**: Waiver is gratuitous abandonment of right

---

## 3. Discharge by Impossibility

**Section 56**: "An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void... A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, unlawful, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful."

**Two Types**:
1. **Initial Impossibility** (at time of formation) → Void ab initio
2. **Supervening impossibility** (after formation) → Contract becomes void

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-IMPOSSIBILITY protocol (detailed coverage)

**Supervening Impossibility** (Section 56):

### A. Destruction of Subject Matter

**Example**: Contract to sell specific car; car destroyed in fire before delivery → Void

**Case: Taylor v. Caldwell (1863)**:
- Contract to rent music hall
- Hall burned down
- Court held: Contract void (impossibility)

### B. Death or Incapacity (Personal Service Contracts)

**Example**: Contract for artist to paint portrait; artist dies → Void

**Case: Robinson v. Davison (1871)**:
- Contract for pianist to perform
- Pianist fell ill (could not perform)
- Court held: Contract void (impossibility)

### C. Change of Law / Supervening Illegality

**Example**: Contract to export goods; government bans export → Void

**Example**: Contract during peacetime becomes illegal during war (trading with enemy) → Void

### D. Non-Occurrence of Contemplated Event

**Frustration of Purpose**: Event that was foundation of contract doesn't occur

**Case: Krell v. Henry (1903)** (Coronation case):
- Contract to rent room to view coronation procession
- Coronation cancelled
- Court held: Contract void (frustration of purpose)

**Indian Application**:

**Case: Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur (1954)** (Supreme Court):
- Land sale agreement
- Government requisitioned land
- Seller could not transfer
- Court held: Contract void (supervening impossibility)

---

## 4. Discharge by Breach

**Breach**: Failure to perform contractual obligations

**Two Types**:
1. **Actual Breach**: Breach at time performance due
2. **Anticipatory Breach**: Breach before performance due (repudiation)

**Effect**: Innocent party can treat contract as discharged + sue for damages

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-BREACH protocol (detailed coverage)

### Actual Breach

**Example**: Seller fails to deliver goods on delivery date → Actual breach

**Innocent Party's Rights**:
1. Treat contract as discharged (terminate)
2. Sue for damages

### Anticipatory Breach

**Example**: Seller declares (before delivery date) that they will not deliver → Anticipatory breach

**Innocent Party's Options** (Hochster v. De La Tour):
1. **Immediately sue** for breach (before performance date)
2. **Wait** till performance date and sue then

**Case: Frost v. Knight (1872)**:
- Defendant engaged to marry plaintiff
- Defendant repudiated engagement before marriage date (father still alive; marriage conditional on father's death)
- Court held: Plaintiff can sue immediately (anticipatory breach)

---

## 5. Discharge by Lapse of Time

**Limitation Act 1963**: Prescribes time limits for suing on contracts

**General Limitation Periods**:
- **Contracts**: 3 years from breach
- **Sale of goods**: 3 years from delivery
- **Negotiable instruments**: 3 years from maturity
- **Suits on judgments**: 12 years

**Effect of Limitation**: Right to sue barred (contract not performed but cannot enforce in court)

**Debt Still Exists**: Limitation bars remedy, not right
- Debtor can voluntarily pay time-barred debt (payment valid)
- Promise to pay time-barred debt (written, signed) → Revives debt (Section 25(3))

**See**: Limitation Act 1963

---

## 6. Discharge by Operation of Law

### A. Merger

**Merger**: Lower right merges into higher right

**Example**: Simple contract debt merged into judgment decree
- Creditor sues; obtains court judgment
- Original contract merged into judgment
- Cannot sue on original contract (must execute judgment)

### B. Death

**General Rule**: Death does NOT discharge contract (obligations pass to legal representatives)

**Exception**: Personal service contracts
- Contract for specific person's services
- Death → Contract discharged (cannot be performed by representatives)

**Example**: Contract for famous singer to perform → Singer dies → Discharged

### C. Insolvency

**Adjudication of Insolvency**: Insolvent discharged from pre-insolvency debts (after discharge order)

**Effect**: Contractual debts incurred before insolvency → Discharged

**But**: Creditors can claim from assets vested in Official Assignee

**See**: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016

### D. Material Alteration

**Unilateral Material Alteration**: If one party materially alters contract without consent → Contract voidable

**Material Alteration**: Substantial change (price, quantity, date)

**Example**: Creditor alters promissory note (increases amount) without debtor's consent → Note voidable

**Case: Pigot's Case (1614)**:
- Material alteration without consent vitiates instrument
- Debtor discharged

**Indian Position**: Material alteration without consent → Contract voidable at option of non-altering party

---

## Comparison Table: Discharge Methods

| Method | Basis | Mutual Consent Required? | Effect |
|--------|-------|--------------------------|--------|
| **Performance** | Completion of obligations | No | Contract fulfilled |
| **Novation** | New contract substituted | Yes | Original discharged; new contract formed |
| **Rescission** | Mutual cancellation | Yes | Both parties released |
| **Alteration** | Material change in terms | Yes | Original discharged; altered contract formed |
| **Remission** | Acceptance of less | No (promisee's unilateral act) | Promisor discharged to extent remitted |
| **Waiver** | Voluntary relinquishment | No (unilateral) | Right waived |
| **Impossibility** | Supervening event | No (operation of law) | Contract void |
| **Breach** | Failure to perform | No | Innocent party can terminate + damages |
| **Lapse of Time** | Limitation period expires | No | Right to sue barred |
| **Operation of Law** | Death, insolvency, merger | No | Contract discharged by law |

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Confusing Novation with Amendment

**Mistake**: Treating minor changes as novation

**Correct**:
- **Amendment**: Minor change (doesn't discharge original)
- **Novation**: Substantial change (discharges original; creates new)

### 2. Assuming Part Payment Discharges Debt

**Mistake**: Thinking partial payment = full discharge

**Correct**:
- **General rule**: Part payment NOT discharge (creditor can sue for balance)
- **Exception**: Remission under Section 63 (creditor accepts less in full settlement)

### 3. Thinking Impossibility Always Discharges

**Mistake**: Claiming impossibility for every difficulty

**Correct**:
- **Impossibility**: Objective impossibility (cannot be performed by anyone)
- **Difficulty/Hardship**: Not impossibility (contract still binding)

### 4. Forgetting Limitation Periods

**Mistake**: Delaying suit beyond limitation period

**Correct**: Sue within 3 years of breach (general contracts)

### 5. Unilateral Alteration

**Mistake**: One party alters contract assuming it's valid

**Correct**: Material alteration without consent → Voidable (other party can avoid)

# Contract Formation Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-FORMATION
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Contract Formation
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 1-30)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872 (no recent amendments)

---

## Overview

This protocol covers the **essential elements** for formation of a valid contract under the Indian Contract Act 1872. A contract is an agreement enforceable by law.

**Key Principle**: Not all agreements are contracts; only those agreements that meet all essential elements become legally enforceable contracts.

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 2(e): Definition of Agreement

```
"Every promise and every set of promises, forming the consideration for each other, is an agreement."
```

**Formula**: Promise + Promise = Agreement

### Section 2(h): Definition of Contract

```
"An agreement enforceable by law is a contract."
```

**Formula**: Agreement + Enforceability = Contract

### Section 10: What Agreements are Contracts

```
"All agreements are contracts if they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object, and are not hereby expressly declared to be void."
```

**Essential Elements** (from Section 10):
1. **Offer** (Proposal)
2. **Acceptance**
3. **Consideration**
4. **Capacity** (Competency to contract)
5. **Free Consent**
6. **Lawful Object**
7. **Lawful Consideration**
8. **Not Expressly Void**
9. **Certainty** (Section 29)
10. **Possibility of Performance** (Section 56)

---

## Element 1: Offer (Proposal)

### Section 2(a): Definition of Proposal (Offer)

```
"When one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other to such act or abstinence, he is said to make a proposal."
```

**Components**:
1. **Signification of willingness**: Intention to be bound
2. **To do or abstain**: Positive or negative act
3. **To obtain assent**: Seeking acceptance
4. **Communicated to offeree**: Offer must reach offeree

### Types of Offers

#### 1. Specific Offer vs General Offer

**Specific Offer**: Made to a specific person or group
- Example: A offers to sell car to B for Rs. 5 lakhs
- Only B can accept

**General Offer**: Made to public at large
- Example: Reward for finding lost dog
- Any member of public who knows of offer can accept

**Landmark Case: Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1893)** (English case, persuasive in India)
- Advertisement: "£100 reward to anyone who uses smoke ball as directed and still catches flu"
- Court held: General offer to world at large
- Anyone who performs conditions accepts offer
- Consideration: Using product + giving up legal right (to sue for flu)

#### 2. Express vs Implied Offer

**Express Offer**: Made by words (written or oral)
- Example: Written job offer letter

**Implied Offer**: Made by conduct
- Example: Bus conductor stands at bus stop with empty bus → Implied offer to carry passengers
- **Case: Wilkie v. London Passenger Transport Board (1947)**: Bus at stop implies offer to transport

#### 3. Cross Offers

**Cross Offer**: Two parties make identical offers to each other simultaneously without knowledge of other's offer

**Rule**: Cross offers do NOT constitute contract (no acceptance)

**Case: Tinn v. Hoffmann & Co. (1873)**:
- A wrote to B offering to sell iron
- B simultaneously wrote to A offering to buy iron (same terms)
- Letters crossed in post
- Court held: NO contract (no acceptance of either offer)

### Essential Characteristics of Valid Offer

1. **Intention to Create Legal Relations**:
   - Serious offer intended to create binding obligation
   - NOT mere invitation to treat or social invitation
   - **Case: Balfour v. Balfour (1919)**: Domestic agreements generally lack intention to create legal relations

2. **Definite and Certain Terms**:
   - Offer must be clear, not vague
   - **Section 29**: Agreement void if meaning uncertain
   - **Case: Guthing v. Lynn (1831)**: "I will give £5 more if horse is lucky" → Void (uncertain)

3. **Communicated to Offeree**:
   - Offer must reach offeree
   - Cannot accept offer without knowledge
   - **Case: Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt (1913)**: Servant found lost nephew without knowing of reward offer → Cannot claim reward (no knowledge of offer)

4. **Distinguished from Invitation to Treat**:
   
   **Invitation to Treat**: Invitation to others to make offer (not itself an offer)
   
   Examples:
   - **Display of Goods in Shop**: NOT offer; invitation to customer to offer to buy
     - **Case: Pharmaceutical Society v. Boots (1953)**: Goods on self-service display are invitation to treat; customer's taking goods to counter is offer; cashier's acceptance completes contract
   - **Auction**: Auctioneer's request for bids is invitation; bidder's bid is offer; fall of hammer is acceptance
     - **Section 64 Sale of Goods Act**: Sale by auction complete when auctioneer announces completion by fall of hammer
   - **Advertisement**: Generally invitation to treat (unless reward case like Carlill)
   - **Tender Notice**: Invitation to submit bids
   - **Catalogue/Price List**: Invitation to offer

### Termination of Offer

**When does an offer cease to exist?**

#### 1. Revocation (Section 5)

**Section 5**: "A proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards."

**Rule**: Offer can be revoked BEFORE acceptance, not after

**Communication of Revocation** (Section 6):
- Revocation complete when it comes to knowledge of offeree

**Case: Dickinson v. Dodds (1876)**:
- D offered to sell property to P, offer open till Friday
- Before Friday, D sold to third party
- P learned of sale from friend
- P purported to accept on Friday
- Court held: Offer revoked when P learned of sale; no contract

**Exception: Irrevocable Offer** (Supported by consideration)
- If offeree pays to keep offer open (option contract) → Offer cannot be revoked during option period

#### 2. Lapse of Time (Section 6(3))

**Section 6(3)**: Proposal lapses "by the lapse of the time prescribed in such proposal for its acceptance, or if no time is so prescribed, by the lapse of a reasonable time."

**Reasonable Time**: Depends on:
- Nature of subject matter (perishable goods = short time)
- Trade custom
- Circumstances

**Case: Ramsgate Victoria Hotel v. Montefiore (1866)**:
- M applied for shares in June
- Company allotted shares in November
- Court held: Offer lapsed by reasonable time; shares allotment too late

#### 3. Rejection (Section 6(1))

**Section 6(1)**: Proposal lapses "by the communication of its rejection by the person to whom it is made."

**Rejection Methods**:
- Express rejection
- **Counter-offer**: Rejection of original offer + new offer
  - **Case: Hyde v. Wrench (1840)**: W offered to sell farm for £1000; H counter-offered £950 (rejected by W); H then tried to accept £1000 offer → Court held: Original offer terminated by counter-offer; cannot be revived

#### 4. Death or Insanity (Section 6(4), (5))

**Section 6(4)**: Offer lapses "by the death or insanity of the proposer, if the fact of his death or insanity comes to the knowledge of the acceptor before acceptance."

**Rule**: 
- If offeree knows of death/insanity before acceptance → Offer lapses
- If offeree does NOT know → Acceptance may be valid (uncertain in India; English law suggests valid)

#### 5. Non-fulfillment of Condition Precedent

If offer states a condition that must be fulfilled before acceptance, failure to fulfill condition → Offer lapses

#### 6. Operation of Law

- Change in law making offer illegal → Offer lapses
- Destruction of subject matter → Offer lapses

## Element 2: Acceptance

### Section 2(b): Definition of Acceptance

```
"When the person to whom the proposal is made signifies his assent thereto, the proposal is said to be accepted. A proposal, when accepted, becomes a promise."
```

**Formula**: Offer + Acceptance = Promise (Agreement)

### Essentials of Valid Acceptance

#### 1. Absolute and Unqualified (Mirror Image Rule)

**Section 7(1)**: "In order to convert a proposal into a promise, the acceptance must be absolute and unqualified."

**Rule**: Acceptance must match offer exactly (mirror image)
- Any variation = Counter-offer (not acceptance)
- Counter-offer rejects original offer

**Case: Neale v. Merrett (1930)**:
- Offer to sell land for £280
- Acceptance: "I accept, send me contract"
- Court held: Valid acceptance (request for contract not a condition)

**Conditional Acceptance**: 
- "I accept subject to contract" → NOT acceptance (condition)
- "I accept if you reduce price" → Counter-offer (not acceptance)

#### 2. Communicated to Offeror

**Section 4**: "The communication of an acceptance is complete - as against the proposer, when it is put in a course of transmission to him so as to be out of the power of the acceptor."

**Rule**: Acceptance must be communicated; mental acceptance insufficient

**Methods of Communication**:
- **Oral**: In person, by phone (instantaneous)
- **Written**: Letter, email, WhatsApp
- **Conduct**: Implied acceptance by performing act

**Silence is NOT Acceptance** (General Rule)

**Case: Felthouse v. Bindley (1862)**:
- Nephew negotiating to sell horse to uncle
- Uncle wrote: "If I hear no more, I consider horse mine"
- Nephew did not reply but told auctioneer not to sell horse
- Auctioneer mistakenly sold horse
- Court held: NO contract (silence not acceptance)

**Exception**: Course of dealing may imply acceptance by silence
- Long-standing business relationship where silence always treated as acceptance

#### 3. Mode of Acceptance (Section 7(2))

**Section 7(2)**: "If the proposal prescribes the manner in which it is to be accepted, and the acceptance is not made in such manner, the proposer may, within a reasonable time after the acceptance is communicated to him, insist that his proposal shall be accepted in the prescribed manner, and not otherwise; but if he fails to do so, he accepts the acceptance."

**Rule**:
- If mode prescribed (e.g., "acceptance by registered post") → Must follow mode
- If mode not followed → Offeror can insist on proper mode OR waive requirement by conduct

#### 4. By Person to Whom Offer is Made (or Authorized Agent)

**Rule**: Acceptance must be by offeree (person to whom offer made) or their authorized agent

**Exception: General Offer**: Anyone can accept (e.g., Carlill case)

#### 5. Before Revocation or Lapse

**Section 5**: Acceptance must be before offer is revoked or lapses

#### 6. Within Reasonable Time (If No Time Specified)

**Section 6(3)**: If no time specified, acceptance must be within reasonable time

#### 7. Must Show Intention to Accept

**Acceptance by Conduct**:
- Performing act requested in offer = Acceptance
- **Case: Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball**: Using smoke ball as directed = Acceptance of offer

### Communication of Acceptance

#### Rule for Different Modes

**Section 4: Communication of Acceptance**

```
"The communication of an acceptance is complete—
(1) as against the proposer, when it is put in a course of transmission to him so as to be out of the power of the acceptor;
(2) as against the acceptor, when it comes to the knowledge of the proposer."
```

**Practical Application**:

**Postal Rule** (Instantaneous vs Non-Instantaneous):

1. **Non-Instantaneous Communication** (Letter):
   - **Against Proposer** (Offeror): Complete when letter POSTED (acceptor drops in mailbox)
   - **Against Acceptor**: Complete when letter RECEIVED by proposer
   
   **Consequence**: 
   - Offeror cannot revoke after letter posted (even if not yet received)
   - Acceptor can withdraw acceptance before letter reaches offeror (telegraph to offeror)

2. **Instantaneous Communication** (Phone, Telex, Fax, Email, WhatsApp):
   - Complete when received and read by offeror
   - **Case: Entores v. Miles Far East Corporation (1955)**: Telex acceptance complete when received
   - Modern view: Email complete when received (even if not opened immediately)

**Case: Adams v. Lindsell (1818)** (Leading case on Postal Rule):
- L wrote offer to A on Sept 2
- Letter misdirected, reached A on Sept 5
- A posted acceptance on Sept 5
- Acceptance reached L on Sept 9
- Meanwhile, L sold goods to third party on Sept 8
- Court held: Contract formed on Sept 5 (when A posted acceptance); L's sale on Sept 8 was breach

### Acceptance by Performance (Unilateral Contract)

**Unilateral Contract**: Offer requests performance of act (not promise to perform)

**Acceptance**: Completing the act

**Example**: Reward offer
- Offer: "Rs. 10,000 reward for finding lost dog"
- Acceptance: Finding and returning dog (not promising to find dog)

**No Need to Communicate Intention**: Performance itself is acceptance
- **Case: Carlill**: Mrs. Carlill did not write to company saying "I accept"; using product was acceptance

## Element 3: Consideration

### Section 2(d): Definition of Consideration

```
"When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise."
```

**Elements**:
1. **At the desire of promisor**: Must be at promisor's request (not voluntary)
2. **By promisee or any other person**: Consideration can move from third party (stranger to consideration allowed)
3. **Past, Present, or Future**: Done, doing, or promises to do

### Types of Consideration

#### 1. **Executed Consideration** (Past)
- Act done BEFORE promise made
- **Indian Law**: Past consideration IS valid (unlike English law)
- **Example**: A saves B's life; later B promises Rs. 1 lakh as reward → Valid contract in India

**Case: Lampleigh v. Braithwait (1615)** (Exception in English law, general rule in India):
- B asked L to seek pardon from king
- L did so
- B later promised to pay £100
- Court held: Valid (implied promise to pay existed from beginning)

#### 2. **Executory Consideration** (Future)
- Promise to do something in future
- Most common type
- **Example**: A promises to deliver goods next month; B promises to pay upon delivery

#### 3. **Present Consideration** (Simultaneous)
- Act and promise simultaneous
- **Example**: Cash sale in shop

### Essentials of Valid Consideration

#### 1. At the Desire of Promisor

**Rule**: Consideration must be at promisor's request, not voluntary

**Case: Durga Prasad v. Baldeo (1880)**:
- D spent money beautifying market at request of Collector
- B (market owner) promised to pay commission on sales
- Later B refused
- Court held: NO contract (D did not act at B's request; acted at Collector's request)

#### 2. May Move from Promisee or Any Other Person (Doctrine of Privity)

**Section 2(d)**: "promisee or any other person"

**Indian Law**: Consideration can move from third party (stranger to consideration)
- Different from English law (consideration must move from promisee)

**Case: Chinnaya v. Ramaya (1882)** (Madras High Court):
- Old lady gifted property to daughter
- Daughter promised to pay annuity to lady's sister
- Later daughter refused
- Court held: Valid contract (consideration moved from mother; sister can enforce even though she gave no consideration)

**BUT: Stranger to Contract Cannot Sue** (Section 2(d) allows stranger to consideration, but Section 2(c) requires privity of contract)

#### 3. Must Be Real, But Need Not Be Adequate

**Section 25 Exception 1**: "An agreement made without consideration is void, unless it is a promise to compensate for something done."

**Adequacy**: Court will not inquire into adequacy (whether fair value)
- Rs. 1 consideration for Rs. 1 crore property → Valid (inadequate but real)
- BUT: Gross inadequacy may indicate fraud/undue influence

**Case: Thomas v. Thomas (1842)**:
- Widow allowed to live in house for £1/year
- Court held: Valid consideration (need not be adequate)

**Real**: Must have some value, not illusory
- "I promise to give you gift if I feel like it" → Illusory (no real obligation)

#### 4. Must Be Lawful (Section 23)

**Section 23**: Consideration unlawful if:
- Forbidden by law
- Defeats provisions of law
- Fraudulent
- Immoral
- Opposed to public policy

**Example**: Agreement to commit crime → Void (unlawful consideration)

#### 5. Must Not Be Illusory or Vague

**Illusory Promise**: No real obligation
- "I will buy if I want to" → Illusory

**Vague**: Uncertain terms
- "I will pay reasonable price" → May be too vague (depends on context)

### Agreements Without Consideration (Section 25)

**General Rule**: Agreement without consideration is VOID

**Exceptions** (Section 25):

#### Exception 1: Love and Affection (Section 25(1))

Agreement without consideration valid if:
1. **In writing and registered**
2. **Between parties standing in near relation to each other**
3. **Made on account of natural love and affection**

**Case: Rajlukhy Dabee v. Bhootnath Mookerjee (1900)**:
- Husband promised to pay wife Rs. 500 as maintenance
- Written and registered
- Court held: Valid (love and affection between spouses)

**Case: Lakshminarayan v. Unknown** (Counter-example):
- Agreement between husband and wife to pay maintenance
- But relationship strained (frequent quarrels)
- Court held: VOID (no natural love and affection despite near relation)

#### Exception 2: Compensation for Past Voluntary Services (Section 25(2))

Agreement to compensate for something done voluntarily in past is valid if:
1. **Act was done voluntarily** (not at promisor's request)
2. **Promisor was benefited** (legally bound to compensate)

**Example**: A saves B's life (voluntarily); B later promises reward → Valid

#### Exception 3: Promise to Pay Time-Barred Debt (Section 25(3))

Promise to pay debt barred by limitation is valid if:
1. **In writing and signed** by debtor or agent
2. **Promise is to pay whole or part** of time-barred debt

**Example**: Debt barred by 3-year limitation; debtor writes promising to pay → Valid (limitation period starts fresh)

#### Exception 4: Agency (Section 185)

Agreement to indemnify agent for acts done in good faith is valid even without consideration

#### Exception 5: Gift (Section 122 Transfer of Property Act)

Gift is valid without consideration if:
- In writing and registered (immovable property)
- Delivery of possession (movable property)

## Practice Examples

### Example 1: Valid Contract Formation

**Facts**:
- A owns car, posts ad: "Selling 2020 Honda City for Rs. 8 lakhs"
- B sees ad, calls A: "I accept your offer, I'll buy for Rs. 8 lakhs"
- A agrees

**Analysis**:
- **Offer**: A's ad (specific offer to sell car for Rs. 8 lakhs)
- **Acceptance**: B's call (absolute, unqualified, communicated)
- **Consideration**: B promises Rs. 8 lakhs (executory consideration)
- **Result**: **VALID CONTRACT** formed when B accepted

### Example 2: Invitation to Treat (Not Offer)

**Facts**:
- Shop displays iPhone with price tag Rs. 50,000
- Customer picks up iPhone, takes to counter
- Cashier refuses to sell, says "Price wrong, should be Rs. 70,000"

**Analysis**:
- **Display of goods**: Invitation to treat (NOT offer)
- **Customer taking to counter**: Offer to buy for Rs. 50,000
- **Cashier's refusal**: Rejection of offer
- **Result**: **NO CONTRACT** (shop not bound by display price)

### Example 3: Counter-Offer (Not Acceptance)

**Facts**:
- A offers to sell land for Rs. 50 lakhs
- B responds: "I'll buy for Rs. 45 lakhs"
- A refuses
- B then says: "OK, I accept Rs. 50 lakhs"

**Analysis**:
- **A's offer**: Rs. 50 lakhs
- **B's response**: Counter-offer of Rs. 45 lakhs (terminates original offer)
- **A's refusal**: Rejection of counter-offer
- **B's purported acceptance**: Cannot accept terminated offer
- **Result**: **NO CONTRACT**

### Example 4: Postal Rule

**Facts**:
- A posts offer letter on Jan 1
- B receives on Jan 5
- B posts acceptance on Jan 7
- A receives acceptance on Jan 10
- A posted revocation on Jan 8 (reaches B on Jan 12)

**Analysis**:
- **Offer communicated**: Jan 5 (when B receives)
- **Acceptance complete (against A)**: Jan 7 (when B posts) - Postal Rule
- **Revocation**: Jan 8 (too late; contract already formed on Jan 7)
- **Result**: **VALID CONTRACT** formed Jan 7

### Example 5: Past Consideration

**Facts**:
- A saves B's child from drowning (voluntary act)
- B, grateful, promises Rs. 1 lakh reward
- Later B refuses to pay

**Analysis**:
- **Offer**: B's promise of Rs. 1 lakh
- **Consideration**: A's past act of saving child
- **Past consideration**: Valid in India (Section 2(d) allows past consideration)
- **Result**: **VALID CONTRACT** (B must pay)

**Note**: In English law, past consideration generally invalid (A could not enforce)

### Example 6: Agreement Without Consideration (Love and Affection)

**Facts**:
- Father promises to give Rs. 10 lakhs to son
- Promise in writing and registered
- Father and son have loving relationship
- Father later refuses

**Analysis**:
- **Consideration**: None (gift promise)
- **Section 25(1) Exception**: Written, registered, near relation, natural love and affection
- **Result**: **VALID CONTRACT** (enforceable despite no consideration)

## Common Pitfalls and Mistakes

### 1. Confusing Invitation to Treat with Offer

**Mistake**: Thinking shop display or advertisement is offer

**Correct**: Display/ad generally invitation to treat; customer's picking goods/responding is offer

### 2. Assuming Silence is Acceptance

**Mistake**: "If I don't hear from you, I assume you accept"

**Correct**: Silence NOT acceptance (Felthouse case); must communicate acceptance

### 3. Counter-Offer Kills Original Offer

**Mistake**: Making counter-offer but thinking can later accept original

**Correct**: Counter-offer terminates original offer; cannot be revived

### 4. Postal Rule Confusion

**Mistake**: Thinking acceptance complete only when received

**Correct**: For posted letter, acceptance complete when posted (against offeror); but can be revoked before receipt (against acceptor)

### 5. Past Consideration Invalid?

**Mistake**: Thinking past consideration always invalid

**Correct**: In India, past consideration IS valid (unlike English law)

### 6. Adequacy of Consideration

**Mistake**: Challenging contract because consideration inadequate (token Rs. 1)

**Correct**: Consideration need not be adequate (court won't inquire into fairness unless fraud/undue influence)

# Free Consent Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-FREE-CONSENT
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Contract Formation - Free Consent
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 13-22)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **free consent** - an essential element of valid contracts. Consent obtained through coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, or mistake is NOT free consent, making the contract **voidable** or **void**.

**Key Principle**: Consent must be genuine and voluntary for a valid contract.

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 10: Free Consent Required

```
"All agreements are contracts if they are made by the free consent of parties..."
```

**Free Consent** is ESSENTIAL element

### Section 13: Definition of Consent

```
"Two or more persons are said to consent when they agree upon the same thing in the same sense."
```

**Consensus ad idem**: Meeting of minds (agreement on same thing in same sense)

### Section 14: What is Free Consent

```
"Consent is said to be free when it is not caused by—
(1) coercion, as defined in Section 15, or
(2) undue influence, as defined in Section 16, or
(3) fraud, as defined in Section 17, or
(4) misrepresentation, as defined in Section 18, or
(5) mistake, subject to the provisions of Sections 20, 21 and 22."
```

**Five Vitiating Factors**:
1. Coercion
2. Undue influence
3. Fraud
4. Misrepresentation
5. Mistake

**Effect**: If consent obtained by any of these → Contract VOIDABLE (can be cancelled by aggrieved party)

---

## 1. Coercion (Section 15)

### Definition

**Section 15**: "Coercion is the committing, or threatening to commit, any act forbidden by the Indian Penal Code, or the unlawful detaining, or threatening to detain, any property, to the prejudice of any person whatever, with the intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement."

**Elements**:
1. **Committing or threatening** to commit an act forbidden by IPC
2. **OR**: Unlawful detaining or threatening to detain property
3. **Intention**: To cause person to enter into agreement
4. **Prejudice**: To any person whatever (not necessarily party to contract)

### Essential Characteristics

#### 1. Act Forbidden by IPC (Criminal Act)

**Examples of Coercion**:
- **Threat of violence**: "Sign this contract or I'll kill you"
- **Threat of criminal prosecution**: "Sign or I'll file false police complaint"
- **Threat of harm to family**: "Sign or I'll hurt your child"
- **Kidnapping**: Detaining person until they sign
- **Assault**: Forcing person to sign under physical force

**NOT Coercion**:
- **Threat of civil suit**: "Sign or I'll sue you" (NOT coercion; legitimate right)
- **Economic pressure**: "Sign or I won't do business with you" (NOT coercion unless threat to do illegal act)

**Case: Chikham Amiraju v. Chikham Seshamma (1918)**:
- Threat to commit suicide unless property transferred
- Court held: Coercion (attempt to suicide was crime under IPC 309 at that time)
- Note: IPC 309 now repealed; attempt to suicide decriminalized; this case may not apply today

#### 2. Unlawful Detention of Property

**Example**: A wrongfully seizes B's goods; refuses to return unless B signs contract → Coercion

**Lawful Detention**: NOT coercion
- Creditor retaining debtor's property under lien (lawful) → NOT coercion

#### 3. Prejudice to Any Person Whatever

**Unique Feature**: Prejudice need not be to party to contract

**Example**: A threatens to harm B's son C unless B signs contract with A → Coercion (prejudice to C)

**Section 15 Explanation**: "It is immaterial whether the Indian Penal Code is or is not in force in the place where the coercion is employed."

#### 4. Intention to Cause Person to Enter Agreement

**Causal Connection**: Coercion must induce consent

**Burden**: Party alleging coercion must prove:
1. Coercive act occurred
2. Consent given because of coercion (would not have consented otherwise)

### Effect of Coercion

**Section 19**: Contract voidable at option of party whose consent obtained by coercion

**Options for Aggrieved Party**:
1. **Rescind (cancel) contract**: Avoid contract and demand return of any benefit
2. **Affirm contract**: Choose to continue with contract

**Section 19A**: Contract induced by coercion voidable; party not bound to perform

### Burden of Proof

**General Rule**: Party alleging coercion must prove coercion

**Exception: Unconscionable Bargain**: If contract grossly unfair + relationship of dominance → Burden shifts to dominant party to prove no coercion/undue influence

---

## 2. Undue Influence (Section 16)

### Definition

**Section 16(1)**: "A contract is said to be induced by 'undue influence' where the relations subsisting between the parties are such that one of the parties is in a position to dominate the will of the other and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage over the other."

**Elements**:
1. **Dominant position**: One party can dominate will of other
2. **Use of dominant position**: To obtain unfair advantage

### When Does Dominant Position Exist?

**Section 16(2)**: Dominant position exists where:

#### (a) Real or Apparent Authority

**Examples**:
- **Employer-employee**
- **Government official-citizen**
- **Police officer-accused**

**Case: Ranganayakamma v. Alwar Setti (1889)**:
- Village headman (authority figure) obtained sale deed
- Court held: Dominant position exists (undue influence presumed)

#### (b) Fiduciary Relationship

**Fiduciary**: Relationship of trust and confidence

**Examples**:
- **Doctor-patient**
- **Lawyer-client**
- **Guardian-ward**
- **Trustee-beneficiary**
- **Spiritual advisor-devotee**

**Case: Inche Noriah v. Shaik Allie Bin Omar (1929)** (Privy Council):
- Old illiterate woman gifted property to nephew (her advisor)
- Court held: Fiduciary relationship; gift set aside (undue influence presumed)

#### (c) Mental Capacity Diminished

**Weakened mental state**: Temporary or permanent

**Examples**:
- **Old age with senility**
- **Illness affecting judgment**
- **Grief/distress** (e.g., death of loved one)

**Case: Raghunath Prasad v. Sarju Prasad (1924)**:
- Pardanashin woman (in purdah, secluded)
- Sold property without independent advice
- Court held: Weakened state (seclusion); undue influence presumed

### Presumption of Undue Influence (Section 16(3))

**Section 16(3)**: "Where a person who is in a position to dominate the will of another, enters into a contract with him, and the transaction appears, on the face of it or on the evidence adduced, to be unconscionable, the burden of proving that such contract was not induced by undue influence shall lie upon the person in a position to dominate the will of the other."

**When Presumption Arises**:
1. **Dominant position** exists (Section 16(2) relationships)
2. **Transaction unconscionable** (grossly unfair)

**Burden Shifts**: Dominant party must prove NO undue influence

**Unconscionable Transaction**: Unfair, one-sided, against natural justice

**Examples**:
- Property worth Rs. 50 lakhs sold for Rs. 5 lakhs
- Gift of entire property by elderly patient to doctor
- Transfer of property by client to lawyer for no consideration

**Case: Mannu Singh v. Umadat Pandey (1890)**:
- Zamindar (landlord) sold property to money-lender for inadequate price
- Money-lender in dominant position
- Court held: Undue influence presumed; money-lender failed to rebut

### Undue Influence vs Coercion

| Aspect | Coercion | Undue Influence |
|--------|----------|-----------------|
| **Nature** | Physical force/threat | Mental/moral pressure |
| **Relationship** | Need not exist | Usually exists (dominant-subservient) |
| **Act** | Criminal act (IPC) | Use of dominant position (not criminal) |
| **Burden** | On party alleging coercion | Shifts to dominant party if transaction unconscionable |
| **Example** | "Sign or I'll shoot you" | Doctor persuading patient to gift property |

### Effect of Undue Influence

**Section 19A**: Contract induced by undue influence voidable at option of aggrieved party

**Section 19A Proviso**: Court has discretion:
- **Set aside contract** entirely, OR
- **Uphold contract** with modified terms (rescind unfair clauses, adjust consideration)

**Example**: Property sold for Rs. 5 lakhs (worth Rs. 50 lakhs) under undue influence → Court may order: (1) Complete rescission, OR (2) Sale upheld but price increased to fair market value

## 3. Fraud (Section 17)

### Definition

**Section 17**: "'Fraud' means and includes any of the following acts committed by a party to a contract, or with his connivance, or by his agent, with intent to deceive another party thereto or his agent, or to induce him to enter into the contract—

(1) the suggestion, as a fact, of that which is not true by one who does not believe it to be true;
(2) the active concealment of a fact by one having knowledge or belief of the fact;
(3) a promise made without any intention of performing it;
(4) any other act fitted to deceive;
(5) any such act or omission as the law specially declares to be fraudulent."

**Elements of Fraud**:
1. **False representation** of fact (knowingly false)
2. **Intent to deceive** or induce contract
3. **Aggrieved party relied** on false representation
4. **Aggrieved party suffered** (or would suffer) loss

### Types of Fraud (Five Acts)

#### (1) Suggestion of Fact That is Not True (False Statement)

**Classic Fraud**: Stating something false as fact

**Elements**:
- Statement of fact (not opinion)
- False
- Maker knows it's false OR does not believe it's true

**Examples**:
- Selling car, stating "This car never met with accident" when car was in major accident
- Selling land, stating "This land has clear title" when land subject to mortgage

**NOT Fraud**:
- **Opinion**: "This is the best car in the market" (opinion, not fact; puffery allowed)
- **Belief**: "I believe this land will become valuable" (future prediction; not fact)

**Case: Derry v. Peek (1889)** (English case, persuasive):
- Company prospectus stated tramway would be steam-powered (pending Board approval)
- Board denied approval
- Court held: NOT fraud (honest belief; no intent to deceive)

#### (2) Active Concealment of Fact

**Active Concealment**: Deliberately hiding material fact

**Example**: 
- Seller paints over termite damage in house
- Seller hides defect in machinery

**Mere Silence**: Generally NOT fraud (caveat emptor - buyer beware)

**Exception: Duty to Speak** (Section 19):
- Fiduciary relationship (lawyer-client, doctor-patient)
- Relationship of trust (partners, trustees)
- Contract of utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei) - Insurance contracts

**Case: Keates v. Cadogan (1851)**:
- Landlord rented house knowing it was uninhabitable
- Did not disclose
- Court held: NO fraud (mere silence; no duty to disclose in lease contract)

**But**: If landlord **actively concealed** defects (plastered over cracks) → Fraud

#### (3) Promise Without Intention to Perform

**False Promise**: Promising to do something with NO intention of doing it

**Example**:
- Borrowing money promising to repay, knowing you cannot repay
- Promising to deliver goods, knowing you cannot deliver

**Difficulty**: Proving intent at time of promise

**Evidence of No Intent**:
- Immediate breach after contract
- Impossibility known to promisor
- Pattern of making promises without performing

**Case: Pollock Principles**: "If at time of making promise, promisor had no intention to perform → Fraud"

#### (4) Any Other Act Fitted to Deceive

**Catch-All Provision**: Any deceptive act

**Examples**:
- Rigging auction (false bidders to inflate price)
- Creating false documents
- Impersonation

#### (5) Act Declared Fraudulent by Law

**Statutory Fraud**: Acts specifically declared fraudulent by law

**Examples**:
- Fraudulent preference (insolvency law)
- Insider trading (securities law)

### Essentials of Fraud

#### 1. Representation of Fact (Not Opinion or Future Promise)

**Fact**: Statement about existing or past circumstances
- "This car has 50,000 km mileage" (fact)

**Opinion**: Subjective belief or judgment
- "This is a good car" (opinion)

**Future Promise**: Statement about future event
- "This land will appreciate" (future prediction; not fraud unless promisor knows it's false AND has no intention to make it happen)

**Exception: Expert Opinion**:
- Opinion by expert may be treated as fact
- Example: Doctor saying "This medicine is safe" when doctor knows it's dangerous → Fraud

**Case: Bisset v. Wilkinson (1927)**:
- Seller stated land could support 2000 sheep (opinion based on estimate)
- Buyer relied; land could only support 1000
- Court held: NOT fraud (honest opinion; buyer should have verified)

#### 2. False to Knowledge of Representor

**Scienter** (guilty knowledge): Representor must KNOW it's false

**Three States of Mind**:
1. **Knows it's false**: Fraud
2. **Believes it's true but careless**: NOT fraud (may be negligent misrepresentation)
3. **Does not believe it's true** (uncertain but represents as fact): Fraud

**Case: Derry v. Peek**: Honest belief (even if unreasonable) → NOT fraud

#### 3. Intent to Deceive or Induce Contract

**Mens Rea**: Fraudulent intent required

**Burden**: Party alleging fraud must prove intent to deceive

#### 4. Reliance by Aggrieved Party

**Inducement**: Fraud must have induced contract
- Aggrieved party relied on false representation
- Would not have entered contract but for fraud

**If No Reliance**: No fraud

**Example**: A lies about car's mileage; B buys car without caring about mileage → No fraud (no reliance)

### Effect of Fraud

**Section 19**: Contract voidable at option of aggrieved party

**Section 19 Proviso**: "If a party whose consent was obtained by fraud had the means of discovering the truth with ordinary diligence, the contract is not voidable."

**Exception to Voidability**: If victim could have discovered truth with ordinary diligence → Contract NOT voidable

**Rationale**: Negligent victim should not be rewarded

**Case: Central Inland Water Transport Corporation v. Brojo Nath (1986)**:
- Supreme Court: Even if party could have discovered fraud, contract still voidable if fraud practiced

**Current Position**: Courts reluctant to deny relief merely because victim was careless

**Remedies**:
1. **Rescission**: Cancel contract and restore parties to original position
2. **Damages**: Sue for tort of deceit (fraud in tort)

**Section 64**: Party rescinding contract must restore any benefit received

## 4. Misrepresentation (Section 18)

### Definition

**Section 18**: "Misrepresentation means and includes—

(1) the positive assertion, in a manner not warranted by the information of the person making it, of that which is not true, though he believes it to be true;
(2) any breach of duty which, without an intent to deceive, gains an advantage to the person committing it, or any one claiming under him, by misleading another to his prejudice or to the prejudice of any one claiming under him;
(3) causing, however innocently, a party to an agreement to make a mistake as to the substance of the thing which is the subject of the agreement."

**Key Difference from Fraud**: NO intent to deceive (innocent misrepresentation)

### Types of Misrepresentation

#### (1) Unwarranted Positive Assertion

**Innocent False Statement**: Person believes it's true but it's false

**Example**: Seller says "This car never met with accident" honestly believing it's true, but car was in accident (seller didn't know)

#### (2) Breach of Duty Gaining Advantage

**Example**: Agent fails to disclose material fact to principal (breach of fiduciary duty)

#### (3) Innocent Mistake as to Subject Matter

**Example**: Both parties believe painting is by Picasso; turns out it's fake → Innocent misrepresentation

### Misrepresentation vs Fraud

| Aspect | Fraud | Misrepresentation |
|--------|-------|-------------------|
| **Knowledge** | Knows it's false OR doesn't believe it's true | Believes it's true (but it's false) |
| **Intent** | Intent to deceive | No intent to deceive (innocent) |
| **Remedy** | Rescission + Damages (tort of deceit) | Rescission only (no damages) |
| **Burden** | On party alleging fraud | On party alleging misrepresentation |

**Case: Peek v. Gurney (1873)**:
- Prospectus contained false statements made in honest belief
- Court held: Misrepresentation (not fraud); no damages (but rescission available)

### Effect of Misrepresentation

**Section 19**: Contract voidable at option of aggrieved party

**But**: If aggrieved party had means to discover truth with ordinary diligence → Contract NOT voidable (Section 19 proviso)

**Remedies**:
1. **Rescission**: Cancel contract
2. **NO Damages**: Cannot sue for damages (no fraud; no tort of deceit)

---

## 5. Mistake (Sections 20-22)

### Introduction

**Mistake**: Erroneous belief about fact or law

**Effect**: Depends on type of mistake
- **Bilateral mistake of fact**: Void (Section 20)
- **Unilateral mistake**: Generally no effect (contract valid)
- **Mistake of law**: Generally no effect (ignorantia juris non excusat)

### Section 20: Bilateral Mistake of Fact

**Section 20**: "Where both the parties to an agreement are under a mistake as to a matter of fact essential to the agreement, the agreement is void."

**Elements**:
1. **Bilateral** (both parties mistaken)
2. **Mistake of fact** (not law)
3. **Essential to agreement** (goes to root of contract)

#### What is "Essential to Agreement"?

**Examples of Essential Mistakes** (Agreement VOID):

1. **Mistake as to Subject Matter Existence**:
   - **Case: Couturier v. Hastie (1856)**: Contract to sell cargo of corn; unknown to parties, cargo already perished at sea → Void (subject matter non-existent)
   - **Indian Analogy - Section 20 Illustration**: A agrees to buy B's horse; unknown to both, horse died → Void

2. **Mistake as to Identity of Subject Matter**:
   - A thinks selling horse X; B thinks buying horse Y → Void (no consensus ad idem)

3. **Mistake as to Quality**:
   - **If quality goes to root of contract** → Void
   - **Case: Raffles v. Wichelhaus (1864)** (Peerless case): Contract to buy cotton arriving on ship "Peerless"; two ships named "Peerless" (one in Oct, one in Dec) → Void (ambiguity as to which ship)

**Examples of NON-Essential Mistakes** (Agreement VALID):

1. **Mistake as to Peripheral Quality**:
   - A buys painting thinking it's valuable; turns out it's not → Valid (quality not essential; buyer's mistake on value)

2. **Mistake as to Motive**:
   - A buys land intending to build factory; later discovers zoning doesn't allow factory → Valid (mistake as to motive/future use; not essential to agreement)

### Section 21: Mistake as to Law

**Section 21**: "A contract is not voidable because it was caused by a mistake as to any law in force in India..."

**General Rule**: Mistake of law is NO excuse

**Ignorantia juris non excusat**: Ignorance of law is no excuse

**Example**: A pays money to B under mistake of law (thinking legally obliged); A cannot recover (mistake of law)

**Exception**: Mistake of **foreign law** treated as mistake of fact → Agreement voidable

### Section 22: Unilateral Mistake

**Section 22**: "A contract is not voidable merely because it was caused by one of the parties to it being under a mistake as to a matter of fact."

**General Rule**: Unilateral mistake (one party mistaken) → Contract VALID

**Exception**: Mistake as to **identity** or **nature of contract** → Void

#### Exception 1: Mistake as to Identity of Person

**Mistake as to identity** (Not mere creditworthiness) → Void

**Case: Cundy v. Lindsay (1878)**:
- L received order from "Blenkarn" who pretended to be reputable firm "Blenkiron & Co."
- L sold goods on credit to "Blenkarn" thinking it was "Blenkiron"
- Court held: Void (mistake as to identity)

**Contrast - Mistake as to Creditworthiness** (Contract Valid):
- A thinks B is creditworthy; B is not → Valid (mistake as to attribute, not identity)

**Case: King's Norton Metal Co. v. Edridge (1897)**:
- Order from "Hallam & Co." (fictitious firm)
- Seller thought it was large firm; actually one person
- Court held: Valid contract (mistake as to attributes, not identity)

#### Exception 2: Mistake as to Nature of Contract (Non Est Factum)

**Non est factum**: "It is not my deed"

**Rule**: If person signs document fundamentally different from what they thought → Void

**Requirements**:
1. **Fundamental difference** (not just terms)
2. **Not negligent** (reasonable care taken)

**Example**: Illiterate person asked to sign "receipt"; actually mortgage deed → Void (non est factum)

**Case: Saunders v. Anglia Building Society (1971)**:
- Elderly woman signed document thinking it was gift to nephew; actually sale to third party
- Court held: Valid (not fundamentally different; both were dispositions of property)

## Remedies for Vitiated Consent

### Voidable Contracts (Coercion, Undue Influence, Fraud, Misrepresentation)

**Section 19**: Voidable at option of aggrieved party

**Options**:
1. **Rescission**: Cancel contract and restore original position
2. **Affirmation**: Continue with contract
3. **Damages**: Only for fraud (tort of deceit); not for others

**Section 64**: When party rescinds voidable contract, must restore any benefit received (or compensate if cannot restore)

**Loss of Right to Rescind**:
- **Affirmation**: Aggrieved party affirms contract (expressly or by conduct)
- **Lapse of time**: Unreasonable delay
- **Third party rights**: Innocent third party acquired rights in subject matter

### Void Contracts (Bilateral Mistake)

**Effect**: Void ab initio (no contract exists)

**Consequences**:
- No obligations on either party
- Any property/money transferred can be recovered
- Third party acquiring rights in good faith protected

## Practice Examples

### Example 1: Coercion

**Facts**:
- X threatens to file false criminal case against Y unless Y sells land for Rs. 10 lakhs
- Y, in fear, sells land (worth Rs. 50 lakhs)

**Analysis**:
- **Coercion**: Threat to commit act forbidden by IPC (false case = IPC 182, 211)
- **Intention**: To induce contract
- **Effect**: Contract VOIDABLE at Y's option (Section 19)
- **Remedy**: Y can rescind and recover land

### Example 2: Undue Influence

**Facts**:
- Elderly patient gifts Rs. 20 lakhs to doctor who treated her
- Doctor-patient relationship
- Gift made without independent advice

**Analysis**:
- **Fiduciary relationship**: Doctor-patient (Section 16(2)(b))
- **Unconscionable**: Large gift to doctor (unfair)
- **Presumption**: Undue influence presumed (Section 16(3))
- **Burden**: On doctor to prove no undue influence
- **Effect**: Contract VOIDABLE (likely rescinded)

### Example 3: Fraud

**Facts**:
- Seller shows car, says "No accident, 50,000 km"
- Actually: Major accident, 1,50,000 km (odometer tampered)
- Buyer relies, buys for Rs. 5 lakhs

**Analysis**:
- **False representation**: Accident history, mileage
- **Knowledge**: Seller knows it's false
- **Intent to deceive**: To induce sale
- **Reliance**: Buyer relied
- **Effect**: Contract VOIDABLE + Damages (tort of deceit)

### Example 4: Misrepresentation

**Facts**:
- Seller honestly believes car never had accident (previous owner lied)
- Tells buyer "No accident"
- Actually car was in accident
- Buyer discovers after purchase

**Analysis**:
- **Innocent false statement**: Seller believed it was true
- **No intent to deceive**: Misrepresentation (not fraud)
- **Effect**: Contract VOIDABLE (but NO damages)
- **Remedy**: Rescission only

### Example 5: Bilateral Mistake

**Facts**:
- A agrees to sell B a painting believed to be by Picasso for Rs. 1 crore
- Both genuinely believe it's Picasso original
- Later discovered to be fake (worth Rs. 10,000)

**Analysis**:
- **Bilateral mistake**: Both parties mistaken
- **Essential to agreement**: Identity/authenticity of painting (goes to root)
- **Effect**: Contract VOID (Section 20)
- **Result**: A must return Rs. 1 crore; B returns painting

### Example 6: Unilateral Mistake (Not Voidable)

**Facts**:
- A sells land to B for Rs. 50 lakhs
- A thinks land cannot be used for commercial purposes
- B knows land has commercial zoning (worth Rs. 2 crores)
- A later discovers mistake

**Analysis**:
- **Unilateral mistake**: Only A mistaken (about commercial use)
- **Not as to identity**: Parties agree on same land
- **General rule**: Unilateral mistake → Contract VALID
- **Effect**: Contract VALID (A cannot rescind)

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Coercion vs Undue Influence

**Mistake**: Confusing the two

**Correct**:
- **Coercion**: Physical/criminal threat
- **Undue Influence**: Mental/moral pressure using dominant position

### 2. Fraud vs Misrepresentation

**Mistake**: Thinking they're the same

**Correct**:
- **Fraud**: Intentional deception → Rescission + Damages
- **Misrepresentation**: Innocent false statement → Rescission only (no damages)

### 3. Mere Silence ≠ Fraud

**Mistake**: Thinking failure to disclose is always fraud

**Correct**: Mere silence NOT fraud (caveat emptor) UNLESS duty to disclose (fiduciary relationship, insurance contracts)

### 4. Opinion vs Fact

**Mistake**: Treating all statements as factual representations

**Correct**: 
- **Opinion/Puffery**: "Best car in town" → Not fraud
- **Fact**: "50,000 km mileage" → Can be fraud if false

### 5. Bilateral vs Unilateral Mistake

**Mistake**: Thinking all mistakes void contract

**Correct**:
- **Bilateral mistake (essential)**: VOID
- **Unilateral mistake**: Generally VALID (exceptions: identity, non est factum)

### 6. Mistake of Law vs Fact

**Mistake**: Thinking mistake of law voids contract

**Correct**: Mistake of law NO excuse (Section 21); contract VALID

# Contract Impossibility and Frustration Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-IMPOSSIBILITY
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Contract Performance - Impossibility
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Section 56, 32-36)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **impossibility of performance** and **frustration of contract** - circumstances under which parties are excused from performing contractual obligations due to unforeseen events making performance impossible or purposeless.

**Key Principle**: Doctrine of frustration = "If the foundation of a contract is destroyed by an unforeseen event, the contract is discharged."

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 56: Doctrine of Impossibility / Frustration

```
"An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void.

A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, unlawful, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful."
```

**Two Parts**:
1. **Initial Impossibility** (at time of formation) → Agreement VOID ab initio
2. **Supervening Impossibility** (after formation) → Contract becomes VOID

---

## Initial Impossibility

**Rule**: Agreement to do impossible act → VOID from beginning

### Types of Initial Impossibility

#### 1. Physical Impossibility

**Impossible by laws of nature**

**Example**: Agreement to make sun rise from west → VOID (physically impossible)

#### 2. Legal Impossibility

**Forbidden by law**

**Example**: Agreement to import goods that are banned → VOID (legally impossible)

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-LEGALITY protocol (unlawful object)

#### 3. Non-Existence of Subject Matter (Unknown to Parties)

**Subject matter doesn't exist at time of contract** (but parties don't know)

**Case: Couturier v. Hastie (1856)** (English case):
- Contract to sell cargo of corn
- Unknown to parties, cargo had perished during voyage (before contract)
- Court held: Contract VOID (subject matter non-existent)

**Section 20 Illustration (Indian Contract Act)**:
- A agrees to buy B's horse
- Unknown to both, horse had died before agreement
- Agreement VOID

---

## Supervening Impossibility / Frustration

**Rule**: If performance becomes impossible after contract formed (due to event beyond parties' control) → Contract becomes VOID

**Doctrine**: **Frustration of Contract** (Taylor v. Caldwell doctrine)

### Landmark Case: Taylor v. Caldwell (1863)

**Facts**:
- Contract to rent music hall for concerts on specific dates
- Before first concert, hall burned down (not fault of either party)
- Plaintiff sued for breach

**Court Held**:
- Contract **implied condition**: Hall's continued existence necessary for performance
- Hall destroyed → Implied condition failed
- Contract **frustrated** (discharged); both parties excused

**Principle**: If foundational assumption destroyed → Contract frustrated

**Indian Law**: Section 56 incorporates Taylor v. Caldwell principle

---

## Grounds for Supervening Impossibility / Frustration

### 1. Destruction / Loss of Subject Matter

**Specific goods perish**

**Example**: Contract to sell specific car; car destroyed before delivery → VOID (Section 56)

**Sale of Goods Act 1930, Section 7**:
"Where there is a contract for the sale of specific goods, the contract is void if the goods, without the knowledge of the seller, have perished at the time when the contract is made."

**Section 8**: If goods perish before risk passes to buyer → Contract VOID

**Case**: Contract to sell specific painting; painting destroyed in fire → VOID

### 2. Death / Permanent Incapacity (Personal Service Contracts)

**Personal performance essential**; person dies or becomes incapable

**Example**: Contract for singer to perform; singer dies → VOID

**Case: Robinson v. Davison (1871)**:
- Contract for pianist to perform at concert
- Pianist fell seriously ill (unable to perform)
- Court held: Contract frustrated (personal incapacity)

**Non-Personal Service**: Death does NOT frustrate
- Example: Contract to supply goods; supplier (company) → Company's obligations pass to successors

### 3. Supervening Illegality / Change of Law

**Performance becomes illegal** after contract formed

**Example**: Contract to export goods; government bans export → VOID

**Example**: Contract during peace; war breaks out (trade with enemy becomes illegal) → VOID

**Case: Baily v. De Crespigny (1869)**:
- Contract to lease land; landlord agreed not to build on adjoining land
- Railway Company acquired land by compulsory statute (landlord had no choice)
- Court held: Contract frustrated (performance became illegal/impossible by law)

### 4. Non-Occurrence / Cancellation of Contemplated Event (Frustration of Purpose)

**Event that was foundation of contract doesn't occur**

**Coronation Cases** (English law, persuasive in India):

**Case: Krell v. Henry (1903)**:
- Contract to rent room for 2 days to view King Edward VII's coronation procession
- Coronation cancelled (King's illness)
- Court held: Contract frustrated (sole purpose was viewing procession; purpose frustrated)

**Contrast: Herne Bay Steamboat Co. v. Hutton (1903)**:
- Contract to hire steamboat to view naval review (King's fleet)
- Naval review cancelled
- Court held: Contract NOT frustrated (viewing fleet not sole purpose; cruise still possible)

**Distinction**: Frustration requires event to be **foundation** of contract (not merely motive)

**Indian Application**:

**Case: Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur & Co. (1954)** (Supreme Court):
- Contract for sale of land (building plots)
- Government requisitioned land (wartime acquisition; seller lost possession)
- Seller could not transfer land
- Supreme Court held: Contract frustrated (performance impossible due to requisition)

### 5. Excessive Delay / Inordinate Delay

**Performance delayed so long** that it defeats object of contract

**Test**: Would delayed performance be radically different from what was contracted?

**Example**: Contract to ship perishable goods; ship detained for months → Frustrated

**Case: Jackson v. Union Marine Insurance Co. (1874)**:
- Charter party (ship hire) for voyage to load cargo
- Ship damaged; repairs took 8 months
- Court held: Contract frustrated (delay so long that commercial purpose defeated)

**Indian Position**: Delay must be **substantial** and **defeat purpose**

### 6. Government Intervention / Expropriation

**Government action** makes performance impossible

**Examples**:
- **Requisition** of property (Satyabrata Ghose case)
- **Compulsory acquisition** of land
- **Wartime restrictions**

---

## Limitations on Doctrine of Frustration

**Frustration is NARROW doctrine** - Not available in all hardship cases

### 1. Self-Induced Frustration

**Rule**: If party's own act causes impossibility → NOT frustration

**Example**: Seller contracts to sell specific goods; seller sells goods to someone else → NOT frustration (self-induced)

**Case: Maritime National Fish Ltd. v. Ocean Trawlers Ltd. (1935)**:
- Contract to charter trawler (fishing license required)
- Charterer applied for 3 licenses; got only 1
- Charterer allocated license to own trawler (not chartered one)
- Court held: NOT frustration (charterer's choice caused impossibility)

### 2. Foreseen / Foreseeable Events

**Rule**: If event was foreseeable and should have been provided for → NOT frustration

**Example**: Contract in monsoon season; floods occur (foreseeable) → NOT frustration

**Test**: Would reasonable person have contemplated event and provided for it?

### 3. Mere Hardship / Increased Difficulty / Expense

**Rule**: Frustration ≠ Hardship

**Mere increase in cost or difficulty** does NOT frustrate contract

**Example**: Contract to supply goods at fixed price; cost of raw material increases 50% → NOT frustration (still possible, just less profitable)

**Case: Davis Contractors Ltd. v. Fareham UDC (1956)**:
- Construction contract for houses
- Labor shortage; costs increased; took longer
- Contractor claimed frustration
- Court held: NOT frustration (performance still possible; mere hardship/expense doesn't excuse)

**Indian Position**: Same (Section 56 requires impossibility, not hardship)

**Case: Alopi Parshad v. Union of India (1960)** (Supreme Court):
- Contract to supply food grains
- Government controlled prices; performance became unprofitable
- Court held: NOT frustration (still possible; economic hardship doesn't excuse)

### 4. Partial Impossibility

**Rule**: If part of contract impossible, rest still performs able → NOT frustrated (unless part essential)

**Example**: Contract to supply 1000 units; supplier can supply 800 → NOT frustrated (partial performance possible)

**But**: If essential part impossible → Frustrated

### 5. Alternative Modes of Performance Available

**Rule**: If alternative method exists → NOT frustrated

**Example**: Contract to transport goods by specific ship; ship unavailable but other ships available → NOT frustrated

---

## Contractual Provisions: Force Majeure Clauses

**Force Majeure**: "Superior force" (unforeseeable event beyond parties' control)

**Force Majeure Clause**: Contractual provision excusing performance if specified events occur

**Example Clause**:
"Neither party shall be liable for failure to perform due to acts of God, war, strikes, floods, government action, or other events beyond reasonable control."

**Effect**:
- **If clause present**: Clause governs (not Section 56)
- **If clause absent**: Section 56 applies

**Key Differences**:

| Aspect | Section 56 (Frustration) | Force Majeure Clause |
|--------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| **Scope** | Narrow (impossibility/unlawfulness) | Can be broader (include hardship) |
| **Effect** | Contract VOID (discharged) | Performance suspended (or excused) |
| **Contract Continues?** | NO (void) | YES (unless clause says terminate) |
| **Burden of Proof** | Party claiming must prove impossibility | Party claiming must prove event within clause |

**Case: Energy Watchdog v. CERC (2017)** (Supreme Court):
- Force majeure clause in Power Purchase Agreement
- Change of law affected economics
- Court held: Must prove event within force majeure clause; economic hardship alone not enough

**COVID-19 Pandemic**:

**Government Notification**: Ministry of Finance (Feb 2020) declared COVID-19 as force majeure event for government contracts

**Private Contracts**: Depends on:
1. **Force majeure clause**: If clause covers pandemic/epidemic → Excused
2. **Section 56**: If performance impossible (not mere hardship) → Frustrated

**Case: Halliburton Offshore Services v. Vedanta Ltd. (2020)** (Bombay High Court):
- COVID-19 lockdown; oil rig services
- Court held: Analyze whether performance impossible or mere difficulty

---

## Effect of Frustration / Supervening Impossibility

**Section 56**: "A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible... becomes void..."

**Contract becomes VOID** (not voidable)

### Consequences

#### 1. Parties Discharged from Future Obligations

**No further performance required** by either party

**Example**: Contract frustrated on June 1; neither party obliged to perform after June 1

#### 2. Loss Lies Where It Falls

**General Rule**: No compensation for loss due to frustration

**Reason**: Neither party at fault (supervening event beyond control)

**Example**: Buyer paid advance; goods perish before delivery (frustration)
- **General rule**: Buyer loses advance (loss lies where it falls)
- **Exception**: Sections 64-65 (restitution if benefit received)

#### 3. Restitution (Sections 64-65)

**Section 65**: "When an agreement is discovered to be void, or when a contract becomes void, any person who has received any advantage under such agreement or contract is bound to restore it, or to make compensation for it to the person from whom he received it."

**Mutual Restoration**:
- Money paid → Must be returned
- Goods delivered → Must be returned (or compensate)

**Limit**: Restore benefit actually received (not expected profit)

**Case: Fibrosa Spolka Akcyjna v. Fairbairn (1943)** (English case):
- Buyer paid advance for machinery
- Contract frustrated (war)
- Court held: Buyer can recover advance

**Indian Position**: Section 65 applies (parties restore benefits)

**But**: If benefit consumed/destroyed → May not be recoverable

---

## Contingent Contracts (Sections 32-36)

**Contingent Contract**: Performance depends on happening or non-happening of uncertain future event

**Section 32**: "If two or more parties enter into a contract, the performance of which is dependent on the happening of an event which may or may not happen, such contract is a contingent contract."

**Examples**:
- Insurance contract (pay if loss occurs)
- "I'll sell my house if I get transfer to another city"

### When Contingent Contract Becomes VOID

#### Section 33: Event Becomes Impossible

```
"Contingent contracts to do or not to do anything if an uncertain future event happens, cannot be enforced by law, unless and until that event has happened. If the event becomes impossible, such contracts become void."
```

**Example**: "I'll pay you Rs. 1 lakh if A comes back from dead" → VOID (impossible event)

#### Section 34: Event Must Happen Within Fixed Time

```
"If the future event on which a contract is contingent is the way in which a person will act at an unspecified time, the event shall be considered to become impossible when such person does anything which renders it impossible that he should so act within any definite time, or otherwise than under further contingencies."
```

**Example**: "I'll pay if ship arrives by Dec 31"; ship sinks before Dec 31 → Contract VOID

#### Section 35: Contingent on Event NOT Happening

```
"Contingent contracts to do or not to do anything if an uncertain future event does not happen, can be enforced when the happening of that event becomes impossible, and not before."
```

**Example**: "I'll pay if ship does NOT arrive" → Enforceable when ship's arrival becomes impossible

---

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Destruction of Subject Matter

**Facts**: A contracts to sell specific vintage car to B for Rs. 50 lakhs; car destroyed in fire before delivery

**Analysis**:
- **Supervening impossibility**: Subject matter destroyed (Section 56)
- **Effect**: Contract VOID; both parties discharged
- **Restitution**: If B paid advance, A must return

### Example 2: Personal Incapacity

**Facts**: Famous singer contracts to perform at wedding; singer has accident (permanent injury; cannot sing)

**Analysis**:
- **Personal service**: Yes (famous singer)
- **Permanent incapacity**: Cannot perform
- **Effect**: Contract VOID (Section 56)

### Example 3: Frustration of Purpose

**Facts**: A rents hall from B for wedding on Dec 25; wedding cancelled (bride elopes with someone else)

**Analysis**:
- **Cancellation of wedding**: Frustration of purpose?
- **Test**: Was wedding viewing foundation of contract (like Krell v. Henry)?
- **Likely**: NOT frustrated (hall rental not dependent on wedding; A could use for other purpose)

### Example 4: Mere Hardship (NOT Frustration)

**Facts**:
- A contracts to supply steel at Rs. 50/kg (1000 kg = Rs. 50,000)
- Steel prices rise to Rs. 80/kg
- A claims frustration (uneconomical to perform)

**Analysis**:
- **Hardship**: Yes (A will lose money)
- **Impossibility**: NO (A can still supply steel)
- **Section 56**: Does not apply (mere economic hardship)
- **Effect**: Contract NOT frustrated; A must perform (or pay damages for breach)

### Example 5: Force Majeure Clause

**Facts**:
- Construction contract with force majeure clause covering "acts of God, floods, earthquakes"
- Earthquake damages site; 3-month delay
- Force majeure clause suspends performance during event

**Analysis**:
- **Earthquake**: Within force majeure clause
- **Effect**: Performance suspended (not contract void)
- **After earthquake ends**: Parties resume performance

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Confusing Hardship with Impossibility

**Mistake**: Claiming frustration because performance became difficult/expensive

**Correct**: Frustration requires impossibility or unlawfulness (not mere hardship)

### 2. Self-Induced Impossibility

**Mistake**: Claiming frustration when party's own act caused impossibility

**Correct**: Self-induced events do NOT frustrate

### 3. Foreseeable Events

**Mistake**: Claiming frustration for event that should have been anticipated

**Correct**: Foreseeable risks should be allocated in contract (frustration unavailable)

### 4. Confusing Section 56 with Force Majeure

**Mistake**: Thinking Section 56 same as force majeure clause

**Correct**:
- **Section 56**: Statutory doctrine (narrow); contract VOID
- **Force majeure**: Contractual clause (can be broader); performance suspended

### 5. Expecting Compensation After Frustration

**Mistake**: Thinking frustrated party gets damages

**Correct**: No damages (neither party at fault); loss lies where it falls (except restitution under Section 65)

# Indemnity and Guarantee Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-INDEMNITY-GUARANTEE
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Special Contracts - Indemnity & Guarantee
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 124-147)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers two distinct but related special contracts:
1. **Contract of Indemnity** (Sections 124-125): Promise to compensate for loss
2. **Contract of Guarantee** (Sections 126-147): Promise to answer for another's debt/default

**Key Distinction**: Indemnity has TWO parties (indemnifier, indemnity-holder); Guarantee has THREE parties (creditor, principal debtor, surety).

---

## PART A: CONTRACT OF INDEMNITY

### Section 124: Contract of Indemnity Defined

```
"A contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself, or by the conduct of any other person, is called a 'contract of indemnity'."
```

**Parties**:
1. **Indemnifier**: Person who promises to indemnify (make good the loss)
2. **Indemnity-holder**: Person whose loss is to be made good

**Essential Elements**:
1. Promise to make good loss
2. Loss caused by indemnifier's conduct OR any other person's conduct
3. Consideration (must be present like any contract)

**Example**: A promises to compensate B for any loss B may suffer if B lends money to C. A is indemnifier; B is indemnity-holder.

---

### Types of Indemnity

#### 1. Express Indemnity

**Explicitly stated** in contract

**Example**: Insurance contracts (insurer indemnifies insured)

#### 2. Implied Indemnity

**Implied by law** from circumstances

**Example**: 
- Principal-agent relationship (principal indemnifies agent for lawful acts)
- Director acts on company's behalf (company indemnifies)

---

### Section 125: Rights of Indemnity-Holder

**Indemnity-holder can recover** from indemnifier:

```
"The promisee in a contract of indemnity, acting within the scope of his authority, is entitled to recover from the promisor—

(1) all damages which he may be compelled to pay in any suit in respect of any matter to which the promise to indemnify applies;

(2) all costs which he may be compelled to pay in any such suit if, in bringing or defending it, he did not contravene the orders of the promisor, and acted as it would have been prudent for him to act in the absence of any contract of indemnity, or if the promisor authorized him to bring or defend the suit;

(3) all sums which he may have paid under the terms of any compromise of any such suit, if the compromise was not contrary to the orders of the promisor, and was one which it would have been prudent for the promisee to make in the absence of any contract of indemnity, or if the promisor authorized him to compromise the suit."
```

**Three Categories of Recoverable Amounts**:

#### 1. Damages Compelled to Pay

**All damages** paid in suit relating to indemnified matter

**Example**: 
- A indemnifies B against loss from C
- C sues B; court awards Rs. 10 lakhs to C
- B can recover Rs. 10 lakhs from A (indemnifier)

#### 2. Costs of Suit

**Legal costs** incurred in bringing/defending suit

**Conditions**:
- Did not contravene indemnifier's orders, AND
- Acted prudently (as would have acted without indemnity), OR
- Indemnifier authorized suit

**Example**: B incurs Rs. 2 lakhs legal fees defending against C → B can recover from A (if prudent/authorized)

#### 3. Compromise Amounts

**Sums paid** in settlement/compromise

**Conditions**:
- Not contrary to indemnifier's orders, AND
- Prudent compromise, OR
- Indemnifier authorized compromise

**Example**: B settles with C for Rs. 5 lakhs (instead of fighting for Rs. 10 lakhs claim) → B can recover Rs. 5 lakhs from A (if prudent)

---

### When Does Indemnity-Holder's Right Arise?

**Indian Law** (Gajanan Moreshwar v. Moreshwar Madan - 1942, Bombay HC):

**ONLY after indemnity-holder suffers actual loss** (not when liability arises)

**Example**:
- C sues B for Rs. 10 lakhs
- A (indemnifier) promises to indemnify B
- **Indian view**: B can claim from A only after court orders B to pay (not when suit filed)

**English Law** (contrast): Indemnity-holder can claim when liability arises (even before actual payment)

**Criticism**: Indian view may leave indemnity-holder without funds to pay; some courts now allow claim when liability definite (even if not yet paid)

---

### Indemnity in Insurance Contracts

**Insurance = Contract of Indemnity** (except life insurance)

**Insurer indemnifies insured** against specified losses

**Principles**:
1. **Insurable Interest**: Insured must have interest in subject matter
2. **Utmost Good Faith** (uberrima fides): Full disclosure required
3. **Indemnity**: Compensate actual loss (not profit)
4. **Subrogation**: Insurer steps into insured's shoes (can sue wrongdoer)
5. **Contribution**: If multiple insurers, share loss proportionately

**Example**: Fire insurance on house (Rs. 50 lakhs insured value)
- House burns; actual loss Rs. 40 lakhs
- Insurer pays Rs. 40 lakhs (indemnity for actual loss)

## PART B: CONTRACT OF GUARANTEE

### Section 126: Contract of Guarantee Defined

```
"A 'contract of guarantee' is a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his default. The person who gives the guarantee is called the 'surety'; the person in respect of whose default the guarantee is given is called the 'principal debtor', and the person to whom the guarantee is given is called the 'creditor'. A guarantee may be either oral or written."
```

**Three Parties**:
1. **Creditor**: Person to whom guarantee given (lender, seller)
2. **Principal Debtor**: Person whose debt/default guaranteed (borrower, buyer)
3. **Surety**: Person who gives guarantee (guarantor)

**Nature**: **Surety promises creditor** to pay/perform if principal debtor defaults

**Example**: 
- C (bank) lends Rs. 10 lakhs to B (borrower)
- A signs as guarantor (surety)
- If B defaults, A must pay C

---

### Essential Features

#### 1. Three Parties Required

**Unlike indemnity** (two parties)

#### 2. Concurrence of Three Parties

**All three must agree** (creditor, principal debtor, surety)

#### 3. Primary Liability on Principal Debtor

**Surety's liability = Secondary** (only if principal debtor defaults)

**Primary obligation**: Principal debtor must pay

**Surety's obligation**: Contingent on default

#### 4. Existing or Future Debt

**Can guarantee existing debt** OR future debt

**Example**: Guarantee for running account (future transactions)

#### 5. Oral or Written

**Section 126**: May be oral or written (BUT some statutes require writing, e.g., Statute of Frauds in some jurisdictions)

**Consideration**: Must be present (BUT consideration from creditor to principal debtor sufficient - Section 127)

---

### Section 127: Consideration for Guarantee

```
"Anything done, or any promise made, for the benefit of the principal debtor, may be a sufficient consideration to the surety for giving the guarantee."
```

**Rule**: **Consideration to principal debtor** is sufficient consideration for surety's guarantee

**Surety need not receive direct benefit**

**Example**:
- Bank lends Rs. 10 lakhs to B (principal debtor)
- A guarantees loan (surety)
- **Consideration**: Loan to B (not to A) is sufficient consideration for A's guarantee

**Case: Kashiba v. Shripat (1894)**:
- C lent money to B
- A guaranteed repayment
- Court held: Loan to B is consideration for A's guarantee (A need not receive anything)

---

### Section 128: Surety's Liability

```
"The liability of the surety is co-extensive with that of the principal debtor, unless it is otherwise provided by the contract."
```

**Co-extensive Liability**: Surety liable for **same amount** and **same extent** as principal debtor

**Example**: 
- Principal debtor owes Rs. 10 lakhs + interest
- Surety liable for Rs. 10 lakhs + interest (same extent)

**But**: Contract can **limit** surety's liability

**Example**: "Guarantee limited to Rs. 5 lakhs" → Surety liable only up to Rs. 5 lakhs (even if debt Rs. 10 lakhs)

---

### Types of Guarantee

#### 1. Specific Guarantee (Section 129)

**For single transaction** or specific debt

**Discharge**: When guaranteed debt paid or contract performed

**Example**: A guarantees B's loan of Rs. 5 lakhs from C (specific loan) → Surety's liability ends when Rs. 5 lakhs repaid

#### 2. Continuing Guarantee (Section 129)

**For series of transactions** (running account, multiple debts)

**Extends to**: All transactions until revoked

**Example**: A guarantees B's account with C (supplier) up to Rs. 10 lakhs → Covers all transactions until A revokes

**Section 130**: Continuing guarantee for future transactions revocable (as to future transactions) by:
- Notice to creditor (by surety), OR
- Death of surety (for future transactions; existing liabilities continue)

---

### Rights of Surety

#### 1. Rights Against Creditor

##### a) Section 141: Creditor Must Sue Principal Debtor First?

**General Rule**: **NO** - Creditor can sue surety directly (without suing principal debtor first)

**Surety's liability = Joint with principal debtor** (creditor's choice whom to sue)

**But**: Contract may require creditor to first proceed against principal debtor

##### b) Section 133: Release by Creditor

**If creditor releases principal debtor** → Surety also discharged

**Reason**: Surety's right of subrogation (sue principal debtor) would be affected

**Example**: C agrees to release B from Rs. 10 lakhs debt → A (surety) also discharged

**Exception**: If release is with surety's consent OR "without prejudice to surety's rights"

##### c) Section 134: Material Variance

**If contract between creditor and principal debtor varied** (without surety's consent) → Surety discharged (for transactions after variance)

**Material Variance**: Significant change affecting surety's risk

**Example**: 
- Original loan: Rs. 5 lakhs at 10% interest
- Varied to: Rs. 10 lakhs at 15% interest (without A's consent)
- A (surety) discharged

**Case: Bank of Bihar v. Damodar Prasad (1969)** (Supreme Court):
- Bank agreed to reduce interest (variation)
- Court held: Material alteration without surety's consent → Surety discharged

##### d) Section 135: Creditor's Conduct

**If creditor does any act** (or omits to do act) that impairs surety's remedy against principal debtor → Surety discharged

**Examples**:
- Creditor releases security provided by principal debtor
- Creditor fails to register charge (loses priority)

**Section 139**: Creditor's release of co-surety (without consent) → Other sureties discharged (to extent of released surety's share)

##### e) Section 142: Guarantee Obtained by Misrepresentation/Concealment

**If guarantee obtained by**:
- **Misrepresentation** by creditor, OR
- **Concealment of material facts** (relating to principal debtor's credit/affairs)

**Effect**: Guarantee INVALID

**Example**: Bank conceals principal debtor's prior defaults → Surety's guarantee invalid

**But**: If surety knew facts OR creditor had no knowledge → Guarantee valid

#### 2. Rights Against Principal Debtor

##### a) Section 140: Right of Subrogation

**After paying creditor**, surety is **subrogated** to all creditor's rights against principal debtor

**Subrogation**: Surety steps into creditor's shoes

**Surety can**:
1. Sue principal debtor for amount paid
2. Enforce all securities held by creditor
3. Stand in creditor's place (as if surety paid his own debt and is now creditor)

**Example**:
- A (surety) pays Rs. 10 lakhs to C (creditor) on B's default
- A now has all C's rights:
  - Sue B for Rs. 10 lakhs
  - Enforce mortgage held by C
  - Claim interest, costs (as C could)

**Section 140 applies even if surety pays part** (subrogated pro rata)

##### b) Section 145: Right to Indemnity

**Surety has right** to be indemnified by principal debtor

**Implied contract**: Principal debtor must indemnify surety for all amounts paid + costs

**Example**: A pays Rs. 10 lakhs + Rs. 1 lakh legal fees → B must indemnify A for Rs. 11 lakhs

#### 3. Rights Against Co-Sureties

##### a) Section 146: Contribution Among Co-Sureties

**If multiple sureties** guarantee same debt → Liable to contribute equally (unless contract provides otherwise)

**Equal Contribution** (in absence of agreement)

**Example**:
- Principal debtor defaults on Rs. 30 lakhs
- Three sureties: A, B, C (no limit specified)
- Each liable for Rs. 10 lakhs (equal contribution)

**Unequal Amounts Guaranteed**:
- A guarantees Rs. 20 lakhs
- B guarantees Rs. 10 lakhs
- Principal debtor defaults on Rs. 30 lakhs
- **Contribution**: Pro rata to guaranteed amounts
  - A pays Rs. 20 lakhs (max)
  - B pays Rs. 10 lakhs (max)
  - Total Rs. 30 lakhs covered

**If one surety pays more than his share** → Can claim contribution from co-sureties

---

### Discharge of Surety

**Surety's liability ends** in following cases:

#### 1. Payment / Performance (Section 143)

**Discharge**: When principal debtor pays debt or performs obligation

**Complete satisfaction** of creditor → Surety discharged

#### 2. Release by Creditor (Section 133)

**If creditor releases principal debtor** → Surety discharged

**If creditor releases co-surety** (Section 138) → Other sureties discharged to extent of released surety's share

#### 3. Variation in Contract (Section 133)

**Material alteration** without surety's consent → Surety discharged

#### 4. Creditor's Act/Omission (Section 135)

**Creditor does act** impairing surety's remedy → Surety discharged

**Examples**:
- Releases security
- Fails to sue within limitation period
- Gives time to principal debtor without surety's consent (Section 136)

**Section 136**: Giving time to principal debtor = Variance (surety discharged)

**Exception**: Agreement "without prejudice to surety"

#### 5. Revocation (Continuing Guarantee - Section 130)

**Continuing guarantee**: Revocable by notice (for future transactions)

**Death of surety**: Continuing guarantee terminates (for future transactions)

#### 6. Loss of Security (Section 141)

**If creditor loses or parts with security** (without surety's consent) → Surety discharged (to extent of value of security lost)

#### 7. Composition or Promise Not to Sue (Section 143)

**If creditor compounds with principal debtor** OR agrees not to sue → Surety discharged (unless surety consents)

---

## Indemnity vs. Guarantee: Key Differences

| Aspect | Indemnity | Guarantee |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| **Parties** | TWO (indemnifier, indemnity-holder) | THREE (creditor, principal debtor, surety) |
| **Nature of Liability** | Primary (indemnifier's own act or another's act) | Secondary (contingent on principal debtor's default) |
| **Liability Arises** | When loss occurs | When principal debtor defaults |
| **Request** | No request from third party (indemnifier promises to save from loss) | Surety at request of principal debtor (or creditor) |
| **Right to Sue Third Party** | Indemnity-holder can sue if loss by third party | Surety can sue principal debtor after payment |
| **Discharge** | By payment or performance | By payment, release, variance, creditor's act, etc. (Sections 133-141) |
| **Number of Contracts** | ONE (between indemnifier and indemnity-holder) | THREE (creditor-debtor, creditor-surety, surety-debtor) |
| **Examples** | Insurance, principal-agent indemnity | Loan guarantees, performance bonds |

**Case: Bank of Bihar v. Damodar Prasad (1969)** (Supreme Court):
- Distinguished indemnity and guarantee
- Key: If liability primary (indemnifier's own promise to pay) → Indemnity
- If liability secondary (pay if another defaults) → Guarantee

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Indemnity - Section 125 Rights

**Facts**:
- A (landlord) indemnifies B (tenant) against loss from C (third party claiming ownership)
- C sues B for possession
- B defends; incurs Rs. 2 lakhs legal costs
- Court orders B to pay C Rs. 10 lakhs (C proved ownership)

**Analysis**:
- **Contract of indemnity**: A promised to save B from loss
- **Section 125(1)**: Damages compelled to pay → Rs. 10 lakhs recoverable from A
- **Section 125(2)**: Costs of defending → Rs. 2 lakhs recoverable (if prudent defense)
- **Total**: B can recover Rs. 12 lakhs from A (indemnifier)

### Example 2: Guarantee - Co-extensive Liability

**Facts**:
- C (bank) lends Rs. 10 lakhs to B (borrower) at 12% interest
- A signs as surety (guarantees repayment)
- B defaults after paying Rs. 3 lakhs; owes Rs. 7 lakhs + interest Rs. 1 lakh

**Analysis**:
- **Section 128**: Surety's liability co-extensive with principal debtor
- **A's liability**: Rs. 7 lakhs (principal) + Rs. 1 lakh (interest) = Rs. 8 lakhs
- **Creditor's choice**: C can sue A directly (need not sue B first - Section 141)

### Example 3: Material Variance - Surety Discharged

**Facts**:
- A guarantees B's loan of Rs. 5 lakhs from C at 10% interest (3-year term)
- After 1 year, C and B agree: Increase to Rs. 8 lakhs at 15% interest (without A's consent)
- B defaults on Rs. 8 lakhs

**Analysis**:
- **Material variance** (Section 134): Amount increased, interest increased
- **Without surety's consent**: A not consulted
- **Effect**: A discharged (for variation part)
- **Result**: A liable only for original Rs. 5 lakhs at 10% (not Rs. 8 lakhs at 15%)

### Example 4: Continuing Guarantee - Revocation

**Facts**:
- A guarantees B's account with C (supplier) up to Rs. 20 lakhs (continuing guarantee)
- Transactions: Month 1 (Rs. 5 lakhs), Month 2 (Rs. 3 lakhs), Month 3 (Rs. 4 lakhs)
- After Month 3, A revokes guarantee by notice to C
- Month 4: B buys Rs. 6 lakhs goods; defaults on entire Rs. 18 lakhs

**Analysis**:
- **Section 130**: Continuing guarantee revocable by notice
- **Revocation effect**: Applies to future transactions only
- **A's liability**: Months 1-3 (Rs. 12 lakhs) + any transaction before revocation notice
- **Not liable**: Month 4 (Rs. 6 lakhs) if transaction after revocation notice received by C
- **Result**: A liable up to Rs. 12 lakhs (transactions before revocation)

### Example 5: Surety's Right of Subrogation

**Facts**:
- C lends Rs. 10 lakhs to B (secured by mortgage on B's property)
- A (surety) guarantees loan
- B defaults; A pays Rs. 10 lakhs to C

**Analysis**:
- **Section 140**: Surety subrogated to creditor's rights
- **A's rights after payment**:
  1. Sue B for Rs. 10 lakhs (right of indemnity - Section 145)
  2. Enforce mortgage on B's property (subrogation to C's security)
  3. Claim interest, costs as C could have
- **Result**: A has all remedies C had against B

### Example 6: Contribution Among Co-Sureties

**Facts**:
- Principal debtor owes Rs. 30 lakhs
- Three sureties: A (guaranteed Rs. 15 lakhs), B (guaranteed Rs. 10 lakhs), C (guaranteed Rs. 5 lakhs)
- Creditor sues A; A pays full Rs. 30 lakhs

**Analysis**:
- **Section 146**: Co-sureties contribute equally (or pro rata if unequal guarantees)
- **Guaranteed amounts total**: Rs. 30 lakhs (Rs. 15L + Rs. 10L + Rs. 5L)
- **Each liable**: Pro rata to guaranteed amount
  - A: (15/30) × Rs. 30L = Rs. 15 lakhs
  - B: (10/30) × Rs. 30L = Rs. 10 lakhs
  - C: (5/30) × Rs. 30L = Rs. 5 lakhs
- **A paid Rs. 30 lakhs**: Can claim contribution:
  - From B: Rs. 10 lakhs
  - From C: Rs. 5 lakhs
- **A's final liability**: Rs. 15 lakhs (after contribution)

---

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Confusing Indemnity with Guarantee

**Mistake**: Treating two-party promise to pay as guarantee

**Correct**: 
- **Two parties** → Indemnity
- **Three parties** (creditor, debtor, surety) → Guarantee

### 2. Assuming Creditor Must Sue Principal Debtor First

**Mistake**: Thinking surety liable only after creditor exhausts remedies against principal debtor

**Correct**: Creditor can sue surety directly (Section 141); surety's liability joint with principal debtor

### 3. Not Getting Surety's Consent for Variations

**Mistake**: Creditor and principal debtor vary contract (increase amount, extend time) without informing surety

**Correct**: Material variation without surety's consent discharges surety (Section 134)

### 4. Releasing Principal Debtor Without Considering Surety

**Mistake**: Creditor releases principal debtor, thinking can still claim from surety

**Correct**: Releasing principal debtor discharges surety (Section 133) unless release "without prejudice to surety"

### 5. Continuing Guarantee After Surety's Death

**Mistake**: Creditor continues extending credit after surety's death, expecting surety's estate liable

**Correct**: Death revokes continuing guarantee (for future transactions - Section 130); estate liable only for debts up to death

# Injunction Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-INJUNCTION
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Remedies - Injunction
- **Statute**: Specific Relief Act 1963 (Sections 36-42), Code of Civil Procedure 1908 (Order 39 Rule 1-2)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1963

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **injunctions** - court orders restraining a party from doing (prohibitory injunction) or compelling a party to do (mandatory injunction) a specific act, typically to prevent breach of contract or continuing wrong.

**Key Principle**: Injunction is equitable remedy to prevent irreparable harm where damages are inadequate.

---

## Types of Injunctions

### 1. Temporary / Interim Injunction (Order 39 Rule 1-2 CPC)

**Purpose**: Preserve status quo during pendency of suit

**Duration**: Until final judgment

**Example**: Preventing defendant from selling disputed property during lawsuit

### 2. Perpetual / Permanent Injunction (Sections 36-42 Specific Relief Act)

**Purpose**: Final relief after trial

**Duration**: Permanent (enforceable indefinitely)

**Example**: Permanently restraining defendant from breaching non-compete clause

### 3. Prohibitory Injunction

**Restrains**: Prevents party from doing an act

**Example**: "Defendant restrained from operating competing business within 5 km"

### 4. Mandatory Injunction

**Compels**: Requires party to do an act (undo wrong)

**Example**: "Defendant directed to remove encroachment from plaintiff's land"

**Note**: Mandatory injunction granted sparingly (more intrusive)

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 36: Preventive Relief - When Injunction Granted

```
"(1) A perpetual injunction may be granted to the plaintiff to prevent the breach of an obligation existing in his favour, whether expressly or by implication.

(2) When such obligation arises from contract, the court shall be guided by the rules and provisions contained in Chapter II."
```

**Conditions**:
1. **Obligation** exists in plaintiff's favor
2. **Breach** of obligation (actual or threatened)
3. **Damages inadequate** remedy

### Section 37: Injunction When Refused

**Section 37**: Injunction cannot be granted:

#### (a) To Restrain Judicial/Legislative Proceedings

Cannot prevent court proceedings or legislative acts

**Example**: Cannot restrain someone from filing suit

#### (b) To Restrain Criminal Proceedings

Cannot prevent prosecution for criminal offense

**Exception**: Can restrain if prosecution malicious/vexatious AND no adequate remedy

#### (c) When Equally Efficacious Relief Possible

If plaintiff can get adequate remedy otherwise → Injunction refused

**Example**: If damages sufficient → Injunction not needed

#### (d) Breach of Contract Not Specifically Enforceable

**If specific performance not available** (e.g., personal service) → Injunction for positive covenant also not available

**But**: Negative covenant can be enforced by injunction (see below)

#### (e) Multiplicity of Proceedings

If injunction would cause multiple/complex legal proceedings → May be refused

#### (f) Against Government for Non-Performance of Contract

Generally cannot enjoin government from breaching contract (sovereign immunity)

### Section 38: Damages in Addition to Injunction

**Section 38(1)**: "The plaintiff in a suit for perpetual injunction may claim damages, either in addition to, or in substitution for, such injunction."

**Options**:
1. Injunction + Damages
2. Damages instead of injunction (if injunction refused)

---

## Injunctions in Contract Law

### A. Negative Covenants (Section 42)

**Section 42**: "Where a contract comprises an affirmative agreement to do a certain act, coupled with a negative agreement, express or implied, not to do a certain act, the circumstance that the court is unable to compel specific performance of the affirmative agreement shall not preclude it from granting an injunction to perform the negative agreement."

**Rule**: **Negative covenant** enforceable by injunction **even if positive covenant not specifically enforceable**

**Landmark Case: Lumley v. Wagner (1852)** (English case, persuasive in India):
- Opera singer contracted to sing at plaintiff's theater
- Negative covenant: "Will not sing at other theaters during contract period"
- Singer breached; agreed to sing at competitor's theater
- Court held: Cannot compel singer to sing (personal service), BUT can grant injunction restraining from singing elsewhere

**Application**:
- **Positive covenant**: "Employee will work for employer" → NOT specifically enforceable (personal service)
- **Negative covenant**: "Employee will not work for competitor" → Enforceable by injunction

**Example**: Employment contract with non-compete clause
- Cannot force employee to work (specific performance not available)
- Can restrain employee from working for competitor (injunction available)

**Case: Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning (1967)** (Supreme Court):
- Post-employment non-compete clause (5 years)
- Court held: Unreasonable restraint (Section 27 Contract Act); injunction refused
- **But**: Reasonable restraint during employment → Injunction possible

**Reasonable Negative Covenants**:
- Non-disclosure of trade secrets
- Non-solicitation of clients (reasonable period)
- Non-compete during employment

**Unreasonable Negative Covenants**:
- Permanent non-compete
- Geographically unlimited restraint
- Post-employment non-compete (generally; Section 27)

### B. Breach of Confidence / Trade Secrets

**Example**: Employee about to disclose confidential information
- Injunction to prevent disclosure (protect trade secrets)

**Requirements**:
1. Information confidential
2. Obligation of confidence exists
3. Threatened breach
4. Damages inadequate

### C. Breach of Exclusive Dealing Agreements

**Example**: Distributor agrees to sell only plaintiff's products; starts selling competitor's
- Injunction to restrain selling competitor's products

---

## Temporary Injunction (Order 39 Rule 1-2 CPC)

**Purpose**: Interim relief during pendency of suit

### Order 39 Rule 1: When Temporary Injunction Granted

```
"Where in any suit it is proved by affidavit or otherwise—

(a) that any property in dispute in a suit is in danger of being wasted, damaged or alienated by any party to the suit, or wrongfully sold in execution of a decree, or
(b) that the defendant threatens, or intends to remove or dispose of his property with a view to defrauding his creditors, or
(c) that the defendant threatens to dispossess the plaintiff or otherwise cause injury to the plaintiff in relation to any property in dispute in the suit,

the Court may... grant a temporary injunction to restrain such act, or make such other order for the purpose of staying and preventing the wasting, damaging, alienation, sale, removal or disposition of the property or dispossession of the plaintiff, or otherwise causing injury to the plaintiff in relation to any property in dispute in the suit..."
```

**Conditions**:
1. **Prima facie case**: Plaintiff shows arguable case
2. **Balance of convenience**: In plaintiff's favor
3. **Irreparable injury**: Plaintiff will suffer irreparable harm if injunction not granted

**Test (Three-Fold)**:

#### 1. Prima Facie Case

**Question**: Does plaintiff show serious question to be tried?

**Standard**: Not conclusive proof; just arguable case

**Example**: Plaintiff shows contract exists and breach threatened

#### 2. Balance of Convenience

**Question**: Would plaintiff or defendant suffer greater hardship?

**Balancing**:
- Hardship to plaintiff if injunction NOT granted
- vs Hardship to defendant if injunction granted

**Example**: If plaintiff's business destroyed vs defendant's minor inconvenience → Balance favors plaintiff

#### 3. Irreparable Injury

**Question**: Can damages adequately compensate plaintiff?

**Irreparable**: Cannot be compensated by money

**Examples**:
- Loss of goodwill
- Disclosure of trade secrets
- Destruction of unique property

**Case: Dorab Cawasji Warden v. Coomi Sorab Warden (1990)** (Supreme Court):
- Temporary injunction application
- Court held: Must satisfy all three conditions (prima facie case, balance of convenience, irreparable injury)

---

## Quia Timet Injunction (Anticipatory)

**Quia Timet**: "Because he fears"

**Preventive Injunction**: To prevent anticipated future wrong

**Requirements**:
1. **Strong probability** of future injury (not mere speculation)
2. **Imminent** danger
3. **Irreparable** harm if not prevented

**Example**: Neighbor building structure that will cause damage to plaintiff's property
- Injunction to prevent construction (before actual damage occurs)

**Case: Redland Bricks Ltd. v. Morris (1970)**:
- Defendant's excavation threatened plaintiff's land (subsidence risk)
- Court held: Quia timet injunction granted (prevent future damage)

---

## Mandatory Injunction

**Compels Positive Act**: Requires defendant to do something (undo wrong)

**Standard**: **Higher** than prohibitory injunction (more intrusive)

**When Granted**:
1. **Clear legal right** violated
2. **Defendant caused wrongful change** in status quo
3. **Restoration necessary** (cannot be compensated by damages)

**Example**: Defendant encroached on plaintiff's land
- Mandatory injunction to remove encroachment

**Case: Mahadeolal Kanodia v. Administrator General of West Bengal (1960)**:
- Defendant wrongfully occupied plaintiff's property
- Court held: Mandatory injunction to vacate (restore status quo ante)

**Contrast**: Prohibitory injunction preserves existing state; mandatory injunction restores previous state

---

## Defenses to Injunction

### 1. Plaintiff's Delay / Laches

**Unreasonable Delay**: Plaintiff delayed in seeking relief

**Example**: Breach in 2020; plaintiff seeks injunction in 2024 → May be refused (laches)

**Acquiescence**: Plaintiff silently permitted act → Cannot later object

### 2. Clean Hands Doctrine

**"He who seeks equity must do equity"**

**Plaintiff's misconduct**: If plaintiff breached or acted inequitably → Injunction refused

**Example**: Plaintiff also breached contract → May be estopped from seeking injunction

### 3. Impossibility / Hardship

**Extreme hardship** to defendant with minimal benefit to plaintiff → May be refused

**Balance**: Court weighs hardship to both parties

### 4. Public Interest

**If injunction against public interest** → Refused

**Example**: Injunction would shut down factory (thousands unemployed) for minor breach → May be refused

---

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Non-Compete Clause (Negative Covenant)

**Facts**:
- A (employee) signs employment contract with non-compete clause: "Will not work for competitor for 1 year after leaving"
- A resigns; joins competitor immediately

**Analysis**:
- **Negative covenant**: Section 42
- **Personal service**: Cannot compel A to work (specific performance unavailable)
- **But**: Can restrain A from working for competitor (injunction possible if reasonable)
- **Reasonableness**: 1 year, competitor only → Likely reasonable during employment; post-employment may violate Section 27
- **Result**: Injunction likely REFUSED (post-employment non-compete generally void in India under Section 27 Contract Act)

### Example 2: Trade Secret Protection

**Facts**:
- A (employee) has access to company's secret formula
- A about to join competitor; threatens to disclose formula

**Analysis**:
- **Confidential information**: Trade secret
- **Obligation**: Duty of confidence exists
- **Irreparable harm**: Disclosure cannot be undone; damages inadequate
- **Prima facie case**: Yes
- **Result**: Temporary injunction granted (prevent disclosure)

### Example 3: Exclusive Distribution Agreement

**Facts**:
- A (distributor) agrees to sell only B's (manufacturer) products in territory
- A starts selling competitor C's products

**Analysis**:
- **Negative covenant**: "Will not sell competitor's products"
- **Breach**: A selling C's products
- **Section 42**: Negative covenant enforceable
- **Result**: Injunction granted (restrain A from selling C's products)

### Example 4: Mandatory Injunction (Encroachment)

**Facts**:
- A owns land; B encroaches and builds shed on A's land
- A seeks injunction

**Analysis**:
- **Wrongful change**: B encroached (changed status quo)
- **Clear right**: A's ownership
- **Mandatory injunction**: To remove encroachment (restore status quo)
- **Result**: Mandatory injunction granted (B must remove shed)

### Example 5: Temporary Injunction (Property Sale)

**Facts**:
- A sues B for specific performance of land sale
- B about to sell land to C (third party)
- A seeks temporary injunction

**Analysis**:
- **Prima facie case**: Land sale contract exists
- **Irreparable injury**: If sold to C, A cannot get specific land
- **Balance of convenience**: Favors A (B can sell after trial if A loses)
- **Result**: Temporary injunction granted (B restrained from selling pending trial)

---

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Seeking Injunction for Personal Service

**Mistake**: Trying to compel employee to work via injunction

**Correct**: Cannot compel personal service (positive covenant); only negative covenant (non-compete, confidentiality) enforceable

### 2. Post-Employment Non-Compete in India

**Mistake**: Assuming post-employment non-compete enforceable

**Correct**: Generally VOID under Section 27 (Niranjan Shankar case); only reasonable restraints during employment valid

### 3. Delay in Seeking Injunction

**Mistake**: Waiting months/years before seeking injunction

**Correct**: Apply promptly; delay/laches → Injunction refused

### 4. Assuming Injunction Always Available

**Mistake**: Expecting injunction for all breaches

**Correct**: Only when damages inadequate; irreparable harm shown

### 5. Mandatory vs Prohibitory

**Mistake**: Treating mandatory injunction same as prohibitory

**Correct**: Mandatory injunction harder to get (more intrusive); requires clear legal right

# Lawful Object and Consideration Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-LEGALITY
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Contract Formation - Legality
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 23-24, 26-30)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **legality of object and consideration** - essential elements for valid contracts. Agreements with unlawful object or unlawful consideration are **VOID** (not merely voidable).

**Key Principle**: Contracts must serve lawful purposes and not contravene law, morality, or public policy.

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 10: Lawfulness Required

```
"All agreements are contracts if they are made... for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object..."
```

**Lawful Object AND Lawful Consideration** both required

### Section 23: Unlawful Consideration and Object

```
"The consideration or object of an agreement is lawful, unless—
(1) it is forbidden by law; or
(2) is of such a nature that, if permitted, it would defeat the provisions of any law; or
(3) is fraudulent; or
(4) involves or implies injury to the person or property of another; or
(5) the Court regards it as immoral, or opposed to public policy.

In each of these cases, the consideration or object of an agreement is said to be unlawful. Every agreement of which the object or consideration is unlawful is void."
```

**Five Grounds of Unlawfulness**:
1. Forbidden by law
2. Defeats provisions of law
3. Fraudulent
4. Injurious to person/property
5. Immoral or opposed to public policy

---

## Ground 1: Forbidden by Law

### Definition

**Forbidden by Law**: Prohibited by statute or regulation

**Effect**: Agreement VOID

### Examples

#### 1. Agreements to Commit Crime

**Example**: Contract to murder someone for payment → VOID

**Case: Gherulal Parekh v. Mahadeodas (1959)**:
- Agreement to smuggle goods (prohibited under Customs Act)
- Court held: VOID (forbidden by law)

#### 2. Wagering Agreements (Section 30)

**Section 30**: "Agreements by way of wager are void..."

**Wager**: Bet or gamble where parties have opposite interests; one wins, other loses

**Elements of Wager**:
1. **Future uncertain event** (outcome unknown)
2. **Mutual chance** of gain or loss
3. **No control** over event
4. **No other interest** except winning bet

**Example**: Bet on cricket match outcome → VOID

**Exceptions** (NOT wagering):
- **Insurance contracts**: Insurable interest exists (not wager)
- **Prize competitions**: Skill-based competitions (not wager)
- **Horse racing**: Expressly permitted by statute

**Case: Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball**: Reward for using product NOT wager (unilateral offer; consideration given)

#### 3. Unlicensed Business Agreements

**Example**: Contract by unlicensed money-lender (where license required by law) → VOID

**Case: Smith v. Mawhood (1845)**:
- Contract by unlicensed tobacco seller (license required)
- Court held: VOID (prohibited by law)

#### 4. Agreements for Prohibited Goods/Services

**Examples**:
- Sale of narcotics (prohibited under NDPS Act)
- Sale of human organs (prohibited under Transplantation of Human Organs Act)
- Child labor contracts (prohibited under Child Labour Act)

---

## Ground 2: Defeats Provisions of Law

### Definition

**Defeats Provisions of Law**: Agreement that, if enforced, would nullify or circumvent statutory provisions

**Effect**: Agreement VOID

**Rationale**: Cannot do indirectly what cannot be done directly

### Examples

#### 1. Agreements to Defeat Stamp Duty

**Example**: Two documents executed - one genuine (understating price to avoid stamp duty), one collateral (actual price) → Both VOID

**Case: Smt. Bibi Sayeeda v. Nawab Mohd. Najmul Ikram Khan (1940)**:
- Sale deed understated price to evade stamp duty
- Collateral agreement for balance payment
- Court held: Both documents VOID (defeat stamp law)

#### 2. Agreements to Defeat Insolvency Laws

**Example**: Insolvent transfers property to friend to hide from creditors → VOID

**Fraudulent preference**: Transfer to defeat creditors' rights

#### 3. Agreements in Restraint of Marriage (Section 26)

**Section 26**: "Every agreement in restraint of the marriage of any person, other than a minor, is void."

**Rule**: Cannot prevent adult from marrying

**Example**: Father's will: "Daughter gets property only if she never marries" → Condition VOID

**Exception**: Minor's marriage can be restricted (parent's right)

**Case: Lowe v. Peers (1768)**:
- Promise by man not to marry in return for payment
- Court held: VOID (restraint of marriage)

#### 4. Agreements in Restraint of Parental Rights

**Example**: Agreement that parent will not have custody of child → VOID (defeats family law)

---

## Ground 3: Fraudulent

### Definition

**Fraudulent Agreement**: Agreement itself is fraudulent scheme

**Effect**: Agreement VOID

**Distinction**: 
- **Fraud inducing contract**: Contract voidable (Section 17)
- **Fraudulent agreement**: Agreement itself fraud → VOID (Section 23)

### Examples

#### 1. Agreement to Defraud Third Party

**Example**: A and B agree to falsely claim C owes them money → VOID

**Case: Alexander v. Rayson (1936)**:
- Landlord and tenant agreed to split rent into two agreements (one actual, one false)
- Purpose: Reduce tenant's apparent liability (to defraud rating authority)
- Court held: VOID (fraudulent agreement)

#### 2. Conspiracy to Defraud

**Example**: Bidders at auction agree not to bid against each other (to buy cheap, defraud seller) → VOID

#### 3. Ponzi Schemes / Pyramid Schemes

**Example**: Agreement to run Ponzi scheme (pay old investors with new investors' money) → VOID

---

## Ground 4: Injurious to Person or Property

### Definition

**Injurious Agreement**: Agreement involves harm to person or property

**Effect**: Agreement VOID

### Examples

#### 1. Agreement to Injure Person

**Example**: Contract to assault someone → VOID

#### 2. Agreement to Damage Property

**Example**: Agreement to burn someone's house → VOID

#### 3. Agreement Endangering Life

**Example**: Agreement to participate in dangerous stunt without safety (endangering life) → May be VOID

**Case: Everett v. Williams (1725)** (Highwayman's case):
- Two highwaymen (robbers) agreed to share robbery proceeds
- Later disputed sharing
- Court held: VOID (illegal partnership to injure property of others)

---

## Ground 5: Immoral or Opposed to Public Policy

### A. Immoral Agreements

**Definition**: Agreements contrary to accepted moral standards

**Effect**: Agreement VOID

#### Examples of Immoral Agreements

**1. Agreements for Immoral Purposes**:
- Contract for prostitution
- Agreement to promote adultery

**Case: Pearce v. Brooks (1866)**:
- Contract to supply fancy carriage to prostitute (knowing purpose)
- Court held: VOID (immoral purpose)

**2. Agreements for Sale of Public Offices**:
- Agreement to buy/sell government position → VOID

**Case: Parkinson v. College of Ambulance (1925)**:
- Agreement to procure knighthood for payment
- Court held: VOID (immoral - sale of honors)

**3. Marriage Brokerage Contracts**:
- Agreement to arrange marriage for fee → VOID

**Rationale**: Marriage should be based on consent, not commercial transaction

### B. Agreements Opposed to Public Policy

**Definition**: Agreements harmful to public interest, even if not expressly forbidden

**Court's Discretion**: Court determines what violates public policy

**Categories**:

#### 1. Agreements in Restraint of Trade (Section 27)

**Section 27**: "Every agreement by which any one is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind, is to that extent void."

**General Rule**: Restraint of trade VOID

**Rationale**: Free competition benefits society; individual's right to work

**Example**: A agrees never to practice medicine → VOID

**EXCEPTIONS** (Restraints that are VALID):

**a) Sale of Goodwill (Section 27 Exception 1)**

**Section 27 Exception**: Buyer of goodwill can restrain seller from competing

**Requirements**:
1. **Sale of goodwill** of business
2. **Reasonable restraint** (time, geography, scope)

**Example**: A sells bakery to B; agrees not to open bakery within 5 km for 3 years → VALID

**Reasonableness Test**:
- **Time**: Not perpetual (3-5 years reasonable)
- **Geography**: Not worldwide (local area reasonable)
- **Scope**: Related business only

**Case: Nordenfelt v. Maxim Nordenfelt (1894)**:
- Seller of armaments business agreed not to compete worldwide for 25 years
- Court held: VALID (armaments business global; buyer needed worldwide protection)

**Indian Position**: More restrictive than English law; courts strictly scrutinize reasonableness

**Case: Madhub Chunder v. Raj Coomar Doss (1874)**:
- Attorney agreed not to practice in Calcutta after leaving firm
- Court held: VOID (unreasonable restraint)

**b) Partnership Agreement (Section 27 Exception 2)**

Partners can agree that some will not carry on business during partnership

**Example**: Partnership deed: "Partner A will not run separate business during partnership" → VALID

**c) Employment Contracts - Limited Validity**

**General Rule**: Cannot prevent employee from working after leaving

**Exception: Negative Covenants** (Limited enforceability):
- **Non-solicitation**: Cannot solicit former employer's clients (reasonable period) → May be valid
- **Non-compete**: Cannot work for competitor → Generally VOID in India (Section 27 strict)
- **Confidentiality**: Cannot disclose trade secrets → VALID (protects proprietary info)

**Case: Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning (1967)** (Supreme Court):
- Employment contract: Employee cannot work in similar business for 5 years after leaving
- Court held: VOID (violates Section 27)
- **BUT**: Reasonable restraint during employment valid

**Case: Gujarat Bottling Co. v. Coca Cola Co. (1995)**:
- Franchise agreement: Cannot sell competing products during agreement → VALID
- After termination: Cannot sell competing products → VOID

**b) Agreements in Restraint of Legal Proceedings (Section 28)

**Section 28**: "Every agreement, by which any party thereto is restricted absolutely from enforcing his rights under or in respect of any contract, by the usual legal proceedings in the ordinary tribunals, or which limits the time within which he may thus enforce his rights, is void to that extent."

**General Rule**: Cannot oust jurisdiction of courts

**Effect**: Clauses preventing party from suing → VOID

**Example**: Contract clause: "Parties cannot sue in court for any dispute" → VOID

**EXCEPTIONS**:

**i) Arbitration Clause (Section 28 Exception 1)**

**Arbitration Agreement**: Valid alternative dispute resolution

**Requirements**:
1. **After dispute arises** OR specific arbitration clause
2. **Does not bar courts** ultimately (arbitration award can be challenged in court)

**Example**: "All disputes will be resolved by arbitration" → VALID

**Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996**: Governs arbitration

**ii) Limitation of Time (Section 28 Exception 2)**

**Reasonable time limit**: Can shorten limitation period (but not extinguish right)

**Example**: "Claims must be made within 1 year (instead of 3 years)" → VALID (if reasonable)

**iii) Agreements on Uncertain Matters (Section 29)

**Section 29**: "Agreements, the meaning of which is not certain, or capable of being made certain, are void."

**Certainty Requirement**: Terms must be definite

**Example**: "A agrees to sell goods to B at fair price" → May be VOID (vague - what is "fair price"?)

**But**: If market standard exists or method to determine price → Valid

**Case: Guthing v. Lynn (1831)**:
- "I will give £5 more if horse is lucky"
- Court held: VOID (uncertain - what is "lucky"?)

**Case: May & Butcher v. The King (1934)**:
- Agreement to sell at price "to be agreed later"
- Court held: VOID (price essential; not agreed)

**Indian Position**: Courts try to save contracts if some mechanism to ascertain terms

#### 3. Agreements Interfering with Administration of Justice

**Examples**:
- Agreement to suppress prosecution
- Agreement to procure false evidence
- Agreement to pervert justice

**Case: Keir v. Leeman (1846)**:
- Agreement to compound (settle) criminal prosecution for theft
- Court held: VOID (interferes with justice)

#### 4. Agreements for Trading with Enemy

**During war**: Trading with enemy country VOID

**Rationale**: Aids enemy (against public policy)

#### 5. Agreements Promoting Corruption

**Example**: Agreement to bribe public official → VOID

**Case**: Any agreement involving corruption under Prevention of Corruption Act → VOID

#### 6. Champertous Agreements

**Champerty**: Agreement to finance litigation in return for share of proceeds

**Example**: Lawyer agrees to fund client's case; take 50% of winnings → VOID (champerty)

**Rationale**: Promotes unnecessary litigation

**Exception: Contingency fees** (Some jurisdictions allow; India generally prohibits)

---

## Section 24: Void Agreements if Consideration or Object Unlawful in Part

**Section 24**: "If any part of a single consideration for one or more objects, or any one or any part of any one of several considerations for a single object, is unlawful, the agreement is void."

**Rule**: If any part of consideration or object unlawful → Entire agreement VOID

**Indivisible Agreement**: Cannot sever unlawful part

### Example 1: Part Consideration Unlawful

**Facts**: A agrees to B: "I'll pay you Rs. 10,000 to (1) deliver goods (lawful), and (2) kill my enemy (unlawful)"

**Analysis**:
- **Single consideration**: Rs. 10,000
- **Multiple objects**: (1) Lawful, (2) Unlawful
- **Effect**: Entire agreement VOID (cannot separate unlawful part)

### Example 2: Severable Agreement

**Facts**: Two separate agreements: (1) Pay Rs. 5,000 for delivering goods, (2) Pay Rs. 5,000 for killing enemy

**Analysis**:
- **Severable**: Two distinct agreements
- **Effect**: Agreement (1) VALID; Agreement (2) VOID

**Severability Test**: Can lawful part stand independently?

---

## Doctrine of "In Pari Delicto"

**Maxim**: "In pari delicto potior est conditio defendentis"

**Translation**: "In equal wrongdoing, the position of the defendant is stronger"

**Rule**: If both parties equally guilty (in pari delicto), court will not assist either party

**Effect**: Money/property advanced under illegal contract cannot be recovered

**Case: Holman v. Johnson (1775)**:
- Smuggling agreement
- Buyer paid; seller failed to deliver smuggled goods
- Court held: Cannot recover payment (both parties guilty)

### Exception: Not In Pari Delicto

**If parties NOT equally guilty**, innocent party can recover

**Examples**:
1. **Repentance before performance**: Party withdraws before illegal act → Can recover
2. **Oppression**: Weaker party forced into illegal contract → Can recover
3. **Statute protects one class**: Law protecting specific class of persons

**Case: Hermann v. Jeuchner (1885)**:
- Contract for future prostitution
- Woman repented before performing
- Court held: Can recover payment (not in pari delicto; repented)

---

## Practical Implications

### Due Diligence Checklist

**Before entering contract, verify**:

1. ✓ **Lawful object**: Purpose lawful under all applicable laws?
2. ✓ **Lawful consideration**: Payment/exchange lawful?
3. ✓ **Licenses/permissions**: Required licenses obtained?
4. ✓ **Restraint of trade**: Any non-compete clauses reasonable? (Sale of goodwill exception?)
5. ✓ **Arbitration clause**: Properly drafted? (Not ousting court jurisdiction)
6. ✓ **Certainty**: All terms certain and ascertainable?
7. ✓ **No fraud**: Agreement not scheme to defraud third party?
8. ✓ **Moral standards**: Agreement not immoral?
9. ✓ **Public policy**: Agreement not against public interest?

### Drafting Tips

#### 1. Severability Clause

**Purpose**: If part of contract void, rest remains valid

**Example Clause**: "If any provision of this Agreement is held invalid or unenforceable, such provision shall be severed and the remaining provisions shall continue in full force."

**Effectiveness**: Works ONLY if provisions are truly severable (not if entire object unlawful)

#### 2. Arbitration Clause (Section 28 Exception)

**Valid Clause**: "All disputes arising out of this Agreement shall be referred to arbitration in accordance with the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996. Subject to arbitration, the courts at [City] shall have exclusive jurisdiction."

**Invalid Clause**: "Parties waive all rights to sue in any court" → VOID (ousts jurisdiction)

#### 3. Non-Compete (Restrictive Covenants)

**For Sale of Goodwill**:
- **Geographic limit**: "within 10 km radius of business"
- **Time limit**: "for period of 3 years from sale"
- **Scope limit**: "not carry on similar bakery business"

**For Employment** (Limited validity in India):
- **During employment**: Valid
- **Post-employment**: Generally VOID (Niranjan Shankar case)
- **Confidentiality/Trade Secrets**: Valid (protects proprietary info)

## Flowchart: Is Object/Consideration Lawful?

```
START
  ↓
Is object or consideration FORBIDDEN BY LAW?
(Prohibited by statute - crime, unlicensed business, prohibited goods)
  ├─ YES → Agreement VOID
  └─ NO → Proceed

  ↓
Does it DEFEAT PROVISIONS OF LAW?
(Evade stamp duty, restraint of marriage, insolvency fraud)
  ├─ YES → Agreement VOID
  └─ NO → Proceed

  ↓
Is it FRAUDULENT?
(Scheme to defraud third party, conspiracy)
  ├─ YES → Agreement VOID
  └─ NO → Proceed

  ↓
Is it INJURIOUS to person or property?
(Assault, damage property, endanger life)
  ├─ YES → Agreement VOID
  └─ NO → Proceed

  ↓
Is it IMMORAL?
(Prostitution, adultery, sale of public office)
  ├─ YES → Agreement VOID
  └─ NO → Proceed

  ↓
Is it OPPOSED TO PUBLIC POLICY?
  ├─ Restraint of trade (Section 27) → VOID (unless sale of goodwill exception)
  ├─ Oust jurisdiction of courts (Section 28) → VOID (unless arbitration exception)
  ├─ Uncertain terms (Section 29) → VOID
  ├─ Trading with enemy, corruption, champerty → VOID
  └─ NO → Object and Consideration LAWFUL ✓
```

## Practice Examples

### Example 1: Wagering Agreement

**Facts**:
- A and B bet Rs. 1 lakh on cricket match outcome
- A wins bet; B refuses to pay

**Analysis**:
- **Wager**: Bet on uncertain event (Section 30)
- **Effect**: Agreement VOID
- **Result**: A cannot recover Rs. 1 lakh (void agreement)

### Example 2: Sale of Goodwill

**Facts**:
- A sells bakery business to B for Rs. 50 lakhs
- Agreement: A will not open bakery within 5 km for 3 years
- After 6 months, A opens bakery 2 km away

**Analysis**:
- **Sale of goodwill**: Yes (bakery business sold)
- **Restraint**: 5 km, 3 years (reasonable in geography and time)
- **Exception to Section 27**: Valid (sale of goodwill exception)
- **Result**: B can seek injunction to prevent A from operating

### Example 3: Employment Non-Compete

**Facts**:
- A employed as software engineer at Company X
- Employment contract: "After leaving, cannot work for competing company for 2 years"
- A leaves; joins Company Y (competitor)

**Analysis**:
- **Post-employment non-compete**: Restraint of trade
- **Section 27**: VOID (Niranjan Shankar precedent)
- **Result**: Clause unenforceable; A can join Company Y

### Example 4: Part Consideration Unlawful

**Facts**:
- A agrees to B: "Pay Rs. 1 lakh for (1) delivering goods, and (2) bribing official"
- Single payment for two objects

**Analysis**:
- **Section 24**: Part of object unlawful (bribery)
- **Indivisible**: Single consideration for multiple objects
- **Effect**: Entire agreement VOID
- **Result**: Cannot enforce even lawful part (delivery of goods)

### Example 5: Arbitration Clause

**Facts**:
- Contract clause: "All disputes shall be resolved by arbitration; parties waive right to approach courts"

**Analysis**:
- **Arbitration**: Valid (Section 28 exception)
- **Waive right to courts**: VOID (ousts jurisdiction)
- **Severability**: Arbitration clause valid; waiver clause void
- **Result**: Arbitration valid; but arbitration award can be challenged in court

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Confusing Void with Voidable

**Mistake**: Thinking unlawful agreement is voidable (can be affirmed)

**Correct**: Unlawful agreement is VOID (no contract exists; cannot be affirmed)

### 2. Assuming All Restraints of Trade Void

**Mistake**: Thinking all non-compete clauses void

**Correct**: 
- **Sale of goodwill**: Valid if reasonable
- **Employment post-termination**: Generally void
- **During employment**: Valid

### 3. Arbitration Clause Ousting Jurisdiction

**Mistake**: Drafting clause that completely bars courts

**Correct**: Arbitration valid; but courts retain supervisory jurisdiction (can challenge award)

### 4. Recovering Under Illegal Contract

**Mistake**: Thinking can sue to enforce illegal contract or recover money paid

**Correct**: In pari delicto → Cannot recover (except if not equally guilty)

### 5. Severability of Unlawful Provisions

**Mistake**: Assuming unlawful part can always be severed

**Correct**: 
- **Section 24**: If entire object or consideration unlawful → Entire agreement void (cannot sever)
- **Severability**: Only if provisions truly independent

# Partnership Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-PARTNERSHIP
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Special Contracts - Partnership
- **Statute**: Indian Partnership Act 1932 (Sections 4-73)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1932

## Overview

This protocol covers **partnership** - a relationship between persons who agree to share profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all.

**Key Principle**: Partnership is based on mutual agency; each partner is agent of the firm and other partners for business purposes.

---

## Section 4: Partnership Defined

```
"Partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all."
```

**Essential Elements**:
1. **Association of two or more persons**: Minimum 2 partners (maximum 50 for ordinary business, 20 for banking - Companies Act 2013)
2. **Agreement**: Express or implied contract
3. **Business**: Lawful business (trade, occupation, profession)
4. **Carried on by all or any of them acting for all**: Mutual agency
5. **Sharing of profits**: Agreement to share profits (loss sharing implied)

**Persons**: Individuals, companies, other partnerships (but NOT HUF as partner)

---

## Section 5: Partnership vs. Other Relationships

**Partnership Essentials vs. Similar Relationships**:

### NOT Partnership If:

#### 1. Co-ownership (Section 5(a))

**Joint/co-ownership** ≠ Partnership

**Mere co-ownership**: No partnership (even if share profits from property)

**Example**: A and B jointly own building; share rent → NOT partnership (no business carried on)

**But**: If actively manage and rent out multiple properties as business → May be partnership

#### 2. Sharing of Gross Returns (Section 5(b))

**Sharing gross receipts** ≠ Partnership

**Must share profits** (not just revenue)

**Example**: A and B share stage performance income equally → NOT partnership (unless they run entertainment business)

#### 3. Receipt of Share of Profits (Section 6)

**Receiving profit share** does NOT alone prove partnership

**Share of profits can be**:
- Debt payment (installments calculated from profits)
- Employee remuneration (profit-linked bonus)
- Widow/child of deceased partner receiving annuity
- Lender receiving interest varying with profits
- Previous owner receiving price by installments (calculated from profits)

**None above = Partnership** (unless other elements present)

**Example**: Employee receives 10% of profits as bonus → NOT partner (employee)

---

## Types of Partners

### 1. Actual/Active Partner (Section 12(a))

**Takes active part** in business management

**Liable**: To third parties (known to be partner)

### 2. Sleeping/Dormant Partner (Section 12(b))

**Does not participate** in management (contributes capital; shares profit)

**Liable**: To third parties (even if not known) from date of joining

**Retirement**: Must give public notice (Section 32) to avoid future liability

### 3. Nominal Partner (Section 12(c))

**Lends name only** (no capital contribution; no profit share usually)

**Liable**: To third parties (holding out as partner)

**Example**: Famous person lends name to business (for prestige)

### 4. Partner by Estoppel / Holding Out (Section 28)

**Represents self as partner** OR allows such representation

**Liable**: To third parties who rely on representation (even if not actually partner)

**Section 28**: "Anyone who by words or conduct represents himself, or knowingly permits himself to be represented, to be a partner in a firm, is liable as a partner to anyone who has on the faith of any such representation given credit to the firm."

**Example**: A allows business to use name on letterhead as partner (but not actually partner) → A liable to third parties who relied

### 5. Minor Partner (Section 30)

**Minor admitted to benefits** of partnership (with all partners' consent)

**Rights**: Share profits, access accounts, sue for accounts (on leaving)

**Liabilities**: 
- **NOT personally liable** for firm debts (minor's share in firm liable)
- **On majority**: Can elect within 6 months to become full partner OR leave (Section 30(5))

**If elects to continue**: Becomes full partner (liable for all debts from date admitted as minor)

**If elects to leave**: Liable only for acts when minor (not personally liable for firm debts)

---

## Formation of Partnership

### Partnership Agreement/Deed

**Not mandatory**: Can be oral or written

**But advisable**: Written agreement (partnership deed) to avoid disputes

**Usual Contents**:
1. Name and address of firm and partners
2. Nature of business
3. Capital contribution by each partner
4. Profit/loss sharing ratio
5. Partners' duties and powers
6. Salaries/remuneration (if any)
7. Interest on capital, drawings, loans (if any)
8. Duration (if fixed-term)
9. Accounts and audit
10. Admission, retirement, expulsion of partners
11. Dissolution terms
12. Arbitration clause (for disputes)

### Registration (Section 58-60)

**Optional** (NOT mandatory)

**Registered Firm**: Firm registered with Registrar of Firms

**Advantages of Registration**:
1. **Can sue**: Firm/partner can sue third party or other partner (Section 69)
2. **Claim set-off**: Can claim set-off in suit against firm

**Unregistered Firm Disabilities** (Section 69):
- **Cannot sue**: Firm cannot sue third party for enforcement of contract
- **Partner cannot sue**: Another partner or firm for enforcement of rights

**Can be sued**: Third party can sue unregistered firm (Section 69)

**When to Register**: Anytime during existence of firm

## Mutual Rights and Duties of Partners (Sections 9-13)

### Rights of Partners

#### 1. Right to Take Part in Business (Section 12(a))

**Every partner entitled** to participate in management (unless agreed otherwise)

**Majority decision**: Ordinary business matters decided by majority (Section 13(1))

**Unanimous decision**: Required for:
- Change in nature of business
- Admission of new partner
- Expulsion of partner
- Major alterations

#### 2. Right to Share Profits (Section 13(b))

**Default**: Equal sharing (even if capital unequal)

**Can agree**: Different profit-sharing ratio

**Loss sharing**: Same ratio as profit (Section 13(b)) unless agreed otherwise

#### 3. Right to Interest on Capital (Section 13(c))

**No interest on capital** (unless agreed)

**If agreed**: Rate and terms specified in partnership deed

#### 4. Right to Interest on Advances (Section 13(d))

**Partner advances money** (beyond agreed capital) → Entitled to **6% interest** (Section 13(d))

**From date of advance** until repayment

#### 5. Right to Indemnity (Section 13(e))

**Partner entitled to indemnity** from firm for:
- Acts done in ordinary course of business
- Acts done in emergency to protect firm from loss

**Example**: Partner pays firm's debt from own pocket (in emergency) → Firm must reimburse

#### 6. Right to Access Books (Section 12(b))

**Every partner** entitled to access and inspect partnership books

**Must be kept at principal place** of business

### Duties of Partners

#### 1. Duty to Carry On Business (Section 9)

**Business of firm** carried on by all or any partners acting for all

**Mutual agency**: Each partner is agent of firm (for business purposes)

#### 2. Duty to Act in Good Faith (Section 15-16)

**Fiduciary Duty**: Partner must act in good faith towards other partners

**Section 15**: Render true accounts; full information of all matters affecting firm

**Section 16**: Duty to indemnify for willful neglect/fraud

#### 3. Duty to Disclose (Section 15)

**Duty to render true accounts** and provide full information

**Material information**: Must disclose to other partners

#### 4. Duty to Account for Personal Profits (Section 16(a))

**Section 16(a)**: "Subject to contract between the partners, every partner must account to the firm for any benefit derived by him without the consent of the other partners from any transaction of the firm or from the use of the property or business connection of the firm or the firm name."

**Secret Profit**: Must account to firm

**Example**: Partner uses firm's name to get contract for self → Must account for profit

**Case: Ajaib Singh v. Shamsher Singh (1934)**:
- Partner obtained land allotment using firm's name
- Court held: Must transfer land to firm (profit from firm connection)

#### 5. Duty to Account for Competing Business (Section 16(b))

**Section 16(b)**: "If a partner carries on any business of the same nature as and competing with that of the firm, he must account for and pay to the firm all profits made by him in that business."

**Competing business**: Without other partners' consent

**Remedy**: Other partners can claim profits

**Example**: Partner in textile firm starts own textile business (competing) → Must pay profits to firm

#### 6. Duty Not to Assign Interest Without Consent (Section 29)

**Partner cannot transfer** his partnership interest to outsider (without other partners' consent)

**Can assign**: Right to share of profits (assignee doesn't become partner; no management rights)

---

## Implied Authority of Partner (Section 19)

**Section 19**: "Subject to the provisions of section 22, the act of a partner which is done to carry on, in the usual way, business of the kind carried on by the firm, binds the firm."

**Implied Authority**: Partner can bind firm by acts in ordinary course of business

**Usual Way**: Customary in that type of business

**Examples of Implied Authority**:
- Buy goods for firm
- Sell goods of firm
- Receive payment
- Settle accounts
- Employ staff
- Draw, accept, endorse negotiable instruments (if firm deals in such)

**NOT Implied Authority** (Section 19(2)):
1. Submit dispute to arbitration
2. Open bank account in firm's name (unless expressly authorized)
3. Compromise/relinquish claim
4. Withdraw suit/proceeding
5. Admit liability in suit
6. Acquire immovable property on firm's behalf
7. Transfer immovable property of firm
8. Enter partnership on firm's behalf

**Third Party Protection**: If third party believed partner had authority (reasonable belief) → Firm bound

---

## Restrictions on Partner's Authority (Section 22)

**Section 22**: Majority of partners can restrict partner's authority (if in good faith)

**Restriction binding** only if third party had notice

**Example**: Partners restrict one partner from buying above Rs. 1 lakh; third party doesn't know → Firm bound (no notice of restriction)

---

## Liability of Partners

### 1. Joint and Several Liability (Section 25)

**Every partner jointly and severally liable** for all acts of firm done while a partner

**Joint**: All partners liable together

**Several**: Each partner individually liable for entire debt

**Creditor can sue**:
- All partners jointly, OR
- Any partner individually for full amount

**Example**: Firm owes Rs. 10 lakhs to creditor; creditor can sue any one partner for full Rs. 10 lakhs

### 2. Liability of Incoming Partner (Section 31)

**New partner admitted** → Liable for firm acts **from date of admission** (not prior debts)

**Can agree**: To be liable for prior debts (novation with creditors)

### 3. Liability of Retiring Partner (Section 32)

**Retiring partner** → Liable for firm debts **up to date of retirement**

**After retirement**: NOT liable for new debts (if proper notice given)

**Public Notice Required** (Section 32(3)):
- Advertisement in Official Gazette, AND
- Notice to persons who dealt with firm (individually)

**If no notice**: Retiring partner liable to third parties who had no notice (Section 32(3))

**Agreement with creditors**: Retiring partner can get novation (creditors agree to release)

### 4. Liability of Deceased Partner's Estate (Section 34)

**Deceased partner's estate** → Liable for firm debts up to death

**NOT liable** for subsequent debts

### 5. Liability for Wrongful Acts (Section 26)

**Firm liable** for partner's wrongful act/omission (if in ordinary course of business)

**All partners liable** (joint and several)

**Example**: Partner commits fraud while conducting firm business → Firm and all partners liable

**Case: Hamlyn v. Houston & Co. (1903)**:
- Partner bribed clerk to get competitor's information
- Court held: Firm liable (act in course of business)

### 6. Liability for Misapplication (Section 27)

**Firm liable** if:
- Partner receives money/property from third party
- Partner misapplies it (while acting within apparent authority)

**Example**: Partner receives client's money for investment; misappropriates → Firm liable

---

## Partnership Property (Section 14)

**Partnership Property**: Property brought in or acquired for firm purposes

**Section 14**: "Subject to contract between the partners, the property of the firm includes all property and rights and interests in property originally brought into the stock of the firm, or acquired, by purchase or otherwise, by or for the firm, or for the purposes and in the course of the business of the firm."

**Includes**:
- Capital contributions
- Property purchased with firm money
- Goodwill of business
- Property held/used for firm business

**NOT Partnership Property**:
- Partner's personal property (even if used by firm under license)

**Co-ownership**: Partners are co-owners of partnership property (cannot transfer individual share without consent)

**On Dissolution**: Partnership property applied first to pay firm debts; surplus distributed to partners

---

## Dissolution of Partnership (Sections 39-55)

**Dissolution Types**:

### 1. Dissolution of Partnership (Between Partners)

**One or more partners leave/join** → Partnership changes (but firm may continue)

**Example**: 3-partner firm; 1 retires → Partnership dissolved (but 2 remaining partners can continue firm)

### 2. Dissolution of Firm

**Firm ceases to exist**; business wound up

**All partners leave** OR firm business ends

### Modes of Dissolution

#### A. Dissolution by Agreement (Section 40)

**Partners agree** to dissolve (any time)

**Partnership deed**: May specify dissolution events

#### B. Compulsory Dissolution (Section 41)

**Automatic dissolution** if:
1. **All but one partner insolvent**, OR
2. **Business becomes illegal** (all partners or carrying on business becomes unlawful)

**Example**: Partnership in gambling (gambling becomes illegal) → Firm dissolved

#### C. Dissolution on Contingency (Section 42)

**If partnership deed provides**, firm dissolves on:
- Death of partner
- Insolvency of partner
- Happening of specified event

**Example**: Deed says "firm dissolves on X's death" → X dies → Firm dissolved

**If deed silent**: Death/insolvency does NOT automatically dissolve (continuing partners can continue)

#### D. Dissolution by Notice (Section 43)

**Partnership at will**: Any partner can dissolve by giving notice

**Notice to all other partners**

**Effective**: From date in notice OR date notice communicated (if no date)

**Example**: 3 partners; no fixed term; A gives notice → Partnership dissolves from date in notice

#### E. Dissolution by Court (Section 44)

**Court can dissolve** on suit by partner if:

1. **Partner of unsound mind** (or permanently incapable)
2. **Partner guilty of misconduct** (prejudicial to business)
3. **Partner persistently breaches** agreement
4. **Partner transfers interest** (whole/part) to third party
5. **Business carried on at loss** (cannot be carried on except at loss)
6. **Court satisfied just and equitable** to dissolve

**Case: Ananth Pai v. Shanker Shenoy (1986)**:
- Loss of confidence between partners
- Court held: Just and equitable to dissolve

**Example: Misconduct**: Partner misappropriates firm funds → Court can dissolve

---

## Consequences of Dissolution (Sections 46-55)

### 1. Settlement of Accounts (Section 46-55)

**On dissolution**, accounts settled:

**Priority Order** (Section 48):
1. **Outside creditors** (third parties)
2. **Partner's advances** (beyond capital)
3. **Partner's capital**
4. **Surplus** (if any) → Distributed in profit-sharing ratio

**Losses**: Applied in order:
1. From **profits** (if any)
2. From **capital**
3. From **partners personally** (in profit-sharing ratio)

**Example**:
- Assets: Rs. 100 lakhs
- Outside debts: Rs. 60 lakhs
- Partner A's advance: Rs. 10 lakhs
- Capital (A: Rs. 15 lakhs, B: Rs. 15 lakhs)
- **Settlement**:
  - Pay creditors Rs. 60 lakhs
  - Repay A's advance Rs. 10 lakhs
  - Remaining Rs. 30 lakhs: Return capital (A Rs. 15 lakhs, B Rs. 15 lakhs)
  - No surplus

### 2. Authority of Partners After Dissolution (Section 46)

**Continuing authority**: Only for winding up (not new business)

**Partners can**:
- Complete pending transactions
- Realize assets
- Pay debts
- Distribute surplus

**Cannot**: Start new transactions

### 3. Rights of Partners After Dissolution (Section 46-47)

**Each partner or representative** can:
- Apply partnership property to pay debts/liabilities
- Sell goodwill (unless agreed otherwise)
- Require accounts from date of dissolution

**Section 47**: After dissolution, goodwill sold (unless agreed otherwise)

### 4. Return of Premium on Premature Dissolution (Section 51)

**Premium paid** by partner on joining (for fixed term)

**If dissolved prematurely** (not partner's fault) → Partner entitled to **refund of premium** (or proportionate part)

**Exceptions**: If dissolved due to:
- Partner's death
- Partner's misconduct
- Agreement excluding premium return

### 5. Private Partnership Property (Section 53)

**On partner's insolvency**: Partner's separate property applied:
1. First to **separate debts** (personal creditors)
2. Then to **firm debts**

**Firm property** applied:
1. First to **firm debts**
2. Then to **partner's separate debts** (if any claim)

---

## Limited Liability Partnership (LLP Act 2008)

**LLP**: Hybrid (partnership flexibility + company's limited liability)

**Features**:
1. **Separate legal entity** (like company)
2. **Limited liability**: Partners not personally liable (except own wrongful acts)
3. **No minimum capital**
4. **Flexible management** (like partnership)
5. **Perpetual succession** (doesn't dissolve on partner's death)

**Registration**: Mandatory (with Registrar of Companies)

**Governed by**: Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 (NOT Partnership Act 1932)

---

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Partner by Holding Out

**Facts**:
- A, B, C are partners
- D (not partner) allows name on firm's letterhead as partner
- E (third party) gives credit to firm (relying on D's name)
- Firm defaults

**Analysis**:
- **Section 28**: D represented self as partner (holding out)
- **E's reliance**: E gave credit on faith of D's representation
- **Result**: D liable to E as partner (even though not actually partner)

### Example 2: Competing Business

**Facts**:
- A and B are partners in software firm
- A starts own software company (competing)
- A makes Rs. 10 lakhs profit from own company
- B discovers; demands share

**Analysis**:
- **Section 16(b)**: Partner cannot carry on competing business without consent
- **Duty to account**: A must pay Rs. 10 lakhs to firm
- **Result**: B can claim Rs. 10 lakhs profit (or proportionate share per partnership ratio)

### Example 3: Minor Electing on Majority

**Facts**:
- X (minor) admitted to benefits of partnership at age 16
- On attaining 18, X continues for 8 months (doesn't elect)
- X then wants to leave; claims not liable for firm debts

**Analysis**:
- **Section 30(5)**: Minor must elect within 6 months of majority
- **8 months passed**: No election within 6 months
- **Deemed election**: Deemed to have elected to continue (become full partner)
- **Result**: X is full partner; liable for all firm debts (including during minority period)

### Example 4: Retiring Partner Liability

**Facts**:
- A, B, C are partners
- A retires on June 1
- A gives notice in Gazette but not to individual customers
- On July 1, firm (B, C) borrows Rs. 5 lakhs from D (old customer)
- D had no notice of A's retirement
- Firm defaults

**Analysis**:
- **Section 32(3)**: Retiring partner liable unless notice given
- **A gave Gazette notice**: Yes (but not enough for old customers)
- **D (old customer)**: No individual notice
- **Result**: A liable to D (no proper notice; D didn't know A retired)

### Example 5: Dissolution Settlement

**Facts**:
- Partnership dissolved
- Assets: Rs. 80 lakhs
- Liabilities: Outside debts Rs. 50 lakhs
- Partner A's advance: Rs. 15 lakhs (beyond capital)
- Capital: A Rs. 10 lakhs, B Rs. 10 lakhs
- Profit ratio: A:B = 60:40

**Analysis (Section 48)**:
1. **Pay outside creditors**: Rs. 50 lakhs
2. **Remaining**: Rs. 30 lakhs
3. **Repay A's advance**: Rs. 15 lakhs
4. **Remaining**: Rs. 15 lakhs
5. **Return capital**: A Rs. 10 lakhs, B Rs. 10 lakhs = Rs. 20 lakhs
6. **Shortfall**: Rs. 5 lakhs (not enough for full capital)
7. **Loss of Rs. 5 lakhs** shared in profit ratio (60:40):
   - A loses Rs. 3 lakhs (60%)
   - B loses Rs. 2 lakhs (40%)
8. **Final distribution**:
   - A gets: Rs. 15 lakhs (advance) + Rs. 7 lakhs (Rs. 10L capital - Rs. 3L loss) = Rs. 22 lakhs
   - B gets: Rs. 8 lakhs (Rs. 10L capital - Rs. 2L loss)

---

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Assuming Profit Sharing = Partnership

**Mistake**: Thinking anyone receiving profit share is partner

**Correct**: Profit sharing alone doesn't prove partnership (Section 6); must have all essential elements (agreement, business, mutual agency)

### 2. Not Giving Notice on Retirement

**Mistake**: Retiring partner thinks retirement ends liability (without notice)

**Correct**: Must give public notice (Gazette + individual to known parties) to avoid future liability (Section 32)

### 3. Expelling Partner Without Deed Provision

**Mistake**: Majority partners expel one partner (no provision in deed)

**Correct**: Cannot expel unless partnership deed expressly permits (must be in good faith + bona fide)

### 4. Minor Thinking Can Become Partner

**Mistake**: Admitting minor as full partner

**Correct**: Minor can only be admitted to benefits (not full partner - Section 30); no personal liability

### 5. Unregistered Firm Trying to Sue

**Mistake**: Unregistered firm sues third party for contract enforcement

**Correct**: Unregistered firm cannot sue (Section 69); must register first (though can be sued by third parties)

# Contract Performance Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-PERFORMANCE
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Contract Performance
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 37-50)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **performance of contracts** - who must perform, when, where, and how contracts must be performed under Indian law.

**Key Principle**: Parties must perform their contractual obligations exactly as agreed, unless excused by law.

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 37: Obligation of Parties

```
"The parties to a contract must either perform, or offer to perform, their respective promises, unless such performance is dispensed with or excused under the provisions of this Act, or of any other law."
```

**General Rule**: **Promises must be performed**

**Exceptions** (Performance dispensed with or excused):
- Mutual agreement to rescind
- Impossibility (Section 56)
- Breach by other party
- Waiver
- Novation, remission, or alteration

---

## Who Must Perform? (Sections 40-41)

### Section 40: Person by Whom Promise is to be Performed

```
"If it appears from the nature of the case that it was the intention of the parties to any contract that any promise contained in it should be performed by the promisor himself, such promise must be performed by the promisor. In other cases, the promisor or his representatives may employ a competent person to perform it."
```

**General Rule**: Promisor OR promisor's agent can perform

**Exception**: If contract requires **personal performance** → Only promisor must perform

#### Personal Performance Required

**When is personal performance essential?**

1. **Contracts involving personal skill/taste**:
   - Portrait painting by famous artist
   - Performance by specific musician
   - Legal services by specific lawyer

**Example**: Contract for Lata Mangeshkar to sing at wedding → Only Lata must perform (cannot send substitute)

2. **Contracts based on personal confidence**:
   - Doctor-patient (patient chose specific doctor)
   - Lawyer-client relationship

**Case: British Waggon Co. v. Lea & Co. (1880)**:
- Contract to hire wagons from specific company (reputable)
- Company assigned contract to another company (not reputable)
- Court held: Personal performance required (contract based on confidence in specific company)

#### Performance by Agent/Representative Allowed

**When can agent perform?**

**General contracts** where personality doesn't matter:
- Sale of goods
- Delivery of commodities
- Payment of money
- Construction work (not requiring specific personal skill)

**Example**: Contract to deliver 100 tons of wheat → Seller can use agent to deliver

### Section 41: Effect of Refusal to Accept Offer of Performance

```
"When a promisor has made an offer of performance to the promisee, and the offer has not been accepted, the promisor is not responsible for non-performance, nor does he thereby lose his rights under the contract."
```

**Tender of Performance**: Offer to perform (even if not accepted) = Discharge of obligation

**Effect**:
1. Promisor not liable for non-performance (if promisee refuses tender)
2. Promisor retains rights under contract (can sue for breach by promisee)

**Illustration (Section 41)**:
- A contracts to deliver goods to B on Jan 1
- A tenders goods on Jan 1
- B refuses to accept
- A not liable for non-performance; A can sue B for breach

**Valid Tender Requirements**:
1. **Unconditional**: No strings attached
2. **At proper time and place**: As per contract
3. **Of proper amount/quantity**: Exact as agreed
4. **To proper person**: Promisee or authorized agent

**Tender of Money**:
- Must be in legal tender (currency notes, coins)
- Exact amount (creditor not bound to give change)

**Case: Dixon v. Clark (1848)**:
- Debtor tendered payment in bank notes (legal tender at that time)
- Creditor refused
- Court held: Valid tender; debtor discharged

---

## When Must Contract be Performed? (Sections 46-48)

### Section 46: Time for Performance (When No Time Specified)

```
"When a promise is to be performed on a certain day, and the promisor has not performed it at the appointed time, the promisor may perform it at any time during the usual hours of business on such day, unless it appears from the nature of the contract that performance at any particular time was intended."
```

**Rules**:

1. **If specific time mentioned**: Perform at that time
   - Example: "Deliver goods at 10 AM on Jan 5" → Must deliver at 10 AM

2. **If specific day (no time) mentioned**: Perform during usual business hours
   - Example: "Deliver goods on Jan 5" → Can deliver anytime between 9 AM - 6 PM (usual hours)

3. **If no time specified**: Within reasonable time
   - **Section 46**: "If no time is specified, the promisor must perform the promise within a reasonable time."
   - **Reasonable time**: Depends on nature of contract, trade custom, circumstances

**Case: Pantankar v. State of Maharashtra (1975)**:
- Contract to supply goods; no time specified
- Court held: Must perform within reasonable time (determined by nature of goods - perishable vs non-perishable)

### Section 47: Time is of the Essence

```
"When a contract specifies that time is of the essence, failure to perform on time is a breach of condition, and the aggrieved party can terminate the contract."
```

**Time of the Essence**: Punctuality is critical; delay = breach

**When is time of the essence?**

1. **Expressly stated**: "Time is of the essence"
2. **Implied from nature of contract**:
   - Perishable goods (flowers for wedding, food)
   - Market fluctuation goods (shares, commodities)
   - Seasonal goods (Christmas decorations in December)
   - Event-specific (goods for specific event - wedding, conference)

**Effect if Time of Essence**:
- Delay in performance = **Breach of condition**
- Aggrieved party can **terminate contract**

**Effect if Time NOT of Essence**:
- Delay in performance = **Breach of warranty** (minor breach)
- Aggrieved party cannot terminate; can only claim damages

**Case: Henthorn v. Fraser (1892)**:
- Sale of land; time NOT of essence (land not perishable)
- Buyer delayed payment
- Court held: Seller cannot terminate; can only sue for damages

### Section 48: Place of Performance

```
"Where a contract requires performance at a specified place, it must be performed at that place. If no place is specified, the promisor must apply to the promisee to appoint a place."
```

**Rules**:

1. **If place specified**: Perform at that place
   - Example: "Deliver goods to warehouse at [address]" → Must deliver there

2. **If no place specified**: Promisee must designate place
   - Promisor requests promisee to specify
   - If promisee doesn't specify → Promisor's own place (promisor's residence/business)

---

## How Must Contract be Performed? (Sections 42-45, 49-50)

### Section 42: Devolution of Joint Liabilities

```
"When two or more persons have made a joint promise, then, unless a contrary intention appears from the contract, all such persons, during their joint lives, must fulfill the promise jointly."
```

**Joint Promise**: Multiple promisors

**Rule**: All must perform jointly (during their lives)

**Death of Joint Promisor**:
- **Section 42**: Deceased's estate liable (legal representatives must perform)
- Other surviving promisors continue to be liable

**Illustration**:
- A, B, C jointly promise to pay Rs. 30,000 to X
- A dies
- B, C must pay (A's estate also liable for 1/3 share)

**Joint and Several Liability**:
- If contract states "joint and several" → Each promisor liable for entire performance
- Promisee can sue any one or all promisors

### Section 43: Joint Promisees - Any One Can Demand Performance

```
"When a promise is made to two or more persons jointly, then, unless a contrary intention appears from the contract, the right to claim performance rests, as between the promisees or their representatives, with them all jointly during their joint lives, and, after the death of any one of them, with the representative of such deceased person jointly with the surviving promisees."
```

**Joint Promisees**: Multiple persons to whom promise made

**Rule**: All must demand jointly (during joint lives)

**Death of Joint Promisee**: Right passes to legal representatives + surviving promisees (jointly)

### Section 49: Manner of Performance When Party Undertakes More Than He Promised

```
"When a party to a contract performs, or offers to perform, his part of it in a manner not sanctioned by the contract, the other party may accept such performance, or he may insist upon the performance of the promise in the manner prescribed by the contract."
```

**Excess Performance**: Performing more than agreed

**Promisee's Options**:
1. **Accept excess performance** (waive contract terms)
2. **Reject excess** (insist on exact performance)

**Example**: Contract to deliver 100 kg wheat; promisor delivers 150 kg
- Promisee can accept 150 kg OR reject excess and demand exactly 100 kg

### Section 50: Performance in Instalments

```
"Where a party to a contract promises to perform his obligations in instalments, the promisee may refuse to accept partial performance unless the contract specifically allows it."
```

**General Rule**: **Entire performance required** (not partial)

**Exception**: If contract allows instalments → Partial performance accepted

**Example**:
- Contract to sell 1000 units
- If contract silent → Seller must deliver all 1000 units at once
- If contract states "delivery in 10 instalments of 100 units" → Buyer must accept instalments

**Illustration**: A contracts to sell and deliver 100 bales of goods to B
- A tenders 50 bales
- B can refuse (entitled to entire 100 bales unless contract allows partial delivery)

---

## Reciprocal Promises (Sections 51-58)

### Definition (Section 2(f))

```
"Promises which form the consideration or part of the consideration for each other are called reciprocal promises."
```

**Reciprocal Promises**: Mutual promises forming consideration for each other

**Example**: Sale contract
- Seller promises to deliver goods
- Buyer promises to pay price
- Both promises are reciprocal (consideration for each other)

### Types of Reciprocal Promises

#### 1. Mutual and Independent Promises (Section 52)

**Section 52**: Promises independent of each other; failure of one does not excuse the other

**Example**: A promises to sell land to B on Jan 1; B promises to pay on Jan 15
- Promises independent
- A must transfer land even if B hasn't paid yet (B's payment date is later)

#### 2. Mutual and Dependent / Conditional Promises (Section 51)

**Section 51**: Performance of one promise depends on prior performance of other

**Order of Performance**:
1. **Promisor must perform first** (then promisee performs)
2. **Simultaneous performance** (both at same time)

**Example**: Cash sale
- Seller delivers goods; buyer pays
- **Simultaneous** (Section 51(1))

**Example**: Land sale with deed execution
- Seller must execute deed first; then buyer pays
- **Conditional** (Section 51(2))

#### 3. Mutual Promises - One Impossible (Section 56)

If one reciprocal promise is impossible → Entire contract void

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-IMPOSSIBILITY protocol

### Section 53: Liability of Party Preventing Performance

```
"When a contract contains reciprocal promises, and one party to the contract prevents the other from performing his promise, the contract becomes voidable at the option of the party so prevented; and he is entitled to compensation from the other party for any loss which he may sustain in consequence of the non-performance of the contract."
```

**Prevention of Performance**: One party hinders other's performance

**Effect**:
1. Contract **voidable** at option of prevented party
2. **Damages** for loss due to non-performance

**Example**: Employer wrongfully dismisses employee before end of contract period
- Employee prevented from performing
- Employee can claim wages for remaining period + damages

**Case: Planche v. Colburn (1831)**:
- Publisher contracted with author to write book
- Publisher abandoned publication (prevented author from completing)
- Court held: Author can recover for work done + damages

---

## Appropriation of Payments (Sections 59-61)

### Section 59: Application of Payment Where Debt to be Discharged is Indicated

```
"Where a debtor owing several distinct debts to one person, makes a payment to him either with express intimation or under circumstances implying that the payment is to be applied to the discharge of some particular debt, the payment, if accepted, must be applied accordingly."
```

**Debtor's Right**: If debtor specifies which debt to pay → Must be applied accordingly

**Example**: A owes B three debts: Rs. 1,000 (due Jan 1), Rs. 2,000 (due Feb 1), Rs. 3,000 (due Mar 1)
- A pays Rs. 1,000 stating "for Jan 1 debt"
- B must apply payment to Jan 1 debt (cannot apply to other debts)

### Section 60: Application of Payment Where Debt Not Indicated (Creditor's Right)

```
"Where the debtor has omitted to intimate, and there are no circumstances implying to which debt the payment is to be applied, the creditor may apply it at his discretion to any lawful debt actually due and payable to him from the debtor."
```

**Creditor's Right**: If debtor doesn't specify → Creditor can apply to any debt

**Limitations**:
- **Lawful debt** (not time-barred or illegal)
- **Actually due and payable** (not future debt)

### Section 61: Application of Payment Where Neither Party Appropriates (Clayton's Rule)

```
"Where neither party makes any appropriation, the payment shall be applied in discharge of the debts in order of time, whether they are or are not barred by the law of limitation."
```

**Clayton's Rule**: Payment applied to oldest debt first (FIFO - First In, First Out)

**Example**: A owes B three debts: Rs. 1,000 (incurred 2020), Rs. 2,000 (2021), Rs. 3,000 (2022)
- A pays Rs. 1,500 without specifying
- B doesn't specify
- Applied: Rs. 1,000 to 2020 debt (fully paid); Rs. 500 to 2021 debt

**Time-Barred Debts**: Clayton's Rule applies even if debt time-barred

---

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Personal Performance

**Facts**: A hires famous chef B to cook for wedding; B sends assistant

**Analysis**:
- **Personal skill**: Yes (famous chef)
- **Section 40**: Personal performance required
- **Effect**: A can reject; B in breach

### Example 2: Tender of Performance

**Facts**: Seller tenders delivery of goods on due date; buyer refuses to accept

**Analysis**:
- **Section 41**: Valid tender
- **Effect**: Seller not liable for non-performance; seller can sue buyer for breach

### Example 3: Time of Essence

**Facts**: Contract to deliver flowers for wedding on Dec 25; seller delivers Dec 27

**Analysis**:
- **Time of essence**: Yes (event-specific - flowers for wedding)
- **Delay**: Breach of condition
- **Effect**: Buyer can terminate contract + damages

### Example 4: Joint Promise

**Facts**: A, B, C jointly promise to pay Rs. 30,000 to X; A defaults

**Analysis**:
- **Section 42**: Joint liability
- **Effect**: X can sue A, B, C jointly; or sue any one/all

### Example 5: Reciprocal Promises

**Facts**: A promises to deliver goods to B; B promises to pay on delivery; A doesn't deliver

**Analysis**:
- **Reciprocal promises**: Conditional (Section 51)
- **A's non-performance**: B not obliged to pay (condition precedent not met)
- **Effect**: B can sue A for breach

# Contract Rescission Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-RESCISSION
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Remedies - Rescission
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 19, 19A, 39, 64-67), Specific Relief Act 1963 (Section 27)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **rescission** (cancellation) of contracts - the equitable remedy of terminating a contract and restoring parties to their pre-contract position when contract is voidable due to vitiated consent, misrepresentation, breach, or other grounds.

**Key Principle**: Rescission aims to undo contract and restore status quo ante (position before contract).

---

## Nature of Rescission

**Rescission**: Cancellation of contract + Restoration to original position

**Effect**:
- Contract treated as if never existed
- Parties restored to pre-contract position (restitutio in integrum)
- No further obligations

**Equitable Remedy**: Court's discretion (not absolute right)

---

## Grounds for Rescission

### 1. Voidable Contracts (Sections 19, 19A)

**Vitiated Consent**: Contract voidable at option of aggrieved party

#### A. Coercion (Section 15)

**Section 19**: "When consent to an agreement is caused by coercion, fraud or misrepresentation, the agreement is a contract voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused."

**Example**: Threat of violence to sign contract → Victim can rescind

#### B. Undue Influence (Section 16)

**Section 19A**: "When consent to an agreement is caused by undue influence, the agreement is a contract voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused..."

**Example**: Doctor persuades patient to gift property → Patient can rescind

#### C. Fraud (Section 17)

**Fraud**: Intentional false representation inducing contract

**Example**: Seller falsely states car never had accident → Buyer can rescind + damages

#### D. Misrepresentation (Section 18)

**Innocent Misrepresentation**: False statement without intent to deceive

**Example**: Seller honestly but mistakenly states property boundary → Buyer can rescind (no damages)

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-FREE-CONSENT protocol

### 2. Breach of Contract (Section 39, 75)

**Section 39**: "When a party to a contract has refused to perform, or disabled himself from performing, his promise in its entirety, the promisee may put an end to the contract..."

**Material Breach**: Breach going to root of contract

**Repudiation**: Party refuses to perform (anticipatory or actual breach)

**Effect**: Innocent party can rescind + damages

**Example**: Seller repudiates sale before delivery date → Buyer can rescind + damages

### 3. Mutual Mistake (Section 20)

**Bilateral Mistake**: Both parties mistaken about essential fact

**Effect**: Contract VOID (not voidable; automatically rescinded)

**Example**: Both believe painting is original; it's fake → Contract void

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-FREE-CONSENT protocol

### 4. Failure of Consideration

**Total Failure**: Consideration promised but not provided

**Example**: A pays advance for goods; B doesn't deliver → A can rescind + recover advance

### 5. Impossibility / Frustration (Section 56)

**Supervening Impossibility**: Performance becomes impossible after contract

**Effect**: Contract VOID (automatic rescission)

**Example**: Subject matter destroyed → Contract void

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-IMPOSSIBILITY protocol

---

## Rescission ab initio vs Rescission for Breach

### Rescission ab initio (From Beginning)

**Ground**: Defect in formation (fraud, misrepresentation, coercion, undue influence)

**Effect**: Contract treated as never existed

**Restitution**: Section 64 - Restore benefits received

### Rescission for Breach

**Ground**: Breach during performance

**Effect**: Contract terminated from breach date (not from beginning)

**Damages**: Innocent party can claim damages for breach + rescind

**Section 75**: "A person who rightfully rescinds a contract is entitled to compensation for any damage which he has sustained through the non-fulfillment of the contract."

---

## Effects of Rescission (Section 64)

### Section 64: Consequences of Rescission

```
"When a person at whose option a contract is voidable rescinds it, the other party thereto need not perform any promise therein contained in which he is promisor. The party rescinding a voidable contract shall, if he has received any benefit thereunder from another party to such contract, restore such benefit, so far as may be, to the person from whom it was received."
```

**Mutual Restoration**:
1. **Rescinding party**: Must return benefit received
2. **Other party**: Released from obligations; must return benefit

**"So far as may be"**: Exact restoration if possible; if not, compensate

**Example**: 
- Buyer paid Rs. 10 lakhs; received car
- Buyer rescinds (fraud)
- Buyer returns car; Seller returns Rs. 10 lakhs

---

## Loss of Right to Rescind

**When can aggrieved party NOT rescind?**

### 1. Affirmation (Express or Implied)

**Affirmation**: Party with right to rescind chooses to continue contract

**Express Affirmation**: Clearly states will continue with contract

**Implied Affirmation**: Conduct indicating acceptance

**Example**: Buyer discovers fraud; continues using goods for 6 months → Implied affirmation (lost right to rescind)

**Case: Long v. Lloyd (1958)**:
- Buyer purchased lorry based on misrepresentation
- Buyer used lorry, attempted repairs
- Court held: Implied affirmation; cannot rescind

### 2. Lapse of Time

**Unreasonable Delay**: Aggrieved party delays rescission unreasonably

**Reasonable Time**: Depends on circumstances

**Fraud**: Time runs from **discovery** of fraud (not contract date)

**Misrepresentation**: Time generally runs from contract date

**Example**: Misrepresentation discovered 5 years after contract → Too late to rescind (laches)

**Case: Leaf v. International Galleries (1950)**:
- Painting misrepresented as by famous artist
- Buyer discovered mistake 5 years later
- Court held: Too late to rescind (lapse of time)

### 3. Third Party Rights

**Bona Fide Purchaser**: Innocent third party acquires rights

**Rule**: If third party acquired rights in good faith for value → Cannot rescind against third party

**Example**:
- A sells car to B (induced by fraud)
- B sells car to C (innocent buyer)
- A discovers fraud
- A cannot rescind and recover car from C (C protected)
- **Remedy**: A can sue B for damages (tort of deceit)

**Exception**: If third party had notice of fraud → Not protected

### 4. Impossibility of Restitution

**Cannot Restore**: If benefit cannot be returned → May lose right to rescind

**Example**: Aggrieved party consumed goods → Cannot return

**But**: Court may allow rescission with compensation for consumed goods (Section 64: "so far as may be")

**Substantial Alteration**: If subject matter substantially altered → Restitution impossible

**Example**: Buyer bought cloth, made suits → Cannot return cloth as is (substantial alteration)

---

## Rescission Procedure

### 1. Extra-Judicial Rescission

**Without Court**: Party can rescind by notice to other party

**Requirements**:
1. **Clear communication** of rescission
2. **Return** or offer to return benefit received
3. **Immediate** (no delay)

**Advantage**: Quick; no court involvement

**Risk**: If wrongfully rescinded → Liable for breach

### 2. Judicial Rescission (Section 27 Specific Relief Act)

**Through Court**: File suit for rescission

**Specific Relief Act, Section 27**: Court can rescind contract

**When Necessary**:
- Dispute about right to rescind
- Restitution complex
- Other party denies rescission

**Procedure**:
1. File suit for rescission
2. Plead grounds (fraud, misrepresentation, breach)
3. Offer to restore benefit
4. Court orders rescission + restitution

---

## Rescission vs Other Remedies

### Rescission vs Damages

| Aspect | Rescission | Damages |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Nature** | Equitable (cancel contract) | Legal (monetary compensation) |
| **Effect** | Contract undone; restitution | Contract continues; money paid |
| **When** | Voidable contracts, material breach | Any breach |
| **Plus** | Can claim damages too (fraud, breach) | Cannot rescind |

**Combined**: Rescission + Damages available (fraud, breach)

### Rescission vs Specific Performance

**Opposite Remedies**:
- **Specific Performance**: Compel performance (enforce contract)
- **Rescission**: Cancel contract (avoid performance)

**Cannot Seek Both**: Choose one (but can plead in alternative)

### Rescission vs Termination

**Rescission** (Voidable contracts):
- Undo from beginning (ab initio)
- Restore status quo ante
- Equitable remedy

**Termination** (Breach):
- End contract from breach date (not retroactive)
- No performance from breach onwards
- Damages for breach

---

## Partial Rescission

**General Rule**: Rescission is total (entire contract cancelled)

**Exception (Section 19A)**: Undue influence cases

**Section 19A**: Court may:
- Set aside contract entirely, OR
- Require return of benefit, OR
- **Set aside only part** (retain rest)

**Example**: Sale of property under undue influence at Rs. 10 lakhs (worth Rs. 50 lakhs)
- Court may: (1) Rescind entirely, OR (2) Uphold sale but increase price to Rs. 50 lakhs

---

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Rescission for Fraud

**Facts**:
- A sells car to B for Rs. 5 lakhs
- A falsely states "never in accident" (major accident occurred)
- B discovers fraud after 2 months

**Analysis**:
- **Fraud**: Intentional false representation (Section 17)
- **Voidable**: Section 19
- **B's options**: (1) Rescind + return car + recover Rs. 5 lakhs + damages (tort of deceit), OR (2) Affirm + claim damages only
- **Result**: B can rescind (discovered recently; no affirmation)

### Example 2: Loss of Right (Third Party)

**Facts**:
- A sells car to B (fraud)
- B sells car to C (innocent buyer for value)
- A discovers fraud; seeks to rescind and recover car from C

**Analysis**:
- **Third party rights**: C is bona fide purchaser
- **Protection**: C protected (acquired in good faith for value)
- **Result**: A cannot rescind against C; can only sue B for damages

### Example 3: Affirmation (Implied)

**Facts**:
- A buys property from B based on misrepresentation
- A discovers misrepresentation after 6 months
- A continues living in property for 2 more years
- A then seeks rescission

**Analysis**:
- **Lapse of time**: 2 years after discovery
- **Implied affirmation**: Continued use
- **Result**: Right to rescind LOST (affirmation by conduct + lapse)

### Example 4: Rescission for Breach

**Facts**:
- A (seller) contracts to deliver goods to B on June 1
- On May 15, A repudiates (says won't deliver)
- B rescinds immediately

**Analysis**:
- **Anticipatory breach**: Section 39
- **Material breach**: Total refusal (repudiation)
- **B's rights**: Rescind + damages
- **Result**: Contract rescinded; B can sue for damages (buy from market; claim difference + consequential loss)

### Example 5: Impossibility of Restitution

**Facts**:
- A buys cloth from B (100 meters) based on misrepresentation
- A makes suits from cloth (all consumed)
- A discovers misrepresentation; seeks rescission

**Analysis**:
- **Restitution impossible**: Cloth consumed/substantially altered
- **General rule**: Cannot rescind if cannot restore
- **Section 64**: "So far as may be"
- **Possible outcome**: Court may allow rescission with compensation for cloth value (not return of cloth itself)

---

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Delay in Rescinding

**Mistake**: Discovering fraud/misrepresentation but delaying rescission for years

**Correct**: Rescind promptly after discovery; unreasonable delay = loss of right (laches)

### 2. Affirming After Knowledge

**Mistake**: Continuing to use benefit after discovering fraud, then trying to rescind

**Correct**: Rescind immediately upon discovery; continued use = implied affirmation

### 3. Not Offering Restitution

**Mistake**: Seeking rescission without offering to return benefit

**Correct**: Must restore benefit (or offer to restore) when rescinding (Section 64)

### 4. Forgetting Third Party Rights

**Mistake**: Assuming can always rescind and recover property

**Correct**: If bona fide third party acquired rights → Cannot rescind against them

### 5. Confusing Rescission with Termination

**Mistake**: Using terms interchangeably

**Correct**:
- **Rescission**: Undo from beginning (voidable contracts)
- **Termination**: End from breach date (breach cases)

# Sale of Goods Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-SALE-OF-GOODS
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Special Contracts - Sale of Goods
- **Statute**: Sale of Goods Act 1930 (Sections 1-66)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1930

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **sale of goods** contracts under the Sale of Goods Act 1930 - a specialized category of contracts involving transfer of ownership of movable property for a price.

**Key Principle**: Sale of goods is a special contract governed by both the Sale of Goods Act 1930 (specific provisions) and the Indian Contract Act 1872 (general contract law principles).

---

## Nature and Definition

### Section 4: Contract of Sale

```
"(1) A contract of sale of goods is a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price.

(2) There may be a contract of sale between one part-owner and another.

(3) A contract of sale may be absolute or conditional."
```

**Essential Elements**:
1. **At least two parties**: Buyer and seller
2. **Goods**: Movable property
3. **Transfer of ownership**: Property (ownership) must pass
4. **For a price**: Monetary consideration (not barter)
5. **Contract**: Agreement enforceable by law

---

## Contract of Sale vs Sale (Section 4)

**Two Types**:

### 1. Sale (Executed Contract)

**Property passes immediately** (at time of contract)

**Effect**: 
- Ownership transferred to buyer
- Risk passes to buyer
- Seller becomes creditor for price (not owner)

**Example**: A buys book from shop; pays cash; takes book → Sale

### 2. Agreement to Sell (Executory Contract)

**Property to pass in future** (on fulfillment of condition or at future date)

**Effect**:
- Ownership remains with seller
- Risk remains with seller
- Seller retains proprietary rights

**Example**: A contracts to buy car; delivery next month → Agreement to sell

**Section 4(4)**: "An agreement to sell becomes a sale when the time elapses or the conditions are fulfilled subject to which the property in the goods is to be transferred."

---

## Goods (Section 2(7))

**Definition**: "Every kind of movable property other than actionable claims and money"

### Classification of Goods

#### 1. Existing Goods (Section 6)

**Owned or possessed** by seller at time of contract

**Types**:

**a) Specific Goods (Section 2(14))**:
- Identified and agreed upon at contract
- **Example**: "This Honda City car with registration DL-1234"

**b) Ascertained Goods**:
- Initially unascertained; later identified
- **Example**: "100 bags of wheat" (from seller's godown) → Specific 100 bags separated

**c) Unascertained Goods**:
- Not identified at contract
- **Example**: "100 quintals of wheat" (not separated from bulk)

#### 2. Future Goods (Section 2(6))

**To be manufactured or acquired** by seller after contract

**Example**: Contract to sell car to be manufactured next month

**Section 6(2)**: Agreement to sell future goods → "Agreement to sell" (becomes sale when goods acquired/manufactured)

#### 3. Contingent Goods

**Subject to contingency** (may or may not come into existence)

**Example**: Next season's crop (may fail)

---

## Price (Section 2(10))

**Definition**: "Money consideration for sale of goods"

### Determination of Price (Section 9)

**Three Methods**:

#### 1. Fixed by Contract
- Parties agree on specific price
- **Example**: "Rs. 10 lakhs"

#### 2. Agreed Manner of Ascertainment
- Price determined by formula/method agreed
- **Example**: "Market price on delivery date"

#### 3. Determined by Course of Dealing
- Previous transactions between parties
- **Example**: Regular supplier; price as usual

**Section 9(2)**: If price not determined → Buyer pays **reasonable price** (considering circumstances)

**Section 10**: If price to be fixed by third party valuer:
- Valuer fails to fix → Contract VOID
- BUT if goods delivered and buyer uses → Must pay reasonable price

---

## Conditions and Warranties (Sections 11-17)

**Distinction Critical**: Determines remedy for breach

### Condition (Section 12(2))

**Stipulation essential** to main purpose

**Breach of Condition** → Buyer can:
1. **Reject goods** (treat contract as repudiated), OR
2. **Accept goods** and claim damages (treat as breach of warranty)

**Examples**:
- Sale "by sample" → Goods must correspond to sample (condition)
- Sale "by description" → Goods must match description (condition)

### Warranty (Section 12(3))

**Stipulation collateral** to main purpose

**Breach of Warranty** → Buyer can:
- Only claim **damages** (cannot reject goods)

**Example**: Seller warrants car serviced last month; not serviced → Warranty breach (minor; damages only)

### Section 13: When Condition Becomes Warranty

**Four Situations**:

1. **Acceptance**: Buyer accepts goods (Section 42) → Cannot reject later (condition treated as warranty)
2. **Breach of condition**: Buyer elects to treat as warranty
3. **Fulfillment prevented**: If condition fulfillment prevented by buyer → Treated as warranty
4. **Statute provides**: When Act specifically converts condition to warranty

---

## Implied Conditions and Warranties

**"Implied"**: Not expressly stated; automatically included by law

### Section 14: Implied Conditions

#### (a) Title (Section 14(a))

**Implied Condition**: Seller has right to sell (owns goods or will own at time of sale)

**Breach**: Buyer can reject + recover full price + damages

**Case: Rowland v. Divall (1923)**:
- Buyer bought car from seller
- Car stolen (seller had no title)
- Police seized car (true owner recovered)
- Court held: Total failure of consideration; buyer recovers full price (even after using car for months)

#### (b) Quiet Possession (Section 14(b))

**Implied Warranty**: Buyer shall have and enjoy quiet possession (no disturbance)

**Breach**: If third party lawfully disturbs → Damages

#### (c) Free from Encumbrances (Section 14(c))

**Implied Warranty**: Goods free from undisclosed charges/encumbrances

**Example**: Car sold with undisclosed lien → Breach of warranty

### Section 15: Sale by Description

**Implied Condition**: Goods must correspond to description

**Applies**: Even if buyer examines goods (if sold by description)

**Example**: "Cambodian rice" → Must be Cambodian (not Vietnamese)

**Case: Varley v. Whipp (1900)**:
- Sale of reaping machine (described as new the previous year)
- Machine was old and worthless
- Court held: Breach of condition (did not correspond to description)

### Section 16: Sale by Sample

**Implied Conditions**:
1. **Bulk corresponds** to sample in quality
2. **Opportunity to compare**: Buyer shall have reasonable opportunity to compare bulk with sample
3. **Free from defects**: Goods free from defects rendering them unmerchantable (not apparent on reasonable examination of sample)

**Example**: Fabric sold by sample; bulk different quality → Breach of condition

**Case: Godley v. Perry (1960)**:
- Sale of catapult (sample tested)
- Bulk defective (elastic broke; injured buyer)
- Defect not discoverable on reasonable examination
- Court held: Breach of implied condition (Section 16(c))

### Section 16A: Merchantable Quality

**Implied Condition** (when goods sold by description):
- Goods of **merchantable quality** (fit for ordinary purposes)
- **Exception**: If defects drawn to buyer's attention OR buyer examines and should have discovered

**Merchantable Quality**: As good as goods ordinarily are in that line of trade

**Example**: Buy "apple juice" → Should be fit for drinking (not spoiled)

---

## Transfer of Property (Ownership) - Sections 18-26

**"Property"**: Ownership (not physical possession)

**Why Important?**:
- **Risk** passes with property (Section 26)
- **Remedies** depend on whether property passed
- **Insolvency**: If buyer insolvent after property passes, seller may not recover goods

### Section 18: General Rule

**Property passes** when parties intend it to pass

**Ascertain Intent**: From contract terms, conduct, circumstances

### Section 19: Specific or Ascertained Goods

**Rules for when property passes**:

#### Rule 1: Unconditional Contract for Specific Goods in Deliverable State

**Property passes immediately** when contract made (even if delivery/payment later)

**Deliverable State**: Fit to be delivered (no further act needed)

**Example**: 
- Contract to sell specific car (ready for delivery)
- Property passes when contract made
- Even if payment/delivery next week

**Case: Tarling v. Baxter (1827)**:
- Sale of haystack
- Haystack burned before delivery
- Court held: Property passed when contract made; buyer bears loss

#### Rule 2: Specific Goods Not in Deliverable State

**Property passes** when seller does act to put goods in deliverable state + buyer notified

**Example**:
- Sale of timber (to be cut and seasoned)
- Property passes when timber cut, seasoned, and buyer notified

#### Rule 3: Specific Goods in Deliverable State - Measuring/Testing for Price

**Property passes** when measurement/testing done + buyer notified

**Example**:
- Sale of wheat (price per quintal)
- Property passes when weighed and buyer notified

#### Rule 4: Sale or Return / On Approval

**Property passes** when:
1. Buyer **signifies approval/acceptance**, OR
2. Buyer **does act adopting transaction** (e.g., resells), OR
3. Buyer **retains goods** beyond time fixed (or reasonable time if no time fixed)

**Example**:
- Books sent "on approval" for 7 days
- Buyer keeps beyond 7 days → Property passes (deemed acceptance)

#### Rule 5: Unascertained / Future Goods

**Property passes** when goods in **deliverable state** are **unconditionally appropriated** to contract by seller with buyer's **assent** (express or implied)

**Appropriation**: Irrevocable act setting aside specific goods for this contract

**Assent**: Can be before or after appropriation

**Example**:
- Contract to sell 100 bags of rice (unascertained)
- Seller separates 100 bags, marks buyer's name, notifies buyer
- Property passes (unconditional appropriation + implied assent)

**Delivery to Carrier** (Section 23(1)):
- If seller delivers to carrier for transmission to buyer → Deemed appropriation (property passes)

### Section 20: Risk Passes with Property

```
"Unless otherwise agreed, the goods remain at the seller's risk until the property therein is transferred to the buyer, but when the property therein is transferred to the buyer, the goods are at the buyer's risk whether delivery has been made or not."
```

**Rule**: **Risk follows ownership** (not possession)

**Example**:
- Property passed to buyer
- Goods still with seller (awaiting pickup)
- Goods destroyed by fire (no fault of seller)
- **Loss**: Buyer's (must still pay price)

**Exception**: If either party delays delivery and goods deteriorate → Delay-causing party liable

---

## Performance of Contract (Sections 31-44)

### Section 31: Duties of Seller and Buyer

**Seller's Duty**: Deliver goods

**Buyer's Duty**: Accept and pay

**Concurrent Conditions**: Delivery and payment (unless credit agreed)

### Section 32: Delivery

**Definition**: Voluntary transfer of possession from seller to buyer

**Modes of Delivery**:
1. **Actual Delivery**: Physical handing over
2. **Symbolic Delivery**: Transfer of key, document of title (warehouse receipt, bill of lading)
3. **Constructive Delivery**: 
   - Attornment: Goods with third party; third party acknowledges holds for buyer
   - Transfer of title documents

**Section 35**: Seller must deliver at proper time, place, and manner

### Section 36: Delivery of Wrong Quantity

**Three Scenarios**:

1. **Less than contracted**: Buyer may reject OR accept (pay at contract rate)
2. **More than contracted**: Buyer may:
   - Reject all, OR
   - Accept contract quantity (reject rest), OR
   - Accept all (pay at contract rate for extra)
3. **Mixed with other goods**: Buyer may:
   - Reject all, OR
   - Accept contract goods (reject rest)

### Section 37: Installment Deliveries

**General Rule**: Buyer not bound to accept installments (unless agreed)

**If Agreed**: Each installment is separate contract

**Breach of one installment**: Depends on whether breach goes to whole contract

### Section 38-40: Acceptance

**Acceptance**: Buyer's assent to goods delivered

**When Buyer Deemed to Accept** (Section 42):
1. **Intimates acceptance** to seller
2. **Does act inconsistent** with seller's ownership (e.g., resells)
3. **Retains goods** beyond reasonable time without intimating rejection

**Section 41**: After acceptance, buyer cannot reject (condition becomes warranty; can claim damages only)

**Exception**: Buyer can still reject if:
- Breach goes to root of contract
- Seller concealed latent defect

---

## Rights of Unpaid Seller (Sections 45-54)

**Unpaid Seller** (Section 45):
1. **Price not paid** AND not received equivalent (e.g., dishonored cheque)
2. **Conditional payment** failed (e.g., bill of exchange dishonored)

### Three Rights

#### 1. Lien (Sections 47-49)

**Right to retain possession** until payment (even if property passed to buyer)

**When Available**:
- Goods sold without credit, OR
- Goods sold on credit (credit period expired), OR
- Buyer becomes insolvent

**Lien Lost**:
- Payment/tender of price
- Waiver (seller agrees buyer may sell/dispose)
- Delivery to carrier without reserving right of disposal

**Nature**: Possessory right (not right to sell)

#### 2. Stoppage in Transit (Sections 50-52)

**Right to stop goods** while in transit (before reaching buyer) if buyer becomes insolvent

**Transit Begins**: Goods delivered to carrier

**Transit Ends**:
- Buyer obtains delivery from carrier, OR
- Carrier acknowledges holds goods for buyer, OR
- Buyer obtains delivery before destination, OR
- Carrier wrongfully refuses to deliver

**How to Exercise**: Notice to carrier; carrier must redeliver to seller (seller bears cost)

#### 3. Right of Resale (Section 54)

**Right to resell goods** in certain circumstances

**When Available**:
1. **Perishable goods** (buyer delays taking delivery), OR
2. **Notice to buyer** (seller gives notice of intention to resell; buyer doesn't pay within reasonable time), OR
3. **Seller reserves right of resale**

**Effect of Resale**:
- Original contract rescinded (BUT buyer liable for damages for breach)
- Second buyer gets good title (even if original buyer paid)

---

## Remedies

### Buyer's Remedies

#### 1. Damages for Non-Delivery (Section 57)

**Measure**: Difference between contract price and market price (at time of breach)

**Formula**: Market Price - Contract Price = Damages

**Example**:
- Contract to buy at Rs. 100/kg
- Seller doesn't deliver
- Market price Rs. 120/kg
- Damages: Rs. 20/kg

#### 2. Specific Performance (Section 58)

**When**: Goods are **specific or ascertained** (court's discretion)

**Example**: Sale of rare painting; seller refuses → Court may order delivery

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-SPECIFIC-PERFORMANCE protocol

#### 3. Breach of Warranty (Section 59)

**Buyer's options**:
1. **Damages** for breach (diminution in value), OR
2. **Set-off** damages against price (if sued for price)

**Measure**: Difference between value as warranted and actual value

**Example**:
- Car warranted "2020 model"; actually 2018
- Value difference: Rs. 50,000
- Damages: Rs. 50,000

#### 4. Repudiation Before Delivery (Section 60)

**If seller repudiates before delivery** + buyer elects to treat as breach → Buyer may sue for damages (anticipatory breach)

### Seller's Remedies

#### 1. Action for Price (Section 55)

**When**:
1. **Property passed** to buyer + buyer wrongfully refuses to pay, OR
2. **Price payable on day certain** + buyer wrongfully refuses

**Amount**: Full contract price (not damages)

**Example**: Goods delivered; buyer refuses to pay → Seller sues for Rs. 10 lakhs (price)

#### 2. Damages for Non-Acceptance (Section 61)

**When**: Buyer wrongfully refuses to accept + pay

**Measure**: Difference between contract price and market price (at time of breach)

**Formula**: Contract Price - Market Price = Damages

**Alternative**: If no market, seller may resell and claim difference + incidental expenses

**Example**:
- Contract price Rs. 100/kg
- Buyer refuses
- Market price Rs. 80/kg (seller resells)
- Damages: Rs. 20/kg

---

## Auction Sales (Section 64)

**Section 64: Rules for Auction Sales**

### (1) Sale Complete on Fall of Hammer

**Property passes** when auctioneer's hammer falls (acceptance of bid)

**Before**: Bidder can retract; auctioneer can withdraw goods

### (2) Right to Bid May Be Reserved

**Seller/auctioneer can reserve right** to bid (must be notified)

**If not notified + seller bids** → Sale may be treated as fraudulent by buyer

### (3) Sale Without Reserve

**"Without reserve"**: Goods must be sold to highest bidder (auctioneer cannot withdraw after bidding starts)

### (4) Pretended Bidding / Puffing

**Seller cannot employ person to bid** to increase price (unless notified)

**Effect**: Buyer may avoid contract OR claim damages

---

## Unpaid Seller vs. Other Rights

### Priority of Rights

**Unpaid Seller's Rights vs.**:

#### 1. Bona Fide Purchaser from Buyer

**Section 27-30**: If buyer resold to innocent third party (before seller exercised rights) → Third party protected (gets good title)

**Exceptions**:
- Seller had stopped goods in transit (third party takes subject to seller's rights)
- Seller reserved right of disposal

#### 2. Official Receiver / Liquidator (Buyer's Insolvency)

**Unpaid seller's rights preserved** in insolvency

**Lien + Stoppage**: Available against buyer's estate

---

## Comparison: Sale vs. Other Contracts

### Sale vs. Hire-Purchase

| Aspect | Sale | Hire-Purchase |
|--------|------|---------------|
| **Property** | Passes to buyer (immediate or future) | Remains with owner until all installments paid |
| **Right to return** | Generally no (unless condition breached) | Hirer can return (not obliged to buy) |
| **Default** | Buyer liable for full price | Owner can repossess goods |

**Case: Helby v. Matthews (1895)**:
- Hire-purchase agreement (option to buy after installments)
- Hirer pledged goods
- Court held: Property not passed (hire-purchase ≠ sale); pledge invalid

### Sale vs. Bailment

| Aspect | Sale | Bailment |
|--------|------|-----------|
| **Property** | Transfers | Remains with bailor |
| **Consideration** | Price (money) | No price (may be gratuitous or for service) |
| **Return** | No return (ownership transferred) | Goods returned after purpose fulfilled |

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-BAILMENT-PLEDGE protocol

### Sale vs. Contract for Work & Labour

**Distinction**:
- **Sale of Goods**: Transfer of existing goods (or goods to be produced)
- **Work & Labour**: Skill/service applied; goods incidental

**Test**: Substance of contract (goods transfer OR service provision?)

**Example**:
- **Sale**: Buy ready-made furniture → Sale of goods
- **Work**: Commission carpenter to make custom furniture → Work & labour (primarily service)

**Case: Robinson v. Graves (1935)**:
- Contract for artist to paint portrait
- Court held: Work & labour (not sale); substance is skill/service

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Contract of Sale vs. Agreement to Sell

**Facts**: A contracts to buy B's car for Rs. 5 lakhs; delivery and payment next week

**Analysis**:
- **Specific goods**: Yes (identified car)
- **Deliverable state**: Yes (ready for delivery)
- **Rule 1 (Section 19)**: Property should pass immediately
- **BUT**: Parties intended delivery next week (delayed)
- **Result**: Depends on intent; if parties agreed property passes next week → Agreement to sell (until then)

### Example 2: Risk Passes with Property

**Facts**:
- A agrees to sell specific machine to B for Rs. 10 lakhs
- Contract states "property passes when B pays"
- B hasn't paid yet
- Machine destroyed in fire (no fault)

**Analysis**:
- **Property**: Not passed (payment condition not fulfilled)
- **Section 20**: Risk with property → Risk remains with A (seller)
- **Result**: A bears loss; B not liable to pay

### Example 3: Breach of Condition - Buyer's Rights

**Facts**:
- A buys cloth "pure silk" from B for Rs. 50,000
- Cloth delivered; A accepts and uses for 2 months
- A discovers cloth is polyester blend (not pure silk)
- A seeks to reject goods and recover price

**Analysis**:
- **Sale by description** (Section 15): "Pure silk"
- **Breach**: Cloth not pure silk (breach of condition)
- **Acceptance** (Section 42): A used cloth for 2 months (deemed acceptance)
- **Section 13**: After acceptance, condition becomes warranty
- **Result**: A cannot reject; can only claim damages (difference in value)

### Example 4: Unpaid Seller's Lien

**Facts**:
- A (seller) sells goods to B (buyer) for Rs. 1 lakh (no credit)
- A delivered goods to carrier for transmission to B
- B hasn't paid yet
- Goods still in transit

**Analysis**:
- **Property**: Passed when delivered to carrier (Section 23)
- **Unpaid seller**: Yes (price not paid)
- **Lien**: Lost (delivered to carrier; possession lost)
- **Stoppage in transit**: Available (goods in transit + B not paid)
- **Result**: A can stop goods in transit (if B becomes insolvent); otherwise must wait for delivery

### Example 5: Damages for Non-Delivery

**Facts**:
- Contract to buy 1000 kg rice at Rs. 50/kg (total Rs. 50,000)
- Seller doesn't deliver
- Market price Rs. 60/kg at time of breach
- Buyer had resale contract at Rs. 70/kg (seller knew)

**Analysis**:
- **Section 57**: Damages = Market Price - Contract Price
- **Formula**: (Rs. 60 - Rs. 50) × 1000 kg = Rs. 10,000
- **Special damages**: Loss from resale contract = (Rs. 70 - Rs. 60) × 1000 = Rs. 10,000
- **Hadley v. Baxendale Limb 2**: Seller knew of resale → Special damages recoverable
- **Total**: Rs. 10,000 (market difference) + Rs. 10,000 (resale loss) = Rs. 20,000

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Confusing Property with Possession

**Mistake**: Thinking delivery (possession) = ownership (property)

**Correct**: Property (ownership) and possession are separate; property passes per Sections 18-26 (intent/rules); possession passes on delivery

### 2. Assuming Risk Remains with Possessor

**Mistake**: Thinking risk remains with seller until buyer receives goods

**Correct**: Risk follows property (Section 20), not possession; if property passed, buyer bears risk even if seller still has goods

### 3. Rejecting Goods After Acceptance

**Mistake**: Buyer accepts goods, uses them, then tries to reject months later

**Correct**: After acceptance (Section 42), buyer cannot reject (Section 13); condition becomes warranty; damages only

### 4. Ignoring Buyer's Duty to Mitigate (Unpaid Seller)

**Mistake**: Seller keeps goods idle after buyer's breach; claims full damages

**Correct**: Seller must attempt to resell at best price (mitigate loss); damages = difference between contract and resale price (plus reasonable incidentals)

### 5. Applying Sale of Goods Act to Immovables

**Mistake**: Trying to apply Sale of Goods Act to land/property sale

**Correct**: Sale of Goods Act applies only to **movables** (Section 2(7)); immovable property governed by Transfer of Property Act 1882

# Specific Performance Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-SPECIFIC-PERFORMANCE
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Remedies - Specific Performance
- **Statute**: Specific Relief Act 1963 (Sections 10-20A)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1963

---

## Overview

This protocol covers **specific performance** - an equitable remedy compelling the defaulting party to actually perform the contract (instead of paying damages).

**Key Principle**: Specific performance is granted when damages are inadequate remedy, typically for unique property or where monetary compensation cannot adequately compensate the injured party.

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 10: Cases Where Specific Performance Enforceable

```
"Except as otherwise provided in this Chapter, the specific performance of any contract may, in the discretion of the court, be enforced—

(a) when there exists no standard for ascertaining the actual damage caused by the non-performance of the act agreed to be done; or
(b) when the act agreed to be done is such that compensation in money for its non-performance would not afford adequate relief."
```

**Discretionary Remedy**: Court decides whether to grant

**When Granted**:
1. **No standard** for ascertaining damages, OR
2. **Damages inadequate** remedy

---

## When Specific Performance Granted

### 1. Contracts for Sale of Immovable Property

**General Rule**: Specific performance routinely granted for land/property

**Reason**: Every piece of land is unique; damages inadequate

**Section 10**: Sale of immovable property → Specific performance available

**Example**: A contracts to sell house to B; A refuses → Court can order A to transfer house

**Case: Rajendra Prasad v. Urmila Devi (1980)** (Supreme Court):
- Contract for sale of land
- Seller refused to transfer
- Court held: Specific performance granted (land unique; damages inadequate)

### 2. Contracts for Unique Movables

**Unique/Rare Goods**: Articles with special value (not just monetary)

**Examples**:
- Antique furniture
- Rare paintings/artwork
- Heirlooms with sentimental value
- Limited edition items

**Example**: Contract to sell rare vintage car; seller refuses → Specific performance may be granted

**Case: Behnke v. Bede Shipping Co. (1927)**:
- Contract to sell specific ship
- Court held: Specific performance granted (ship unique; specific tonnage and specifications)

### 3. Partnership Agreements

**Section 14(b)**: Specific performance of partnership contract may be granted

**Reason**: Personal relationship; damages inadequate for breach of partnership terms

### 4. Contracts Where Damages Not Ascertainable

**No Standard**: If impossible to calculate loss in monetary terms

**Example**: Contract for specific performance by artist (unique service)

---

## When Specific Performance NOT Granted

### Section 14: Contracts Not Specifically Enforceable

#### (a) Contracts for Non-Pecuniary Nature

**Section 14(1)(a)**: Not enforceable if "the act agreed to be done is such that the court cannot supervise the execution thereof"

**Example**: Contract for personal services requiring continuous supervision

**Reason**: Courts cannot supervise day-to-day performance

#### (b) Contracts for Personal Service / Trust

**Section 14(1)(c)**: "Contracts involving the performance of personal acts which require personal skill or confidence"

**Examples**:
- Employment contracts (cannot force employee to work)
- Artist to paint portrait (personal skill)
- Doctor to perform surgery (personal service)

**Reason**: Violates personal liberty (Article 21); akin to involuntary servitude

**Case: Chitti Babu v. Sreeramula (1970)**:
- Employment contract
- Court held: Cannot grant specific performance (personal service)

**Exception**: Negative covenants enforceable by injunction
- "Employee will not work for competitor" → Injunction possible (prohibitory)
- "Employee must work for employer" → Specific performance NOT possible (mandatory)

#### (c) Contracts for Standard Goods (Readily Available)

**Section 14(1)(b)**: "Contracts which can be adequately relieved by compensation in money"

**Standard/Generic Goods**: Available in market at determinable price

**Example**: Contract to buy 100 tons of wheat → Specific performance NOT granted (wheat available in market; damages adequate)

**Case: Jai Gopal v. Gumani Ram (1965)**:
- Contract to supply timber
- Court held: Specific performance not granted (timber available in market)

#### (d) Determinable Contracts at Plaintiff's Option

**Section 14(1)(d)**: If contract determinable at plaintiff's option → Not specifically enforceable

**Reason**: Inequitable (plaintiff can exit anytime but force defendant to perform)

#### (e) Performance Involves Multiple Acts

**Section 14(1)(e)**: Contract requiring continuous performance over time

**Example**: Long-term supply contracts requiring daily deliveries

**Reason**: Court cannot supervise ongoing performance

#### (f) Contracts in Nature of Revocable or Terminable

**Section 14(1)(f)**: Contracts which parties could lawfully terminate

**Example**: Partnership at will (can be terminated anytime)

---

## Discretion of Court (Section 20)

**Section 20**: "The jurisdiction to decree specific performance is discretionary, and the court is not bound to grant such relief merely because it is lawful to do so; but the discretion of the court is not arbitrary but sound and reasonable, guided by judicial principles and capable of correction by a court of appeal."

**Discretionary Factors**:

### 1. Conduct of Plaintiff (Clean Hands Doctrine)

**Maxim**: "He who seeks equity must do equity"

**Rule**: Plaintiff's conduct must be fair

**Case: Ameer Bee v. Mohideen (1971)**:
- Plaintiff delayed performance
- Court refused specific performance (plaintiff's conduct)

**Example**: Plaintiff defaulted on payment; later seeks specific performance → May be refused

### 2. Hardship to Defendant

**Section 20(2)(b)**: Court may refuse if performance causes hardship "which the plaintiff either could have avoided or adequately compensated"

**Example**: Defendant mortgaged property to third party in good faith (believing contract cancelled) → Specific performance may cause hardship

**Balance**: Court balances plaintiff's loss vs defendant's hardship

### 3. Adequacy of Damages

**Even if land/unique goods**: If damages adequate in particular case → May refuse specific performance

### 4. Delay / Laches

**Unreasonable Delay**: Plaintiff delayed in seeking relief

**Example**: Breach in 2020; plaintiff sues in 2025 → Court may refuse (laches)

**Limitation**: 3 years from breach (Limitation Act)

### 5. Impossibility / Illegality

**If performance impossible or illegal** → Cannot grant specific performance

**Example**: Property destroyed after contract → Specific performance impossible

---

## Substituted Performance (Section 20A)

**Section 20A** (Added by Amendment Act 2018): If defendant fails to perform decree → Plaintiff can get substituted performance

**Substituted Performance**: Court allows plaintiff to get performance from third party at defendant's cost

**Example**: Defendant to construct building; doesn't perform → Court allows plaintiff to hire contractor; cost recovered from defendant

**Objective**: Ensure actual performance (not just monetary compensation)

---

## Part Performance (Section 53A Transfer of Property Act)

**Section 53A**: Part performance doctrine

**Rule**: If buyer partly performed (took possession, made improvements) → Seller cannot evict

**Protection**: Buyer who partially performed in good faith protected from eviction

**Requirements**:
1. **Contract in writing**
2. **Part performance** by buyer (took possession, paid part price, made improvements)
3. **Good faith**

**Example**: Buyer pays 50%, takes possession, renovates; seller refuses to transfer → Buyer protected from eviction; can seek specific performance

---

## Compensation in Addition to Specific Performance (Section 21)

**Section 21**: "In a suit for specific performance, the plaintiff may also claim compensation for its breach, either in addition to, or in substitution for, such performance."

**Options**:
1. **Specific performance + Compensation** for delay/partial breach
2. **Compensation instead of specific performance** (if court refuses specific performance)

**Example**: Contract to transfer property by June 1; defendant delays till August 1
- Court grants specific performance (transfer now)
- Plus compensation for 2-month delay (loss of use/rental income)

---

## Mutuality Principle (Historical)

**Historical Rule**: Specific performance granted only if remedy available to **both** parties (mutuality)

**Abolished**: Mutuality principle no longer strictly applied in India

**Current Position**: Court grants if appropriate, even if remedy not available to both parties

**Example**: Contract with minor (minor cannot be compelled) → But specific performance may be granted against adult party if equitable

---

## Damages in Lieu of Specific Performance

**If specific performance refused** → Plaintiff can claim damages

**Section 21**: Compensation in substitution for specific performance

**Example**: Court refuses specific performance (defendant's hardship) → Plaintiff awarded damages instead

---

## Practical Examples

### Example 1: Sale of Land (Specific Performance Granted)

**Facts**: A agrees to sell land to B for Rs. 50 lakhs; A refuses to transfer

**Analysis**:
- **Immovable property**: Land unique (Section 10)
- **Damages inadequate**: Cannot replace this specific land
- **Result**: Court grants specific performance (A must transfer land)

### Example 2: Standard Goods (Specific Performance Refused)

**Facts**: A contracts to supply 1000 kg rice to B; A refuses

**Analysis**:
- **Standard goods**: Rice available in market
- **Damages adequate**: B can buy from market; claim difference
- **Section 14(1)(b)**: Damages adequate remedy
- **Result**: Specific performance NOT granted; B gets damages

### Example 3: Personal Service (Refused)

**Facts**: A (employee) contracts to work for B (employer) for 2 years; A quits after 6 months

**Analysis**:
- **Personal service**: Employment contract (Section 14(1)(c))
- **Cannot compel**: Forcing work violates personal liberty
- **Result**: Specific performance NOT granted
- **Alternative**: B can seek injunction (prevent A from working for competitor if negative covenant exists)

### Example 4: Unique Movable (Granted)

**Facts**: A contracts to sell rare antique painting to B; A refuses

**Analysis**:
- **Unique good**: Rare painting (cannot be replaced)
- **Damages inadequate**: Monetary compensation cannot replace unique artwork
- **Section 10**: Adequate relief not possible by damages
- **Result**: Specific performance MAY be granted (court's discretion)

### Example 5: Plaintiff's Conduct (Refused)

**Facts**:
- A (buyer) contracts to buy property from B for Rs. 1 crore
- A pays Rs. 10 lakhs advance; balance due in 3 months
- A delays payment by 2 years
- A now seeks specific performance

**Analysis**:
- **Plaintiff's delay**: Unreasonable delay (2 years)
- **Clean hands doctrine**: A's conduct not equitable
- **Section 20**: Court's discretion
- **Result**: Specific performance likely REFUSED (plaintiff's laches)

## Comparison: Specific Performance vs Damages

| Aspect | Specific Performance | Damages |
|--------|---------------------|---------|
| **Nature** | Equitable remedy | Legal remedy (common law) |
| **Effect** | Actual performance compelled | Monetary compensation |
| **Availability** | Discretionary (court decides) | Right (if breach proved) |
| **When Granted** | Damages inadequate (unique property) | Always available (if breach) |
| **Examples** | Sale of land, unique goods | Standard goods, calculable loss |

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Assuming Specific Performance Always Available

**Mistake**: Expecting specific performance for all breaches

**Correct**: Only when damages inadequate (unique property, no standard for damages)

### 2. Personal Service Contracts

**Mistake**: Seeking specific performance of employment/personal service contracts

**Correct**: Cannot compel personal service (Section 14(1)(c)); only damages or injunction (negative covenant)

### 3. Delay in Filing Suit

**Mistake**: Waiting years before seeking specific performance

**Correct**: File promptly; unreasonable delay (laches) → Court may refuse

### 4. Unclean Hands

**Mistake**: Plaintiff breached or delayed, then seeks specific performance

**Correct**: Plaintiff must have equitable conduct (clean hands doctrine)

### 5. Standard Goods

**Mistake**: Seeking specific performance for readily available market goods

**Correct**: Specific performance not available if goods available in market (damages adequate)

# Void and Voidable Contracts Protocol (India)

## Protocol Metadata
- **Protocol ID**: IL-CONTRACT-VOID-VOIDABLE
- **Version**: 1.0.0
- **Domain**: Contract Law (India)
- **Subdomain**: Contract Formation - Validity Classification
- **Statute**: Indian Contract Act 1872 (Sections 2(g), 2(i), 2(j), 10, 64-67)
- **Last Updated**: January 2025
- **Effective**: Since 1872

---

## Overview

This protocol clarifies the critical distinction between **void agreements**, **void contracts**, and **voidable contracts** - a fundamental classification in contract law.

**Key Principle**: Understanding whether a contract is void or voidable determines enforceability, remedies, and rights of parties.

---

## Statutory Framework

### Section 2(g): Void Agreement

```
"An agreement not enforceable by law is said to be void."
```

**Void Agreement**: No legal effect from inception (void ab initio)

### Section 2(j): Void Contract

```
"A contract which ceases to be enforceable by law becomes void when it ceases to be enforceable."
```

**Void Contract**: Initially valid contract that becomes void due to subsequent impossibility or illegality

### Section 2(i): Voidable Contract

```
"An agreement which is enforceable by law at the option of one or more of the parties thereto, but not at the option of the other or others, is a voidable contract."
```

**Voidable Contract**: Valid until aggrieved party chooses to avoid it

---

## Void Agreements

### Definition and Nature

**Void Agreement**:
- **No legal effect** whatsoever
- **Void ab initio** (void from the beginning)
- **No contract exists** at all
- **Cannot be enforced** by either party
- **Cannot be ratified** or made valid

**Metaphor**: "Agreement is a stillborn child" - never had life

### Causes of Void Agreements

#### 1. Agreement by Minor (Section 11)

**Rule**: Minor's agreement is VOID (Mohori Bibee case)

**Example**: 16-year-old signs contract → VOID

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-CAPACITY protocol

#### 2. Agreement by Person of Unsound Mind (Section 12)

**Rule**: Person of unsound mind (at time of contract) → VOID

**Example**: Insane person signs deed → VOID

#### 3. Agreement Without Consideration (Section 25)

**General Rule**: Agreement without consideration VOID

**Exceptions**: Love and affection (written, registered), past services, time-barred debt

**Example**: Promise to gift Rs. 1 lakh without consideration → VOID (unless exception applies)

#### 4. Agreement with Unlawful Object or Consideration (Section 23)

**Rule**: Unlawful object or consideration → VOID

**Example**: Agreement to commit crime → VOID

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-LEGALITY protocol

#### 5. Agreement in Restraint of Marriage (Section 26)

**Rule**: Restraint of adult's marriage → VOID

**Example**: "I'll pay you Rs. 10 lakhs if you never marry" → VOID

#### 6. Agreement in Restraint of Trade (Section 27)

**General Rule**: Restraint of lawful trade → VOID

**Exception**: Sale of goodwill (reasonable restraint valid)

**Example**: "You cannot practice medicine anywhere" → VOID

#### 7. Agreement in Restraint of Legal Proceedings (Section 28)

**Rule**: Absolute bar on legal proceedings → VOID

**Exception**: Arbitration agreement (valid alternative)

**Example**: "You cannot sue me in any court" → VOID

#### 8. Agreement with Uncertain Meaning (Section 29)

**Rule**: Uncertain or vague terms → VOID

**Example**: "I'll sell at reasonable price" → May be VOID (uncertain)

#### 9. Wagering Agreement (Section 30)

**Rule**: Betting or gambling → VOID

**Example**: Bet Rs. 1 lakh on cricket match → VOID

#### 10. Bilateral Mistake of Fact (Section 20)

**Rule**: Both parties mistaken about essential fact → VOID

**Example**: Both believe painting is original; it's fake → VOID

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-FREE-CONSENT protocol

### Effect of Void Agreement

**Consequences**:

1. **No Legal Rights or Obligations**:
   - Neither party can sue
   - Neither party can enforce
   - No damages, no specific performance

2. **No Estoppel**:
   - Even if party represented agreement as valid
   - Estoppel cannot create contract where none exists

3. **Money/Property Transferred**:
   - **Section 65**: Person receiving advantage under void agreement must restore
   - **But**: Court may deny restitution if both parties equally guilty (in pari delicto)

**Section 65**: "When an agreement is discovered to be void, or when a contract becomes void, any person who has received any advantage under such agreement or contract is bound to restore it, or to make compensation for it to the person from whom he received it."

**Case: Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903)**:
- Minor borrowed money under void agreement
- Court held: Agreement void; minor not obliged to repay (but lender may recover property if still identifiable in minor's possession - equitable relief)

---

## Void Contracts

### Definition and Nature

**Void Contract**:
- **Initially VALID** contract
- **Becomes void** due to subsequent event
- **Void when it ceases to be enforceable** (Section 2(j))

**Key Difference from Void Agreement**:
- Void agreement: Never valid (void ab initio)
- Void contract: Was valid, became void later

### Causes of Void Contracts

#### 1. Supervening Impossibility (Section 56)

**Rule**: If performance becomes impossible after contract formed (without fault of parties) → Contract becomes VOID

**Section 56**: "An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void... A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, unlawful, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful."

**Examples**:

**A. Destruction of Subject Matter**:
- Contract to sell specific goods; goods destroyed before delivery → VOID

**Case: Taylor v. Caldwell (1863)** (English case, persuasive):
- Contract to rent music hall for concerts
- Hall burned down before concert date
- Court held: Contract void (impossibility)

**B. Death or Incapacity** (Personal Service Contracts):
- Contract for artist to paint portrait; artist dies → VOID

**C. Supervening Illegality**:
- Contract to export goods; government bans export → VOID

**Case: Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur (1954)** (Supreme Court):
- Land sale agreement; government requisitioned land (compulsory acquisition)
- Seller could not transfer
- Court held: Contract void (supervening impossibility)

#### 2. Contingent Contract Failure (Sections 32-36)

**Contingent Contract**: Performance depends on happening or non-happening of uncertain future event

**Section 32**: "Contingent contract is a contract to do or not to do something, if some event, collateral to such contract, does or does not happen."

**Examples**:
- "I'll sell you my house if I get transfer to another city"
- Insurance contract (contingent on loss occurring)

**When Contingent Contract Becomes VOID**:

**Section 33**: If event cannot happen → VOID
- "I'll pay you Rs. 1 lakh if A comes back from dead" → VOID (impossible event)

**Section 34**: If event becomes impossible within specified time → VOID
- "I'll pay you if ship arrives by Dec 31"; ship sinks before Dec 31 → VOID

---

## Voidable Contracts

### Definition and Nature

**Voidable Contract**:
- **Valid and enforceable** unless aggrieved party chooses to avoid
- **At option of aggrieved party** (one party can rescind)
- **Other party cannot rescind** (only aggrieved party)
- **Can be affirmed** (aggrieved party chooses to continue)

**Metaphor**: "Contract has a defect, but aggrieved party can choose to overlook it"

### Causes of Voidable Contracts

#### 1. Coercion (Section 15)

**Rule**: Consent obtained by coercion → VOIDABLE

**Example**: "Sign or I'll harm you" → VOIDABLE at victim's option

**See**: IL-CONTRACT-FREE-CONSENT protocol

#### 2. Undue Influence (Section 16)

**Rule**: Consent obtained by undue influence → VOIDABLE

**Example**: Doctor persuades patient to gift property → VOIDABLE

#### 3. Fraud (Section 17)

**Rule**: Consent obtained by fraud → VOIDABLE

**Example**: Seller lies about car's accident history → VOIDABLE

**Plus**: Damages for tort of deceit

#### 4. Misrepresentation (Section 18)

**Rule**: Consent obtained by innocent misrepresentation → VOIDABLE

**Example**: Seller honestly but mistakenly states car never had accident → VOIDABLE

**No damages** (innocent misrepresentation)

#### 5. Unilateral Mistake (Limited Circumstances)

**General Rule**: Unilateral mistake → Contract VALID

**Exceptions** (Contract VOIDABLE or VOID):
- **Mistake as to identity** of person (if identity essential)
- **Non est factum** (fundamental mistake as to nature of document)

**Example**: A intends to contract with B; C impersonates B → VOIDABLE

#### 6. Contract with Intoxicated/Drugged Person

**Rule**: Contract with person so intoxicated they couldn't understand → VOIDABLE

**Requirement**: Other party knew of intoxication

**Example**: Person heavily drunk signs deed; other party knew → VOIDABLE

### Rights of Aggrieved Party (Section 19, 19A)

**Section 19**: "When consent to an agreement is caused by coercion, fraud, or misrepresentation, the agreement is a contract voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused."

**Section 19A**: "When consent to an agreement is caused by undue influence, the agreement is a contract voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused; and the court may set aside the contract, or may require the party benefited to return the benefit, or may set aside the contract only in part, retaining the rest."

**Options**:

1. **Rescind (Avoid) Contract**:
   - Cancel contract entirely
   - Restore parties to original position (Section 64)
   - Demand return of any benefit conferred

2. **Affirm Contract**:
   - Choose to continue with contract
   - Waive right to rescind
   - Enforce contract as valid

3. **Seek Damages** (For Fraud):
   - In addition to rescission
   - Sue for tort of deceit

**Section 64: Restitution**

```
"When a person at whose option a contract is voidable rescinds it, the other party thereto need not perform any promise therein contained in which he is promisor. The party rescinding a voidable contract shall, if he has received any benefit thereunder from another party to such contract, restore such benefit, so far as may be, to the person from whom it was received."
```

**Mutual Restoration**:
- Aggrieved party must return benefit received
- Other party must return benefit received
- Restore parties to pre-contract position

### Loss of Right to Rescind

**When aggrieved party CANNOT rescind**:

#### 1. Affirmation (Express or Implied)

**Express Affirmation**: Aggrieved party states they will continue with contract

**Implied Affirmation**: Conduct indicating acceptance
- **Example**: Aggrieved party uses purchased goods for extended period after discovering fraud → Implied affirmation

**Case: Long v. Lloyd (1958)**:
- Buyer purchased lorry based on seller's misrepresentation
- Buyer used lorry, attempted repairs
- Later tried to rescind
- Court held: Cannot rescind (implied affirmation by continued use)

#### 2. Lapse of Time

**Unreasonable Delay**: If aggrieved party delays rescission unreasonably → Right lost

**Reasonable Time**: Depends on circumstances
- Fraud: Time runs from discovery of fraud (not from contract date)
- Misrepresentation: Generally from contract date

**Case: Leaf v. International Galleries (1950)**:
- Buyer purchased painting (misrepresented as by Constable)
- Discovered mistake 5 years later
- Court held: Cannot rescind (lapsed too much time)

#### 3. Third Party Rights

**Innocent Third Party**: If third party acquires rights in good faith → Cannot rescind

**Example**: A sells car to B (induced by fraud); B sells car to C (innocent buyer); A discovers fraud → A cannot rescind and recover car from C (C is innocent third party; protected)

**But**: A can sue B for damages (tort of deceit)

#### 4. Impossibility of Restitution

**Cannot Restore Original Position**: If benefit cannot be returned → May lose right to rescind

**Example**: Aggrieved party consumed goods → Cannot return; may lose right to rescind

**But**: Court may allow rescission with compensation for consumed goods (Section 64)

## Comparative Table: Void vs Voidable

| Aspect | Void Agreement | Void Contract | Voidable Contract |
|--------|----------------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Legal Effect** | No effect from beginning | Initially valid, becomes void later | Valid until rescinded |
| **Enforceability** | Never enforceable | Was enforceable; ceased to be | Enforceable unless aggrieved party rescinds |
| **Who Can Avoid?** | No one (void by law) | No one (automatic) | Aggrieved party only |
| **Ratification** | Cannot be ratified | Cannot be ratified | Can be affirmed |
| **Examples** | Minor's contract, unlawful object | Supervening impossibility, contingent contract failure | Coercion, fraud, misrepresentation, undue influence |
| **Restitution** | Section 65 (restore benefit) | Section 65 (restore benefit) | Section 64 (mutual restoration) |
| **Damages** | No (void contract) | No (frustration; no fault) | Yes (fraud only - tort of deceit) |

---

## Section 64-67: Rescission and Restitution

### Section 64: Consequences of Rescission

```
"When a person at whose option a contract is voidable rescinds it, the other party thereto need not perform any promise therein contained in which he is promisor. The party rescinding a voidable contract shall, if he has received any benefit thereunder from another party to such contract, restore such benefit, so far as may be, to the person from whom it was received."
```

**Mutual Obligations**:
1. Aggrieved party rescinding must return benefit received
2. Other party released from obligations
3. Other party must return benefit received

**"So far as may be"**: If exact restoration impossible, compensate

### Section 65: Obligation to Return Benefits Under Void Agreements

```
"When an agreement is discovered to be void, or when a contract becomes void, any person who has received any advantage under such agreement or contract is bound to restore it, or to make compensation for it to the person from whom he received it."
```

**Application**:
- Void agreements (Section 2(g))
- Contracts becoming void (Section 2(j))

**Exception**: In pari delicto (both parties equally guilty) → Court may deny restitution

### Section 66: Mode of Communicating Rescission

```
"The rescission of a voidable contract may be communicated or revoked in the same manner, and subject to the same rules, as apply to the communication or revocation of a proposal."
```

**Communication**:
- Must be communicated to other party
- Same rules as offer/acceptance (Section 4)
- Can be express or implied (by conduct)

### Section 67: Effect of Neglect to Rescind Promptly

```
"If a party to a contract has received any benefit thereunder, that contract cannot be rescinded unless such party is able to restore such benefit."
```

**Restitution Requirement**: Must be able to restore benefit (or compensate)

```
START
  ↓
Check FORMATION DEFECTS:
  - Minor/unsound mind?
  - No consideration (without exception)?
  - Unlawful object/consideration?
  - Restraint of marriage/trade/legal proceedings (without exception)?

  └─ NO → Proceed

  ↓
Check if INITIALLY VALID but BECAME VOID:
  - Supervening impossibility (Section 56)?
  - Contingent contract failure (Sections 32-36)?
  ├─ YES → VOID CONTRACT (was valid, now void)
  └─ NO → Proceed

  ↓
Check VITIATED CONSENT:
  - Coercion (Section 15)?
  - Undue influence (Section 16)?
  - Fraud (Section 17)?
  - Misrepresentation (Section 18)?
  - Unilateral mistake (identity/non est factum)?
  - Intoxication (known to other party)?
  ├─ YES → VOIDABLE CONTRACT (aggrieved party can rescind)
  └─ NO → VALID CONTRACT ✓

```

## Practice Examples

### Example 1: Void Agreement (Minor)

**Facts**:
- 16-year-old signs contract to buy car for Rs. 5 lakhs
- Pays Rs. 1 lakh advance
- Later refuses to pay balance

**Analysis**:
- **Minor's agreement**: VOID (Mohori Bibee)
- **Section 65**: Seller must return Rs. 1 lakh advance
- **Effect**: No contract; minor not liable for balance

### Example 2: Void Contract (Supervening Impossibility)

**Facts**:
- A agrees to lease hall to B for wedding on Dec 25
- On Dec 20, hall burns down (not A's fault)
- B demands A arrange alternative hall

**Analysis**:
- **Initially valid**: Contract validly formed
- **Supervening impossibility**: Subject matter destroyed (Section 56)
- **Effect**: Contract becomes VOID
- **Result**: Neither party liable (both excused)

### Example 3: Voidable Contract (Fraud)

**Facts**:
- Seller falsely states car "never had accident"
- Buyer relies, purchases for Rs. 8 lakhs
- Buyer discovers car was in major accident (worth Rs. 4 lakhs)

**Analysis**:
- **Fraud**: False representation + intent to deceive + reliance
- **Effect**: Contract VOIDABLE at buyer's option (Section 19)
- **Buyer's options**:
  1. Rescind + return car + recover Rs. 8 lakhs + damages
  2. Affirm contract + sue for damages only
- **Result**: Buyer chooses to rescind; returns car; recovers Rs. 8 lakhs + damages

### Example 4: Loss of Right to Rescind (Third Party Rights)

**Facts**:
- A sells car to B (induced by B's fraud) for Rs. 5 lakhs
- B sells same car to C (innocent buyer) for Rs. 6 lakhs
- A discovers fraud; wants to rescind and recover car

**Analysis**:
- **Voidable at A's option**: Due to fraud
- **Third party rights**: C acquired rights in good faith
- **Effect**: A cannot rescind and recover car from C (C protected)
- **Remedy**: A can sue B for damages (tort of deceit)

### Example 5: Affirmation (Loss of Right to Rescind)

**Facts**:
- A buys land from B based on B's misrepresentation
- A discovers misrepresentation after 6 months
- A continues using land for 2 more years
- A then tries to rescind

**Analysis**:
- **Misrepresentation**: Contract initially voidable
- **Affirmation by conduct**: Continued use for 2 years after discovery
- **Lapse of time**: Unreasonable delay
- **Effect**: Right to rescind lost (affirmation + lapse of time)
- **Result**: Cannot rescind (but may claim damages if fraud, not mere misrepresentation)

## Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

### 1. Confusing Void with Voidable

**Mistake**: Thinking void agreement can be affirmed/ratified

**Correct**:
- **Void**: Never valid; cannot be ratified
- **Voidable**: Valid until rescinded; can be affirmed

### 2. Thinking Both Parties Can Rescind Voidable Contract

**Mistake**: Assuming either party can avoid voidable contract

**Correct**: Only **aggrieved party** can rescind (party whose consent vitiated)

### 3. Forgetting Restitution Obligations

**Mistake**: Rescinding contract but keeping benefit received

**Correct**: Section 64 - Must restore benefit (mutual restoration)

### 4. Delay in Exercising Right to Rescind

**Mistake**: Discovering fraud/misrepresentation but delaying rescission for years

**Correct**: Rescind **promptly** after discovery; unreasonable delay = loss of right

### 5. Void vs Void Contract

**Mistake**: Treating all void situations the same

**Correct**:
- **Void agreement**: Never valid (formation defect)
- **Void contract**: Was valid, became void (supervening impossibility)

### 6. Assuming Section 65 Always Applies

**Mistake**: Thinking can always recover benefit under void agreement

**Correct**: **In pari delicto** doctrine - If both equally guilty, court may deny restitution
