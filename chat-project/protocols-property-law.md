# Indian Property Law: Easements and Servitudes

## Purpose
This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on easements and servitudes under the Indian Easements Act 1882 (Sections 4-55). It covers essential elements of easements, acquisition by grant/prescription/necessity, rights of way, light and air easements, water rights, termination of easements, licenses vs easements distinction, and practical considerations for property transactions involving easements.

## Activation Triggers
Use this protocol when:
- Client's property has easement (right of way, light, air, drainage)
- Neighbor claiming prescriptive easement (20/30 years use)
- Dispute over access rights/pathway
- Construction blocking neighbor's light or air
- Water flow/drainage issues between properties
- Title verification revealing easement burden
- Creating new easement by deed/agreement
- Termination of easement (abandonment, unity of possession)
- Easement vs license determination
- Quasi-easement in land partitions
- Ancillary rights with property purchase
- Public easement vs private easement
- Extinguishment of easement

## Core Principles

### 1. Section 4: Easement Defined

**"An easement is a right which the owner or occupier of certain land possesses, as such, for the beneficial enjoyment of that land, to do and continue to do something, or to prevent and continue to prevent something being done, in or upon, or in respect of certain other land not his own."**

Essential Elements of Easement:
1. **TWO PROPERTIES** - Dominant heritage (benefited) and Servient heritage (burdened)
2. **BENEFICIAL ENJOYMENT** - For better enjoyment of dominant heritage
3. **RIGHT TO DO OR PREVENT** - Positive (do something) or negative (prevent something)
4. **NOT OWNERSHIP** - Right only, not ownership of servient land
5. **CONTINUES** - Not temporary permission
6. **APPURTENANT** - Attached to land (not personal)

**Key Terminology:**

- **Dominant Heritage/Tenement:** Property benefited by easement
- **Servient Heritage/Tenement:** Property burdened by easement  
- **Dominant Owner:** Owner of dominant heritage
- **Servient Owner:** Owner of servient heritage

**Example:**
- Property A (landlocked) has right of way over Property B to reach public road
- Property A = Dominant heritage (benefited)
- Property B = Servient heritage (burdened)
- Right of way = Easement

**Types of Easements:**

**A. Positive Easements (Right to DO):**
- Right of way (walk, drive over servient land)
- Right to draw water (from well, spring on servient land)
- Right of drainage (discharge water onto servient land)
- Right to use pathway, bridge
- Right to graze cattle

**B. Negative Easements (Right to PREVENT):**
- Right of light (prevent servient owner from blocking light)
- Right of air (prevent blocking air/ventilation)
- Right to lateral support (prevent excavation damaging dominant land)
- Right to prevent construction (beyond certain height)

### 2. Easement vs License

**Critical Distinction:**

| Aspect | Easement | License |
|--------|----------|---------|
| **Interest in Property** | YES (property right) | NO (personal permission) |
| **Transferability** | Transfers with land | NOT transferable |
| **Revocability** | Generally irrevocable | Revocable (Section 60 Easements Act) |
| **Duration** | Permanent (unless terminated) | Temporary |
| **Creation** | Grant, prescription, necessity | Permission/consent |
| **Successor Bound** | Binds successors | Does NOT bind successors |
| **Two Properties Required** | YES (dominant + servient) | NO (licensor's land only) |

**License Defined (Section 52 Easements Act):**
"Where one person grants to another, or to a definite number of other persons, a right to do, or continue to do, in or upon the immovable property of the grantor, something which would, in the absence of such right, be unlawful, and such right does not amount to an easement or an interest in the property, the right is called a LICENSE."

**Example (Easement):**
- A owns Plot 1 (landlocked)
- A has permanent right of way over B's Plot 2 to reach road
- Right transfers when A sells Plot 1
- = EASEMENT

**Example (License):**
- A allows B (neighbor) to park car in A's driveway occasionally
- Permission personal to B
- Revocable by A anytime
- Does not transfer if B sells property
- = LICENSE

### 3. Acquisition of Easements

**Section 15-24: Four Methods**

#### Method 1: By GRANT (Section 15)

**Express Grant:**
- Created by written document
- Deed of easement/grant
- Registered (if immovable property)

**Example:**
- A grants right of way to B (owner of adjoining landlocked plot)
- Execute registered deed granting easement
- Permanent easement created

**Implied Grant:**
- Quasi-easement (Section 15)
- When land divided, existing usage continues as easement
- Example: Single property with pathway to backyard; on partition, pathway becomes easement for backyard owner

**Registration:**
- Easement deed must be REGISTERED if:
  - For period > 1 year, OR
  - Perpetual easement, OR
  - On immovable property
- Section 17 Registration Act 1908

---

#### Method 2: By PRESCRIPTION (Section 15-25)

**Section 15:**
```
"An easement may be acquired by virtue of a local custom and by long and 
uninterrupted enjoyment of the easement."
```

**Prescriptive Easement = Acquisition by Long Use**

**Requirements (ALL Must Be Satisfied):**

1. **CONTINUOUS USE** - Uninterrupted use for required period
2. **OPEN AND PEACEABLE** - Not by force, stealth, or permission
3. **AS OF RIGHT** - Nec vi, nec clam, nec precario (without force, secrecy, permission)
4. **STATUTORY PERIOD** - 20 years (as of right without interruption)

**Section 25 - Prescriptive Period:**
```
"Where the access and use of light or air to and for any building has been 
actually enjoyed therewith for the full period of twenty years, the right to 
such access and use shall be absolute and indefeasible."
```

**20 Years Rule:**
- For light and air: 20 years
- For other easements: 20 years (general rule)

**Exception - Government Land:**
- Section 29: Easement cannot be acquired by prescription against Government land
- 30 years minimum (if at all permissible by statute)

**Evidence of Prescriptive Easement:**
- Witness testimony (long-time residents)
- Photographs showing pathway/usage
- Municipal records, maps
- Property tax documents showing pathway

**Example:**
- Property A owner walking through Property B for 25 years to reach road
- Usage continuous, open, as of right (not by permission)
- After 20 years: Easement acquired by prescription
- B cannot obstruct pathway

**Interruption of Prescription:**
- If servient owner obstructs use for 1 year+ continuously
- Prescription period resets
- Section 17: Interruption for less than 1 year = No effect

---

#### Method 3: By NECESSITY (Section 13)

**Easement of Necessity = Landlocked Property**

**Section 13:**
```
"Where, on the partition of any property, one portion has, and the other has not, 
access to a public way or navigable river, the former portion is subject to an 
easement of way across it in favor of the latter portion to the public way or 
navigable river."
```

**Conditions:**
1. Property landlocked (no access to public road)
2. Necessity arose due to partition/conveyance
3. No alternative access available

**Example:**
- Single property with road access in front
- Divided into front and back portions
- Front portion has road access
- Back portion landlocked
- Easement of way automatically created in favor of back portion across front

**Scope:**
- Necessity route (shortest, least burdensome)
- Cannot claim multiple routes (only one necessary)
- Servient owner can designate route (if reasonable)

**Cessation:**
- If alternative access becomes available
- Easement of necessity terminates

---

#### Method 4: By LOCAL CUSTOM (Section 15)

**Local Custom = Community Practice**

**Conditions:**
1. Custom ancient (from time immemorial)
2. Continuous and certain
3. Reasonable
4. Not against statute

**Example:**
- Village custom allowing all residents to draw water from community well
- Easement by custom

**Rarely Applied:** Modern courts rarely recognize new customs

---

## Rights and Obligations

### Rights of Dominant Owner (Section 26-40)

**Right 1: Use Easement for Intended Purpose (Section 26)**
- Dominant owner can use easement for purpose granted
- Cannot exceed scope
- Cannot burden servient land beyond original grant

**Example:**
- Right of way granted for pedestrian use
- Dominant owner cannot use for vehicular traffic (excess burden)

**Right 2: Make Necessary Repairs (Section 34)**
- Dominant owner can enter servient land to repair easement
- Must cause least inconvenience
- Example: Repair pathway, clear drainage

**Right 3: Transfer with Property (Section 32)**
- Easement appurtenant (attached to land)
- Automatically transfers with sale/gift of dominant heritage
- Buyer inherits easement rights

### Duties of Dominant Owner (Section 31-33)

**Duty 1: Not Increase Burden (Section 31)**
- Cannot increase burden on servient heritage beyond original grant
- Cannot extend use to other properties
- Example: Right of way for Plot A only; cannot allow Plot C owner to use

**Duty 2: Maintain Easement (Section 33)**
- Dominant owner must maintain easement (if required by grant)
- Pay for repairs (unless grant specifies otherwise)
- Keep pathway/drainage clear

**Duty 3: Use Reasonably (Section 26)**
- Use easement in manner least burdensome to servient owner
- Not cause unnecessary damage

### Rights of Servient Owner (Section 41-45)

**Right 1: Use Servient Heritage (Section 41)**
- Servient owner can use servient land (subject to easement)
- Cannot obstruct easement
- Example: If right of way across corner, servient owner can use rest of land

**Right 2: Alter Route (Section 42)**
- If route becomes inconvenient, servient owner can provide alternative
- Alternative must be equally convenient
- Requires dominant owner's consent OR court approval

**Right 3: Resistance to Excessive Use (Section 44)**
- If dominant owner exceeds scope, servient owner can resist
- File suit for injunction

### Duties of Servient Owner (Section 41-43)

**Duty 1: Not Obstruct Easement (Section 41)**
- Cannot build/plant/excavate obstructing easement
- Must maintain status quo
- Example: Cannot build wall across right of way

**Duty 2: Not Diminish Easement (Section 43)**
- Cannot reduce light/air to dominant property
- Cannot alter condition of servient land adversely affecting easement

## Specific Types of Easements

### 1. Right of Way (Section 13, 15)

**Purpose:** Access from dominant property to public road

**Types:**

**A. Permanent Right of Way:**
- Granted by deed OR acquired by prescription
- Fixed route
- Defines width, mode of use (pedestrian/vehicular)

**B. Easement of Necessity:**
- Arises due to landlocked property
- Shortest route
- Servient owner can designate route (if reasonable)

**Width of Pathway:**
- As specified in grant
- If not specified: Reasonable width for intended use
  - Pedestrian: 3-5 feet
  - Vehicular: 8-12 feet (single lane)

**Mode of Use:**
- Pedestrian only OR
- Vehicular (two-wheeler/four-wheeler)
- Cannot upgrade without consent (e.g., pedestrian to vehicular)

**Maintenance:**
- Usually dominant owner's responsibility
- Grant may specify otherwise

**Common Disputes:**
- Servient owner building gate/wall obstructing path
- Dominant owner widening path without consent
- Vehicular use when grant was for pedestrian

**Remedies:**
- Injunction to remove obstruction
- Mandatory injunction to restore access
- Damages for obstruction

---

### 2. Right of Light and Air (Section 7, 25)

**Section 7:**
```
"Where, by the exercise of a right in or over immovable property, light or 
air is in fact withdrawn from other immovable property, the withdrawal is 
not actionable unless the right is expressly granted or acquired by 
prescription."
```

**Key Principle:**
- NO natural right to light/air
- Must be acquired by grant OR prescription (20 years)

**Acquisition by Prescription (Section 25):**
- Building enjoyed light/air for 20 years uninterruptedly
- Right becomes absolute and indefeasible
- Neighbor cannot obstruct thereafter

**Measurement:**
- Light and air measured at the plane of windows/openings
- Not entire property

**Obstruction:**
- Neighbor building structure blocking light = Actionable (if 20-year right established)
- Remedy: Injunction to demolish OR damages

**Ancient Lights Doctrine:**
- If building enjoyed light for 20+ years
- Neighbor cannot build obstructing light
- Applies to existing openings (not new windows)

**Example:**
- A's house has windows facing east (towards B's land)
- A's house standing for 25 years with windows enjoying light
- B wants to build 3-story building blocking A's light
- A can claim easement of light (20-year prescription)
- Injunction against B's construction

**Limitations:**
- Right extends to light/air actually enjoyed
- Cannot claim light/air over entire plot
- Reasonable obstruction permissible (if does not substantially reduce light)

---

### 3. Right of Water (Section 7A-7D)

**Types:**

**A. Right to Draw Water:**
- Dominant owner can draw water from well/spring on servient land
- Amount: As specified in grant OR reasonable use

**B. Right to Flow Water:**
- Discharge water (rain, waste) onto servient land
- Must follow natural flow OR grant

**C. Right to Irrigate:**
- Use water from servient owner's source for irrigation
- Quantity and timings as per grant

**Riparian Rights (Not Easement):**
- Owner of land abutting river/stream has right to use water
- Natural flow right (not easement)
- Governed by common law

**Surface Water:**
- Natural drainage flow cannot be obstructed
- Lower land must accept natural flow from higher land

**Example:**
- A's property at higher level
- Rainwater naturally drains to B's property (lower level)
- B cannot obstruct natural drainage
- A has natural right (not easement)

---

### 4. Right of Lateral Support (Section 7E)

**Definition:** Right to have one's land supported by adjoining land

**Natural Right (Not Easement):**
- Every landowner has natural right to lateral support from adjoining land
- Neighbor cannot excavate causing subsidence/collapse

**Example:**
- A excavates on his land near boundary
- A's excavation causes B's land to collapse/subside
- A liable for damages (breach of natural right)

**Artificial Support (Buildings):**
- Support to building (not land) is NOT natural right
- Must be acquired by grant or prescription

**Remedies:**
- Injunction to stop excavation
- Mandatory injunction to restore support
- Damages for collapse

---

## Termination of Easements (Section 46-55)

### Method 1: Release (Section 46)

**Voluntary Surrender:**
- Dominant owner releases easement (in writing)
- Executed release deed
- Easement extinguished

**Example:**
- A has right of way over B's land
- A finds alternative access
- A executes release deed releasing easement
- Easement terminated

---

### Method 2: Unity of Ownership (Section 47)

**Merger Doctrine:**
- When dominant and servient heritages come into same ownership
- Easement extinguished by merger

**Example:**
- Property A has right of way over Property B
- A purchases B (both properties now owned by A)
- Easement merged and extinguished

**Revival:**
- If properties later separated (sold to different owners)
- Easement does NOT automatically revive
- Must be re-created by grant or prescription

---

### Method 3: Permanent Alteration (Section 48)

**Destruction of Servient Heritage:**
- If servient property destroyed (earthquake, flood)
- Easement extinguished

**Permanent Alteration Making Easement Impossible:**
- Example: Right of way path submerged due to reservoir construction
- Easement terminates

---

### Method 4: Abandonment (Section 50)

**Non-Use + Intent to Abandon:**
- Dominant owner must:
  - Cease using easement for long period, AND
  - Demonstrate intent to abandon

**Mere Non-Use Insufficient:**
- 20-year non-use does not automatically extinguish
- Must prove abandonment intent

**Evidence of Abandonment:**
- Blocking own access to servient land
- Building structure making easement unusable
- Written statement of no further use

---

### Method 5: Limitation (Section 51)

**20-Year Rule:**
- If easement not exercised for 20 years continuously
- Easement extinguished by limitation

**Applies Only If:**
- Servient owner obstructed AND dominant owner acquiesced
- Dominant owner did not challenge obstruction for 20 years

**Example:**
- B blocks A's right of way with wall
- A does not object or file suit for 20 years
- Easement extinguished by limitation

---

### Method 6: Expiry of Purpose (Section 49)

**Easement Created for Specific Purpose:**
- When purpose achieved or becomes impossible
- Easement terminates

**Example:**
- Easement granted for construction (temporary access for construction vehicles)
- Construction completed
- Easement expires

## Common Scenarios and Solutions

### Scenario 1: Landlocked Property - Claiming Easement of Necessity

**Facts:**
- A owns Plot 1 (back portion)
- B owns Plot 2 (front portion with road access)
- Both plots were single property earlier (partitioned 5 years ago)
- A has no access to road except through B's Plot 2
- B refuses to allow access

**Legal Analysis:**

**Section 13 - Easement of Necessity:**
- Plot 1 landlocked due to partition
- No alternative access
- Easement of necessity arises automatically

**A's Rights:**
- File suit for declaration and injunction
- Court will grant easement of necessity
- Route: Shortest and least burdensome to B

**B's Position:**
- Cannot deny access (statutory easement)
- Can suggest alternative route (if equally convenient)

**Remedies:**
- Declaration of easement of necessity
- Injunction restraining B from obstructing
- Mandatory injunction to remove obstruction (if built)

---

### Scenario 2: Prescriptive Right of Way - 20-Year Use

**Facts:**
- A's property accessed via pathway through B's property
- A using pathway for 22 years continuously
- B now wants to block pathway (building wall)
- A claims prescriptive easement

**Legal Analysis:**

**Section 15 + 25 - Prescription:**
- 20-year continuous use required
- Use must be as of right (nec vi, nec clam, nec precario)

**A Must Prove:**
1. **Continuous use** for 22 years (uninterrupted)
2. **Open and peaceable** (not by force or stealth)
3. **As of right** (not by permission/license)

**B's Defense:**
- Use was permissive (license, not easement)
- Interruption for 1+ year (breaks prescription)
- A used alternate route intermittently

**Evidence:**
- Witnesses (long-time residents)
- Photographs
- Tax records, maps showing pathway

**Likely Outcome:**
- If A proves 20+ years as of right → Prescriptive easement
- B cannot obstruct
- Injunction against wall construction

---

### Scenario 3: Light and Air - Neighbor's Construction Blocking Windows

**Facts:**
- A's house built in 1995 with east-facing windows
- A enjoyed light/air through windows for 30 years
- B (neighbor) wants to build 4-story building in 2025 blocking A's windows
- A objects

**Legal Analysis:**

**Section 25 - Prescription (Light/Air):**
- A's building enjoyed light for 30 years (>20 years required)
- Absolute and indefeasible right acquired
- B cannot obstruct

**A's Remedies:**
- Injunction to restrain B's construction
- If already built: Mandatory injunction to demolish/alter

**B's Defense:**
- Right limited to light actually enjoyed (not all light)
- Reasonable obstruction permissible
- A can still receive adequate light

**Court's Approach:**
- Measure light before and after construction
- If substantial diminution → Injunction granted
- If minor reduction → May permit with damages

**Quantum of Light:**
- Courts apply "reasonable light" test
- Not absolute darkness required for injunction
- Substantial obstruction sufficient

---

### Scenario 4: Right of Way - Excessive Use (Vehicular vs Pedestrian)

**Facts:**
- A granted right of way to B in 1990 (deed specifies "pedestrian access")
- B now using path for heavy vehicles (trucks)
- Pathway damaged, A's property disturbed
- A claims excessive use

**Legal Analysis:**

**Section 31 - Not Increase Burden:**
- Easement scope defined by grant (pedestrian)
- B cannot upgrade to vehicular use
- Excessive burden on servient heritage

**A's Remedies:**
- Injunction to restrain vehicular use
- Limit use to pedestrian only
- Damages for damage caused

**B's Defense:**
- Usage evolved with needs
- Reasonable expansion
- Vehicular access necessary (no alternative)

**Court's Approach:**
- Strict interpretation of grant
- Pedestrian ≠ Vehicular
- A entitled to injunction
- B must seek permission OR use alternative route

---

### Scenario 5: Easement vs License - Neighbor's Parking Permission

**Facts:**
- A allowed B to park car in A's driveway for 2 years
- No written agreement
- A now wants to revoke permission
- B claims easement acquired

**Legal Analysis:**

**Easement vs License:**
- No grant deed (no written easement)
- Permission appears to be license (personal, revocable)
- 2 years < 20 years (prescription not satisfied)

**License Characteristics Present:**
- Personal permission to B
- No two properties (B parking in A's land)
- Revocable

**Section 60 Easements Act - License Revocable:**
- Grantor can revoke license anytime (unless coupled with interest)
- Notice required (reasonable)

**A's Rights:**
- Revoke license (give reasonable notice)
- File suit for injunction if B refuses

**B's Defense:**
- Claim prescriptive easement (fails - only 2 years)
- Claim irrevocable license (unlikely - no consideration)

**Outcome:**
- License, not easement
- A can revoke
- B must stop parking

---

### Scenario 6: Unity of Ownership - Easement Extinguished

**Facts:**
- Property A had right of way over Property B
- A purchased B in 2020 (both properties now owned by A)
- A sold B to C in 2025
- C blocks pathway claiming no easement exists

**Legal Analysis:**

**Section 47 - Unity of Ownership:**
- When A owned both properties → Easement merged and extinguished
- On sale to C → Easement did NOT automatically revive

**C's Position:**
- Easement extinguished by merger
- No easement burden on C's property

**A's Position:**
- Easement implied by quasi-easement (if existing use continued)
- Section 15 quasi-easement exception

**Quasi-Easement (Section 15):**
- If pathway continued to be used during unified ownership
- On partition/sale, quasi-easement converts to easement

**Likely Outcome:**
- If A continued using pathway during unified ownership → Quasi-easement
- C's property subject to easement (implied)
- If pathway abandoned during unified ownership → No easement

---

### Scenario 7: Drainage - Natural Flow Obstruction

**Facts:**
- A's property at higher level
- Rainwater naturally flows to B's property (lower level)
- B built wall blocking natural drainage
- Water stagnates on A's property

**Legal Analysis:**

**Natural Drainage Right (Not Easement):**
- Natural flow of surface water cannot be obstructed
- Lower land must accept natural flow from higher land
- B has no right to obstruct

**A's Remedies:**
- Injunction to remove wall
- Mandatory injunction to restore drainage
- Damages for water stagnation/damage

**B's Defense:**
- Right to build on own land
- A's drainage excessive (not natural)

**Court's Approach:**
- Natural flow protected (B cannot obstruct)
- If A's drainage artificial (from construction) → Different analysis
- B may divert, not obstruct entirely

## Integration with Other Protocols

### Property Sale Integration

**Due Diligence:**
- Verify easement burdens (encumbrance certificate)
- Check title documents for grants
- Physical inspection for pathways, encroachments

**Sale Deed Disclosure:**
- Seller must disclose easement burdens
- Buyer takes subject to existing easements
- Non-disclosure = Misrepresentation (rescission, damages)

### Registration Act Integration

**Section 17 - Easement Registration:**
- Easement for >1 year OR perpetual → Registration mandatory
- Unregistered easement: Inadmissible as evidence

**Section 27 - Priority:**
- Registered easement binds subsequent buyers
- Constructive notice to world

### Civil Procedure Integration

**Injunction Suits (Order XXXIX CPC):**
- Temporary injunction to restrain obstruction
- Mandatory injunction to remove obstruction
- Suit for declaration and permanent injunction

**Jurisdiction:**
- Where servient heritage situated (Section 16 CPC)

### Limitation Act Integration

**Article 65 - Possession Suits:**
- 12 years from accrual
- Applies to easement obstruction suits

**20-Year Prescription:**
- Section 25 Easements Act: 20 years uninterrupted use

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Assuming Natural Right to Light/Air
**Consequence:** No actionable right if not acquired by grant/prescription
**Solution:** Verify 20-year enjoyment before claiming easement of light/air

### 2. Creating Oral Easement (Immovable)
**Consequence:** Unenforceable (registration required)
**Solution:** Execute registered deed for permanent easements

### 3. Permissive Use Claimed as Prescriptive Easement
**Consequence:** License ≠ Easement; no prescription for permissive use
**Solution:** Use must be as of right (not by permission) for prescription

### 4. Ignoring Easement Burdens in Property Purchase
**Consequence:** Buyer takes subject to easements; no recourse
**Solution:** Check encumbrance certificate, physical inspection

### 5. Blocking Easement Without Legal Action
**Consequence:** Possible contempt, damages
**Solution:** File suit for termination/modification; don't self-help

### 6. Exceeding Scope of Easement
**Consequence:** Dominant owner liable; injunction, damages
**Solution:** Adhere to grant terms; seek variation if needed

### 7. Not Maintaining Easement (Dominant Owner)
**Consequence:** Damage to servient land; liability
**Solution:** Regular maintenance as per grant terms

### 8. Claiming Easement Against Government Land (Prescription)
**Consequence:** Cannot acquire prescriptive easement against Government
**Solution:** Seek formal grant/permission

### 9. Relying on Unity of Ownership to Extinguish Easement
**Consequence:** Quasi-easement may revive on re-partition
**Solution:** Execute release deed if intend to extinguish permanently

### 10. Not Documenting Easement Alteration
**Consequence:** Disputes over changed route/terms
**Solution:** Execute variation deed; register modifications

# Indian Property Law: Gift of Immovable Property

## Purpose
This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on gift of immovable property under the Transfer of Property Act 1882 (Sections 122-129). It covers essential elements of valid gift, registration requirements, delivery/acceptance, revocation principles, onerous gifts, universal donee, suspended/conditional gifts, Hindu law gift concepts (Hiba, musha, musha-bil-iwaz), and practical considerations for gift transactions.

## Activation Triggers
Use this protocol when:
- Client wants to gift property to family member/third party
- Analyzing validity of gift deed
- Challenge to gift deed (by donor's legal heirs, creditors)
- Revocation of gift (by donor or court)
- Onerous gift acceptance/rejection
- Conditional gift (condition precedent/subsequent)
- Universal donee determination
- Gift vs Will distinction
- Gift vs Sale determination (inadequate consideration)
- Muslim law Hiba (gift) under TPA framework
- Tax planning through gifts (avoiding capital gains)
- Gift to minor/person of unsound mind

## Core Principles

### 1. Section 122: Gift Defined

**"Gift is the transfer of certain existing movable or immovable property made voluntarily and without consideration, by one person (donor), to another (donee), and accepted by or on behalf of the donee."**

Essential Elements of Gift:
1. **TRANSFER** - Of existing property (not future property)
2. **IMMOVABLE OR MOVABLE** - Any property (land, building, goods, shares)
3. **VOLUNTARY** - Free will (no coercion, fraud, undue influence)
4. **WITHOUT CONSIDERATION** - Gratuitous (if consideration present → Sale)
5. **DONOR** - Transferor (owner of property)
6. **DONEE** - Transferee (recipient)
7. **ACCEPTANCE** - By or on behalf of donee
8. **EXISTING PROPERTY** - Present property (not spes successionis - expectancy)

**Key Distinctions:**

| Aspect | Gift | Sale | Will |
|--------|------|------|------|
| **Consideration** | NO consideration (gratuitous) | Price paid | NO consideration |
| **Transfer** | Inter vivos (during lifetime) | Inter vivos | Testamentary (after death) |
| **Acceptance** | Required during donor's lifetime | Implied by payment | NO acceptance (testator dead) |
| **Revocable** | Generally NO (exceptions) | NO | YES (anytime before death) |
| **Registration** | MANDATORY (immovable) | MANDATORY (immovable Rs. 100+) | Not registered (probate/succession) |
| **Delivery** | Required | Required | NA (possession after death) |
| **Time of Transfer** | Immediately on registration | Immediately on registration | After death and probate |

### 2. Section 123: Registration Requirement

**Section 123 TPA + Section 17 Registration Act:**

**Immovable Property:**
```
"For the purpose of making a gift of immovable property, the transfer must 
be effected by a REGISTERED INSTRUMENT signed by or on behalf of the donor, 
and attested by at least TWO WITNESSES."
```

**Registration is MANDATORY** for gift of immovable property (NO exception for value threshold)

**Effect of Non-Registration:**
- Gift deed VOID (Section 49 Registration Act)
- No transfer of ownership
- Donee gets NO rights
- Donor remains owner

**Movable Property:**
- Registration NOT required
- Delivery of possession sufficient (Section 123)

### 3. Essential Requirements for Valid Gift

**Requirement 1: Existing Property Only (Section 124)**

```
Section 124: "The gift of a thing not in existence at the date of the gift is VOID."
```

**Valid:**
- Gift of house (exists)
- Gift of land (exists)
- Gift of specific crops standing on land (exists)

**VOID:**
- Gift of future crops (not yet grown)
- Gift of property to be acquired in future
- Gift of expectancy of succession (spes successionis)

**Requirement 2: Voluntarily Made (Free Consent)**

Gift must be made:
- Without coercion (Section 15 Contract Act)
- Without fraud (Section 17 Contract Act)
- Without undue influence (Section 16 Contract Act)
- Without misrepresentation (Section 18 Contract Act)

If consent vitiated:
- Gift VOIDABLE at donor's option (Section 126)
- Can be rescinded

**Requirement 3: Acceptance by Donee (Section 122)**

**Acceptance Required:**
- During donor's lifetime
- Before or at time of execution (contemporaneous)
- Can be express OR implied

**Express Acceptance:**
- Donee signs gift deed (accepts transfer)
- Common practice: Donee as party to deed

**Implied Acceptance:**
- Donee takes possession
- Donee acts as owner (pays taxes, makes alterations)
- Presumed if deed registered and possession delivered

**Rejection:**
- If donee rejects, no transfer
- Must reject during donor's lifetime
- After acceptance, cannot reject (unless onerous gift)

**Acceptance by Guardian (Donee Minor/Unsound Mind):**
- Guardian can accept on behalf of minor/incapacitated person
- Section 122 proviso: Natural guardian OR court-appointed guardian

**Requirement 4: Transfer Must Be Effected (Delivery)**

**Immovable Property:**
- Registration of gift deed (Section 123)
- Delivery of possession (symbolic or actual)

**Symbolic Delivery:**
- Handing over keys
- Handing over title documents
- Attornment (acknowledgment by occupier)

**Actual Delivery:**
- Physical handover of property
- Donee takes possession

**Registration + Possession:**
- Both required for complete transfer
- Registration alone insufficient (if possession not delivered)
- Possession alone insufficient (if gift not registered)

### 4. Section 126: Gifts May Be Suspended or Revoked

**General Rule: Gift is IRREVOCABLE**

Once gift validly made and accepted:
- Donor CANNOT revoke unilaterally
- Gift cannot be taken back
- Donee becomes absolute owner

**Exceptions (Gift Can Be Revoked/Set Aside):**

**Exception 1: Consent Vitiated (Section 126(a))**
- Coercion, fraud, misrepresentation, undue influence
- Donor can rescind gift
- Must act within reasonable time

**Exception 2: Condition Precedent Fails (Section 126(b))**
- Gift conditional on future event
- Event fails to occur
- Gift fails

Example: "I gift this house to A if he marries within 1 year" - If A does not marry within 1 year, gift fails.

**Exception 3: Donee Predeceases Donor (During Donor's Lifetime)**
- If donee dies before acceptance
- Gift fails (no one to accept)

**Exception 4: Mutual Agreement**
- Donor and donee mutually agree to rescind
- Execute deed of rescission
- Retransfer to donor

**Exception 5: Court Order**
- Creditors' suit to set aside gift (fraudulent transfer)
- Gift to defraud creditors → Can be set aside

**Exception 6: Onerous Gift Rejected (Section 127)**
- Donee rejects onerous gift
- Gift fails

**Section 126 Does NOT Allow:**
- Revocation by donor alone (without donee's consent)
- Revocation for donor's changed circumstances
- Revocation for donee's ingratitude/misconduct

**Muslim Law Exception: Hiba (Gift) Revocability**
- Under Muslim Personal Law, gift (Hiba) by parent to child revocable before delivery
- After delivery: Generally irrevocable (exceptions for parent-child)

## Framework

### Phase 1: Pre-Gift Planning

**Step 1: Donor's Objectives**

Understand purpose:
- Tax planning (avoid capital gains tax)
- Estate planning (distribute property during lifetime)
- Family settlement
- Religious/charitable donation
- Love and affection

**Step 2: Property Valuation**

For immovable property:
- Obtain valuation report
- Determine stamp duty (based on market value or circle rate, whichever higher)

**Step 3: Tax Implications**

**For Donor:**
- **NO Capital Gains Tax** - Gift is not "transfer" for consideration (Section 47 Income Tax Act)
- Exemption from capital gains (even if property appreciated)

**For Donee:**
- **NO Gift Tax** - Gift Tax abolished in India (post-1998)
- But if gift from non-relative → Income taxable under Section 56(2)(x) Income Tax Act
- Exemption: Gifts from relatives (defined list) exempt

**Relatives Defined (Section 56(2)(x)):**
- Spouse
- Brother/sister
- Brother/sister of spouse
- Brother/sister of parents
- Lineal ascendant/descendant of individual or spouse
- Spouse of lineal ascendant/descendant

**If Donee is Non-Relative:**
- Gift of immovable property > Rs. 50,000 → Taxable in donee's hands
- Tax = Fair market value of property as "Income from Other Sources"

**Example:**
- Father gifts house worth Rs. 1 crore to son → NO tax (relative)
- Friend gifts house worth Rs. 1 crore to friend → Rs. 1 crore taxable income for donee

**Step 4: Legal Capacity Check**

**Donor Must Be:**
1. **Competent to Contract** (Section 11 Contract Act)
   - Major (18 years)
   - Sound mind
   - Not disqualified by law

2. **Entitled to Transfer Property** (Section 7 TPA)
   - Owner (sole or co-owner)
   - Authorized agent/guardian/trustee

**Who CANNOT Gift:**
- Minor (gift by minor VOID - Mohori Bibee principle)
- Person of unsound mind
- Person not entitled (non-owner, trespasser)

**Donee Can Be:**
- Any person (including minor)
- Company, trust, society (juristic persons)

### Phase 2: Drafting Gift Deed

**Mandatory Contents:**

**1. TITLE**
   - "GIFT DEED" or "DEED OF GIFT"

**2. DATE AND PLACE**
   - Date of execution
   - Place of execution

**3. PARTIES**
   - **Donor** (Transferor): Full name, age, address, occupation
   - **Donee** (Transferee): Full name, age, address, occupation
   - If donee minor: Guardian's details

**4. RECITALS**
   - Donor's ownership and title
   - Relationship with donee (if any)
   - Motive for gift (love and affection, natural love, consideration of marriage, etc.)

**5. PROPERTY DESCRIPTION (Schedule)**
   - Full address
   - Survey numbers, plot numbers
   - Boundaries (North, South, East, West)
   - Area (sq. ft. / sq. meters / acres)
   - Built-up area (if building)

**6. CONSIDERATION CLAUSE**
   - "Out of natural love and affection"
   - "Without any monetary consideration"
   - Emphasize gratuitous nature

**7. TRANSFER CLAUSE**
   - "Hereby transfer, grant, gift, convey all my right, title, interest, and ownership in the said property described in the Schedule to the Donee absolutely and forever"

**8. DELIVERY OF POSSESSION**
   - "Delivered possession of the said property to the Donee"
   - Donee acknowledges receipt

**9. ACCEPTANCE CLAUSE**
   - "The Donee hereby accepts the gift"
   - Donee's signature

**10. COVENANTS**
   - Donor declares:
     - Absolute owner
     - No encumbrances (mortgage, charge, litigation)
     - Right to gift
     - Property free from liabilities

**11. ATTESTATION**
   - Two witnesses
   - Full name, address, signature

### Phase 3: Stamp Duty and Registration

**Step 1: Stamp Duty Calculation**

**Stamp Duty on Gift Deed:**
- Similar to sale deed (state-specific)
- Based on market value or circle rate/guidance value (whichever higher)
- Rate: 3-8% (varies by state)

**Example (Maharashtra):**
- Property value: Rs. 50 lakhs
- Stamp duty: 5% (for gift to non-relative) OR 3% (for gift to relative)
- Gift to son (relative): Rs. 50 lakhs × 3% = Rs. 1.5 lakhs

**Concessional Stamp Duty (Many States):**
- Gift to blood relative: Reduced rate (2-3%)
- Gift to non-relative: Regular sale deed rate (5-7%)

**Step 2: Registration Procedure**

**Documents Required:**

**Donor Must Produce:**
1. Gift deed (original + 2 copies)
2. Identity proof (Aadhaar, PAN, passport)
3. Address proof
4. Title documents (sale deed, previous chain)
5. Encumbrance certificate (15 years)
6. Tax receipts (property tax paid)
7. NOC (if applicable - society, bank)

**Donee Must Produce:**
1. Identity proof (Aadhaar, PAN, passport)
2. Address proof
3. PAN card (mandatory for registration)

**Registration Steps:**

1. **Book Appointment** - Online slot at Sub-Registrar office
2. **Pay Stamp Duty** - E-stamping OR stamp paper
3. **Appear in Person** - Donor, donee, 2 witnesses
4. **Document Verification** - Sub-Registrar checks documents
5. **Signature/Thumb Impression** - Parties execute deed
6. **Pay Registration Fee** - 1% (approx) of property value
7. **Registration Complete** - Deed entered in register
8. **Collect Certified Copies** - Registered gift deed (1-7 days)

**Limitation for Registration:**
- Section 25 Registration Act: Within 4 MONTHS of execution
- Extension: Additional 4 months (with penalty)
- After 8 months: Registration refused

**Effect of Registration:**
- Transfer of ownership from donor to donee
- Donee becomes absolute owner
- Gift deed admissible as evidence
- Notice to world

### Phase 4: Post-Registration

**Step 1: Delivery of Possession**

Even after registration, deliver possession:
- Physical handover
- Hand over keys, documents
- Donee takes actual possession

**Step 2: Mutation of Records**

Update revenue records:
1. **Revenue Department** - Mutation in land records (7/12, RTC, property card)
2. **Municipal Corporation** - Property tax records
3. **Society** - Membership transfer (if flat)
4. **Utility Providers** - Electricity, water connection transfer

**Step 3: Update PAN/Aadhaar**

Donee updates:
- PAN database (property ownership)
- Aadhaar address (if shifting residence)

## Special Gift Concepts

### 1. Onerous Gift (Section 127)

**Definition:** Gift with attached burden/obligation

**Section 127 TPA:**
```
"If a gift is in the form of a single transfer to the same person of several things 
of which one is, and the others are not, burdened by an obligation, the donee can 
take nothing by the gift unless he accepts it fully."
```

**Example 1:**
- A gifts house + car to B
- House has mortgage debt of Rs. 10 lakhs
- B must accept BOTH (house with debt + car) OR reject BOTH
- Cannot cherry-pick (take car, reject house)

**Example 2:**
- A gifts property worth Rs. 1 crore to B
- Condition: B must pay Rs. 20 lakhs to A's creditor
- B can:
  - Accept fully (take property + pay Rs. 20 lakhs), OR
  - Reject fully (refuse gift)

**All or Nothing Principle:**
- Donee cannot accept beneficial part and reject onerous part
- Must accept entire gift or reject entirely

**Election by Donee:**
- Donee must elect within reasonable time
- If accepts part, deemed to accept all
- If rejects part, deemed to reject all

### 2. Universal Donee (Section 128)

**Section 128:**
```
"Where a gift consists of the donor's whole property, the donee is personally liable 
for all the debts and liabilities of the donor at the time of the gift to the extent 
of the property comprised therein."
```

**Universal Donee = Donee of ENTIRE property**

**Principle:**
- Donor gifts ALL property to donee
- Donee liable for donor's debts (to extent of property value)
- Creditors can sue donee

**Example:**
- A owns property worth Rs. 1 crore (total assets)
- A has debts of Rs. 30 lakhs
- A gifts entire property to son B
- B liable for Rs. 30 lakhs debts (as universal donee)
- Creditors can claim from property

**Limitation:**
- Liability limited to value of property gifted
- Donee not personally liable beyond property value

**Distinction from Onerous Gift:**
- Onerous gift: Specific burden attached to specific property
- Universal donee: General liability for all debts

### 3. Conditional and Contingent Gifts

**A. Condition Precedent**

**Definition:** Gift takes effect ONLY if condition fulfilled

**Example:**
- "I gift this house to A if he completes MBA degree"
- If A completes MBA → Gift takes effect
- If A does not complete MBA → No gift

**Legal Position:**
- Condition must occur before gift takes effect
- If condition fails → Gift fails (reverts to donor/estate)

**B. Condition Subsequent**

**Definition:** Gift already effective, but can be defeated if condition occurs

**Example:**
- "I gift this house to A, but if he sells it within 10 years, gift revoked"
- Gift effective immediately
- But revoked if A sells within 10 years

**Void Conditions (Section 26 Contract Act):**
- Restraint of marriage (void)
- Restraint of trade (void, with exceptions)
- Restraint of legal proceedings (void)
- Uncertain/impossible conditions (void)

**Example (Void):**
- "I gift house to A on condition he never marries" → Condition VOID (restraint of marriage)
- Gift takes effect without condition (A gets house, can marry)

**C. Contingent Gift (Section 124)**

**Definition:** Gift depends on uncertain future event

**Example:**
- "I gift house to A if X dies during my lifetime"
- X's death is uncertain event

**Validity:**
- If contingency clear and possible → Valid
- If contingency impossible → Gift void

### 4. Gift to Unborn Person (Section 13 TPA)

**General Rule: VOID**

Section 13: Transfer to unborn person is VOID (subject to exceptions)

**Exception (Rule of Perpetuity):**
- Transfer for benefit of unborn person valid IF:
  - Transfer is to living person FIRST, and
  - On death of living person, to unborn person
  - Unborn person must come into existence within LIFE INTEREST + 18 years

**Example (Valid):**
- "I gift house to my wife A for her life, and after her death to my grandson B (not yet born)"
- If B born within A's lifetime → Valid
- If B not born by A's death → Gift fails

**Example (Void):**
- "I gift house to my grandson B (not yet born)" - Direct gift to unborn → VOID

### 5. Gift Inter Vivos vs Testamentary Transfer

**Gift Inter Vivos (During Lifetime):**
- Transfer during donor's lifetime
- Acceptance during donor's lifetime
- Irrevocable (once accepted)
- Registered gift deed
- Ownership passes immediately

**Will (Testamentary):**
- Takes effect after testator's death
- No acceptance required (testator dead)
- Revocable anytime before death
- Not registered (probate after death)
- Ownership passes after death + probate

**Distinguishing Test:**
- If transfer intended to take effect during transferor's lifetime → Gift
- If transfer intended to take effect after transferor's death → Will

**Problem:** Donatio Mortis Causa (Gift in Contemplation of Death)
- Gift made in contemplation of imminent death
- Takes effect immediately BUT revocable if donor survives
- Indian law: Treated as gift if registration done (irrevocable)

## Muslim Law Gift Concepts (Hiba)

**Hiba (Gift under Muslim Personal Law):**

Muslim law gift (Hiba) governed by Muslim Personal Law, but TPA applies if:
- Property situated in areas where TPA applicable
- Registration required (Section 123 TPA)

**Essential Elements of Hiba:**

1. **Declaration (Ijab)** - Donor's offer to gift
2. **Acceptance (Qubul)** - Donee's acceptance
3. **Delivery of Possession** - Actual or constructive

**Distinction from TPA Gift:**
- TPA: Registration mandatory (immovable)
- Hiba: Delivery sufficient (traditionally), but registration required by statute

**Types of Hiba:**

**A. Hiba-bil-Iwaz (Gift for Consideration)**
- Gift in exchange for something
- Similar to sale (but called gift)
- Irrevocable

**B. Hiba-ba-Shartul-Iwaz (Gift with Stipulation for Return)**
- Gift with condition of return gift
- If return gift given → Both irrevocable
- If return gift not given → Original gift revocable

**Revocability of Hiba:**

**General Rule:** Hiba irrevocable after delivery

**Exception:** Revocable in following cases:
1. **Parent to Child** - Parent can revoke gift to child (before delivery or after if possession not taken)
2. **Gift Not Delivered** - If possession not delivered, revocable
3. **Gift for Consideration** - Hiba-bil-Iwaz irrevocable

**Musha (Gift of Undivided Share):**
- Gift of undivided share in property
- Valid under Muslim law
- Delivery: Symbolic (joint possession continues)

## Common Scenarios and Solutions

### Scenario 1: Father Gifts Property to Son, Then Changes Mind

**Facts:**
- Father executed registered gift deed gifting house to son
- Possession delivered
- Son living in house for 2 years
- Father now wants property back (financial difficulty)

**Legal Position:**
- Gift validly made (registered + accepted + possession delivered)
- Gift IRREVOCABLE (no exception applies)
- Father cannot unilaterally revoke

**Father's Options:**

**Option 1: Request Son to Re-Gift**
- Request son to voluntarily re-gift property
- Son executes gift deed gifting back to father
- Entirely voluntary (son can refuse)

**Option 2: Purchase Back**
- Father purchases property from son (sale transaction)
- Son agrees to sell for consideration
- Tax implications (capital gains for son)

**Option 3: Court Relief (Difficult)**
- Challenge gift on grounds of coercion/undue influence/fraud
- Burden of proof on father (heavy)
- Unlikely to succeed (2 years passed, possession delivered)

**Son's Position:**
- Absolute owner (gift validly completed)
- No legal obligation to return
- Can refuse father's request

---

### Scenario 2: Gift Deed Executed But Possession Not Delivered

**Facts:**
- Mother executed registered gift deed gifting flat to daughter
- Gift deed registered 1 year ago
- But mother still living in flat (possession not delivered)
- Mother now wants to revoke gift

**Legal Position:**

**Analysis:**
- Gift deed registered (Section 123 satisfied)
- But possession NOT delivered (incomplete transfer)

**Two Views:**

**View 1 (Strict):** Gift incomplete until possession delivered
- Registration alone insufficient
- Donor can revoke before delivery

**View 2 (Liberal):** Registration sufficient (possession formality)
- Once registered, ownership transferred
- Donor's continued possession as licensee/permissive occupier

**Courts Generally Follow View 2:**
- Registration transfers ownership
- Possession delivery is formality (can be enforced)
- Daughter can file suit for possession

**Mother's Position:**
- Difficult to revoke (gift deed registered)
- Can argue non-delivery = incomplete gift
- Better to seek daughter's consent for rescission

**Daughter's Position:**
- File suit for possession (specific performance)
- Compel mother to deliver possession
- Claim ownership based on registered deed

---

### Scenario 3: Gift to Non-Relative vs Relative (Tax Implication)

**Facts:**
- Uncle gifts property worth Rs. 80 lakhs to nephew
- Question: Is nephew taxable?

**Legal Analysis (Income Tax Act Section 56(2)(x)):**

**Nephew as Relative:**
Check if nephew falls within "relative" definition:
- Section 56(2)(x): Nephew NOT in list of relatives
- Therefore, nephew is NON-RELATIVE for IT Act purpose

**Tax Implication:**
- Gift of immovable property > Rs. 50,000 from non-relative → Taxable
- Nephew must pay income tax on Rs. 80 lakhs as "Income from Other Sources"
- Tax rate: As per nephew's income tax slab (say 30% + cess = Rs. 24 lakhs approx)

**Solution:**
- Sale at circle rate/fair market value (instead of gift)
- Uncle pays capital gains tax (may have exemptions - Section 54, 54F)
- Nephew no gift tax (consideration paid)

**If Nephew Were Son:**
- Son is "lineal descendant" → Relative
- Gift exempt from tax in son's hands (NO tax)

---

### Scenario 4: Gift to Minor

**Facts:**
- Grandfather gifts property to grandson (8 years old)
- Grandfather executes gift deed
- Father (natural guardian) accepts on behalf of grandson

**Legal Position:**

**Validity:**
- Minor can be donee (no restriction)
- Acceptance by natural guardian valid (Section 122 proviso)
- Registration in minor's name with guardian details

**Gift Deed Contents:**
- Donee: Minor grandson (through natural guardian father)
- Father accepts gift on behalf of minor
- Property vests in minor (ownership)
- Father manages as guardian (no ownership)

**Tax Implication:**
- Gift from grandfather (lineal ascendant) to grandson (lineal descendant) → Relative
- NO tax in minor's hands

**Clubbing Provision (Section 64(1A) IT Act):**
- Income from gifted property clubbed with parent's income
- Until minor attains majority (18 years)
- Example: Rental income from gifted house taxed in father's hands

**After Majority:**
- Grandson becomes absolute owner
- Can manage property independently
- Income taxed in grandson's hands

### Scenario 5: Gift Challenged by Creditors

**Facts:**
- A has debts of Rs. 50 lakhs to Bank
- A gifts property worth Rs. 1 crore to son (to avoid creditors)
- Bank challenges gift as fraudulent transfer

**Legal Provisions:**

**Section 53 TPA: Fraudulent Transfer**
```
"Every transfer of immovable property made with intent to defeat or delay creditors 
shall be voidable at the option of any creditor so defeated or delayed."
```

**Essential Elements:**
1. Transfer of property
2. Intent to defeat/delay creditors
3. Creditor defeated or delayed

**Bank's Remedy:**
- File suit to set aside gift
- Prove fraudulent intent
- If succeeded: Gift declared void, property available for creditors

**A's Defense:**
- Gift made out of natural love and affection (no fraudulent intent)
- Debts can be paid from other assets
- Gift not sole asset transfer

**Court's Approach:**
- Examine circumstances:
  - Was A insolvent at time of gift?
  - Did gift render A unable to pay debts?
  - Was gift to close relative (son) suspicious?
  - Temporal proximity (gift immediately before creditor's claim?)

**Likely Outcome:**
- If A has no other assets + gift rendered him insolvent → Gift likely voidable
- If A has sufficient other assets to pay debts → Gift may be upheld

**Universal Donee Principle (Section 128):**
- If gift of ENTIRE property → Son liable for debts (up to property value)
- Bank can claim from son as universal donee

### Scenario 6: Gift vs Sale (Inadequate Consideration)

**Facts:**
- A "sells" property worth Rs. 1 crore to daughter for Rs. 10 lakhs
- Transaction documented as "sale deed"
- Revenue authorities claim it's gift (stamp duty difference)

**Distinction:**

**Sale:** Transfer for consideration (price)
**Gift:** Transfer without consideration

**Test:**
- Is consideration nominal (token) OR genuine price?

**Factors Indicating Gift:**
- Grossly inadequate consideration (Rs. 10 lakhs vs Rs. 1 crore)
- Transferee is close relative (daughter)
- Love and affection expressed

**Factors Indicating Sale:**
- Genuine negotiations
- Market-driven price
- Commercial transaction

**Legal Position:**
- If consideration nominal + close relative → Courts may treat as gift
- If genuine price (even if low) → Sale

**Practical Implication:**
- Gift: Higher stamp duty (5-7% on full value)
- Sale: Stamp duty on consideration (if consideration < circle rate, on circle rate)

**Tax Implication:**
- Gift: No capital gains for donor, gift tax risk for donee (if non-relative)
- Sale: Capital gains tax for seller

**Recommendation:**
- If intent is gift → Execute as gift deed
- If genuine sale → Show fair consideration

---

### Scenario 7: Conditional Gift - Condition Fails

**Facts:**
- Father gifts property to son on condition: "Son shall not sell property during father's lifetime"
- After 5 years, son sells property
- Father claims gift revoked

**Legal Analysis:**

**Nature of Condition:**
- Condition subsequent (gift already effective, but defeated if condition breached)
- Restraint on alienation

**Validity of Condition:**
- Absolute restraint on alienation = VOID (Section 10 TPA)
- Qualified restraint (during father's lifetime) = Valid? (debatable)

**Section 10 TPA:**
```
"Where property is transferred subject to a condition absolutely restraining the 
transferee from parting with or disposing of his interest, the condition is VOID."
```

**Court's Approach:**
- Absolute restraint (forever) → VOID
- Temporary restraint (limited period/purpose) → May be valid

**In This Case:**
- Restraint during father's lifetime = Reasonable period
- Courts may uphold condition as valid

**Effect of Breach:**
- If condition valid + breached → Gift may revert to father
- Father can file suit to revoke gift and recover property

**Son's Defense:**
- Condition void (restraint on alienation)
- Gift absolute and unconditional

**Likely Outcome:**
- Condition may be held valid (limited restraint)
- Son's sale in breach → Father may recover property

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Not Registering Gift Deed (Immovable Property)
**Consequence:** Gift VOID; no transfer of ownership
**Solution:** ALWAYS register gift deed for immovable property (Section 123)

### 2. Gift Without Donee's Acceptance
**Consequence:** No valid gift (acceptance required)
**Solution:** Obtain express acceptance (donee signs deed) OR implied acceptance (delivery of possession)

### 3. Gift of Future Property
**Consequence:** Gift VOID (Section 124)
**Solution:** Gift only existing property; cannot gift property to be acquired in future

### 4. Not Delivering Possession After Registration
**Consequence:** Incomplete transfer; donor may argue gift not completed
**Solution:** Deliver possession immediately after registration (symbolic or actual)

### 5. Gifting to Defraud Creditors
**Consequence:** Gift voidable (Section 53 TPA); creditors can set aside
**Solution:** Clear all debts before gifting; or gift only surplus property

### 6. Ignoring Tax Implications (Non-Relative Donee)
**Consequence:** Donee taxable on gift value (Section 56(2)(x))
**Solution:** Check if donee is "relative"; if not, consider sale instead

### 7. Onerous Gift Without Clear Acceptance
**Consequence:** Donee may reject onerous part; gift fails
**Solution:** Clearly disclose burden; obtain donee's written acceptance of entire gift

### 8. Gift Deed Without Two Witnesses
**Consequence:** Unattested deed may be challenged; registration may be refused
**Solution:** Ensure two witnesses attest gift deed (Section 123)

### 9. Not Updating Mutation After Gift
**Consequence:** Revenue records show donor as owner; title disputes
**Solution:** Update mutation in revenue records, municipal tax records, society records

### 10. Expecting Revocability (General Gifts)
**Consequence:** Cannot revoke validly made gift (except specific exceptions)
**Solution:** Understand gift is irrevocable; consider will if want revocability

# Indian Property Law: Lease of Immovable Property

## Purpose
This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on lease of immovable property under the Transfer of Property Act 1882 (Sections 105-117) and state-specific Rent Control Acts. It covers essential elements of lease, rights and duties of lessor and lessee, types of leases (fixed term, periodic, perpetual), determination/termination of lease, forfeiture, ejectment procedures, subletting, assignment, fixtures, and rent control legislation implications.

## Activation Triggers
Use this protocol when:
- Client wants to lease property (as lessor or lessee)
- Drafting lease agreement/rent agreement
- Dispute over lease terms, rent payment, maintenance
- Termination/eviction issues
- Subletting/assignment disputes
- Registration requirements for lease deed
- Forfeiture for breach of covenant
- Fixtures and improvements ownership issues
- Rent control legislation applicability
- Distinction between lease and license
- Leave and license vs lease determination
- Ejectment suit by landlord
- Redemption/continuation of lease

## Core Principles

### 1. Section 105: Lease Defined

**"Lease of immovable property is a transfer of a right to enjoy such property, made for a certain time, express or implied, or in perpetuity, in consideration of a price paid or promised, or of money, a share of crops, service, or any other thing of value, to be rendered periodically or on specified occasions to the transferor by the transferee, who accepts the transfer on such terms."**

Essential Elements of Lease:
1. **TRANSFER** - Of right to enjoy (not ownership)
2. **IMMOVABLE PROPERTY** - Land, buildings, rights in land
3. **CERTAIN TIME** - Fixed period OR perpetual OR periodic
4. **CONSIDERATION** - Rent/premium/share of crops/service
5. **PERIODIC OR SPECIFIED** - Payment at intervals OR specified occasions
6. **TWO PARTIES** - Lessor (landlord/owner) and Lessee (tenant)
7. **ACCEPTANCE** - Lessee accepts transfer

**Key Distinctions:**

| Aspect | Lease | License | Sale |
|--------|-------|---------|------|
| **Transfer** | Right to enjoy | Personal permission | Ownership |
| **Interest Created** | Proprietary (in rem) | Personal (in personam) | Absolute ownership |
| **Transferability** | Assignable (unless restricted) | Not assignable | Permanent |
| **Duration** | Fixed/Perpetual | Revocable | Permanent |
| **Possession** | Transferred | Not transferred (mere use) | Transferred |
| **Registration** | Required (>12 months) | Not required | Required (Rs. 100+) |

### 2. Section 106: Duration of Lease

**A. Fixed-Term Lease:**
- Specific period stated (1 year, 5 years, 10 years, 99 years)
- Terminates automatically on expiry (unless renewed)
- No notice required for termination

**B. Periodic Lease:**
- Month-to-month, year-to-year
- Continues automatically until notice given
- Notice period: Equal to period of tenancy (1 month for monthly, 6 months for yearly)

**C. Perpetual Lease:**
- In perpetuity (no fixed end date)
- Rare in modern practice
- Terminable only by forfeiture or agreement

**Section 106 Notice Requirements (Periodic Tenancy):**

For Month-to-Month Tenancy:
- Notice: **15 days** minimum
- Expiring with end of month of tenancy

For Year-to-Year Tenancy:
- Notice: **6 months** minimum
- Expiring with end of year of tenancy

Example:
- Monthly rent payable on 1st of each month
- Lessor wants to terminate
- Notice: 15 days before end of month
- If notice given on 10th January, earliest termination: 28th/29th February

### 3. Registration Requirements (Section 107)

**Section 107 TPA + Section 17 Registration Act:**

**Registration MANDATORY if:**
1. Lease period > 12 months, OR
2. Lease reserves rent AND period > 12 months

**Registration NOT Required if:**
1. Lease period ≤ 12 months, AND
2. No future rent reserved (lump sum payment)

**Examples:**

| Lease Terms | Registration Required? |
|-------------|------------------------|
| 1 year lease, monthly rent Rs. 10,000 | NO (≤12 months) |
| 2 year lease, monthly rent Rs. 10,000 | YES (>12 months) |
| 11 month lease, monthly rent Rs. 10,000 | NO (≤12 months) |
| 5 year lease, one-time payment Rs. 5 lakhs | YES (>12 months) |
| 6 month lease, renewable | NO (initial period ≤12 months) |

**11-Month Lease Practice:**
- Common to avoid registration
- Lease for 11 months + option to renew
- Each renewal is fresh 11-month lease
- Legal validity upheld by courts

**Effect of Non-Registration (If Required):**
- Lease deed inadmissible as evidence (Section 49 Registration Act)
- But oral lease/possession may create periodic tenancy
- Lessee's possession not illegal (becomes tenant at will/sufferance)

### 4. Lease vs License (Easement Act 1882 Section 52)

**Critical Distinction:**

**License (Section 52 Easement Act):**
"Where one person grants to another, or to a definite number of other persons, a right to do, or continue to do, in or upon the immovable property of the grantor, something which would, in the absence of such right, be unlawful, and such right does not amount to an easement or an interest in the property, the right is called a license."

**Test (Authoritative):**

Associated Hotels of India Ltd. v. R.N. Kapoor (SC 1959):
"The test is: Does the document create an interest in the property? If YES → LEASE; If NO → LICENSE"

**Factors Indicating LEASE:**
1. Exclusive possession to transferee
2. Fixed term/duration specified
3. Rent reserved and payable periodically
4. Transferable interest
5. Restriction on lessor's entry
6. Renewal clause
7. Lessee can exclude others (including lessor)

**Factors Indicating LICENSE:**
1. Personal permission (no exclusive possession)
2. Revocable at will
3. No interest in property created
4. Licensor retains control
5. Not transferable/assignable
6. Licensor can enter anytime
7. No fixed term (or term with easy revocation)

**"Leave and License" Agreements:**
- Common in Maharashtra (Bombay Rent Act)
- Intended as license to avoid rent control
- Court examines substance over form
- If creates exclusive possession + fixed term + rent → LEASE (despite "license" label)

**Practical Tip:**
Many agreements labeled "Leave and License" are actually LEASES - courts look beyond nomenclature to actual rights transferred.

## Framework

### Phase 1: Creating Lease

**Step 1: Pre-Lease Verification (Lessee's Due Diligence)**

**A. Lessor's Title Verification:**
- Examine lessor's sale deed, previous chain
- Verify lessor is OWNER or authorized to lease
- Check for co-owners (all must consent if ownership joint)
- Verify no mortgage/charge prohibiting lease

**B. Property Status Check:**
- Physical inspection (boundaries, condition, encroachment)
- Municipal tax status (arrears, if any)
- Encumbrance certificate (no litigation/charges)
- Building approval (if construction)
- Occupancy certificate

**C. Rent Control Applicability:**
- Check if property falls under Rent Control Act (state-specific)
- If covered: Restrictions on rent increase, eviction grounds limited
- If exempt: Freedom to contract

**Step 2: Negotiate Lease Terms**

**Key Commercial Terms:**

1. **RENT**
   - Monthly/quarterly/annual amount
   - Payment mode (cash, cheque, online transfer)
   - Due date (1st of month, advance/arrears)
   - Late payment penalty/interest

2. **SECURITY DEPOSIT**
   - Amount (usually 2-10 months' rent)
   - Refundable on termination
   - Interest on deposit (if agreed)
   - Adjustment against last months' rent (if permitted)

3. **DURATION**
   - Fixed term (11 months, 1 year, 3 years, 5 years, etc.)
   - Start date and end date
   - Lock-in period (cannot terminate before specified period)

4. **RENEWAL**
   - Option to renew (how many times)
   - Rent escalation on renewal (% increase)
   - Notice period for renewal (30-60 days before expiry)

5. **MAINTENANCE**
   - Who bears maintenance cost (lessor/lessee)
   - Major repairs vs minor repairs (responsibility split)
   - Property tax, water/electricity charges (who pays)

6. **USE**
   - Permitted use (residential, commercial, industrial)
   - Restrictions (no alterations, no subletting, no illegal activities)

7. **TERMINATION**
   - Notice period for termination (1-3 months)
   - Early termination penalty (if lock-in breached)
   - Grounds for termination (breach, non-payment)

**Step 3: Draft Lease Deed**

**Mandatory Contents:**

**1. PARTIES**
   - Lessor (Landlord): Full name, address, age
   - Lessee (Tenant): Full name, address, age

**2. RECITALS**
   - Lessor's ownership/title
   - Property description

**3. PROPERTY DESCRIPTION (Schedule)**
   - Address, survey number, boundaries, area
   - Specific portion (if part of larger property)

**4. TERM/DURATION**
   - Start date: ___
   - End date: ___
   - Lock-in period: ___

**5. RENT**
   - Monthly rent: Rs. ___
   - Payable on: ___ day of each month
   - Mode: ___
   - Escalation: ___% per year (if applicable)

**6. SECURITY DEPOSIT**
   - Amount: Rs. ___
   - Refundable: Yes/No
   - Interest: ___% per annum (if applicable)
   - Conditions for forfeiture: ___

**7. PERMITTED USE**
   - Residential/Commercial/Industrial
   - Restrictions: No subletting, no alterations without permission

**8. MAINTENANCE AND REPAIRS**
   - Minor repairs: Lessee
   - Major repairs: Lessor
   - Property tax: Lessor
   - Water/electricity: Lessee (actual consumption)

**9. LESSEE'S COVENANTS**
   - Pay rent punctually
   - Use property for permitted purpose only
   - Maintain property in good condition
   - Not make alterations without consent
   - Not sublet without consent
   - Allow lessor to inspect (with reasonable notice)
   - Return property in good condition on termination

**10. LESSOR'S COVENANTS**
   - Quiet enjoyment (no disturbance)
   - Deliver possession
   - Maintain structural soundness
   - Pay property tax

**11. TERMINATION**
   - Notice period: ___ months
   - Grounds for termination: Non-payment, breach, illegal use
   - Forfeiture clause (if breach)

**12. DISPUTE RESOLUTION**
   - Arbitration clause / Jurisdiction

**13. ATTESTATION**
   - Two witnesses

**Sample Clauses:**

```markdown
## Rent Clause
The Lessee shall pay to the Lessor monthly rent of Rs. 50,000/- (Rupees Fifty Thousand only) on or before the 5th day of each English calendar month, commencing from 1st January 2025. The rent shall be paid by electronic transfer to the Lessor's bank account (details provided separately).

## Escalation Clause
The rent shall be increased by 5% annually on each anniversary of the commencement date. For the second year (starting 1st January 2026), the rent shall be Rs. 52,500/-, and so on.

## Security Deposit Clause
The Lessee has paid to the Lessor a security deposit of Rs. 5,00,000/- (Rupees Five Lakhs only) to secure the performance of the Lessee's obligations. The deposit shall be refunded within 30 days of termination, subject to deductions for any damages, arrears, or unpaid charges. No interest shall accrue on the security deposit.

## Maintenance Clause
The Lessee shall be responsible for all minor repairs and day-to-day maintenance, including plumbing, electrical fittings, painting, and upkeep. The Lessor shall be responsible for major structural repairs, including roof, walls, foundation, and external drainage. Property tax, building insurance, and society charges (if any) shall be borne by the Lessor. Electricity, water, and gas charges shall be borne by the Lessee based on actual consumption.

## Subletting Clause
The Lessee shall not sublet, assign, or part with possession of the leased property or any part thereof without the prior written consent of the Lessor. Any subletting without consent shall be deemed a material breach, entitling the Lessor to terminate the lease forthwith.

## Termination Clause
Either party may terminate this lease by giving 2 (two) months' prior written notice to the other party. However, during the initial 11 months (lock-in period), neither party shall terminate except in case of material breach by the other party.

## Forfeiture Clause
If the Lessee fails to pay rent for 2 (two) consecutive months, or commits any material breach of the covenants herein, the Lessor shall have the right to forfeit the lease and re-enter the property after giving 15 days' written notice to remedy the breach. If the breach is not remedied within 15 days, the lease shall stand terminated, and the Lessee shall vacate forthwith.
```

**Step 4: Stamp Duty and Registration**

**Stamp Duty on Lease:**
- Varies by state
- Based on: Rent + Security Deposit + Lease Period
- Formula (common): (Average Annual Rent × Lease Period) × Stamp Duty Rate

**Example Calculation (Maharashtra):**
- Monthly rent: Rs. 50,000
- Annual rent: Rs. 6,00,000
- Lease period: 5 years
- Stamp duty rate: 0.25% for lease
- Stamp duty: (Rs. 6,00,000 × 5) × 0.25% = Rs. 7,500

**Registration Procedure (If Required):**

1. **Draft Lease Deed** - On stamp paper OR e-stamping
2. **Pay Stamp Duty** - Calculate and pay as per state rules
3. **Execute** - Both parties sign before two witnesses
4. **Appear at Sub-Registrar** - Within 4 months of execution
5. **Document Verification** - Sub-Registrar verifies
6. **Pay Registration Fee** - Usually 1% of stamp duty value (varies by state)
7. **Registration Complete** - Deed entered in register, serial number assigned
8. **Collect Certified Copies** - Registered lease deed (1-7 days)

**11-Month Leases:**
- Stamp duty payable (even if not registered)
- Stamp duty on notarized leave and license: Lower rates (state-specific)
- Maharashtra: 0.25% of total rent OR Rs. 1,000 (whichever lower) + notary charges

**Step 5: Delivery of Possession**

Methods:
1. **Physical Handover** - Keys delivered, lessee enters property
2. **Symbolic Delivery** - Handing over keys at lessor's office
3. **Constructive Delivery** - Attornment by prior occupier (if any)

**Possession Receipt:**
Lessee should give written acknowledgment of:
- Possession received on [date]
- Condition of property (good/fair)
- Inventory of fixtures (if furnished)
- Meter readings (electricity, water)

### Phase 2: During Lease Term

**Lessee's Ongoing Obligations:**

1. **Pay Rent Timely**
   - On due date specified
   - Late payment → Interest + penalty (as per agreement)
   - Persistent default → Forfeiture

2. **Use Property Properly**
   - For permitted purpose only
   - No illegal activities (drug trafficking, manufacturing without license)
   - No nuisance to neighbors

3. **Maintain Property**
   - Keep in good condition (ordinary wear and tear excepted)
   - Minor repairs (plumbing, electrical)
   - Not commit waste (damage/destruction)

4. **Not Alter Property**
   - No structural changes without lessor's consent
   - Fixtures installed by lessee removable (unless agreed otherwise)

5. **Allow Inspection**
   - Lessor can inspect with reasonable notice (usually 24-48 hours)
   - Not unreasonably refuse entry

6. **Not Sublet**
   - Without lessor's consent (if covenant prohibits)
   - Subletting without consent → Forfeiture

**Lessor's Ongoing Obligations:**

1. **Quiet Enjoyment** (Section 108(a))
   - Not disturb lessee's possession
   - Not interfere with lessee's use

2. **Maintain Structural Soundness**
   - Major repairs (roof, walls, foundation)
   - Keep property habitable/fit for purpose

3. **Pay Property Tax** (Unless Otherwise Agreed)
   - Lessor responsible for statutory dues
   - Lessee not liable for lessor's tax defaults

4. **Disclose Defects** (Section 108(b))
   - Inform lessee of material defects known to lessor

**Common Issues During Lease:**

**Issue 1: Rent Increase Dispute**
- If rent control applicable: Statutory limit on increase
- If no rent control: As per agreement OR market practice
- Standard: 5-10% annual escalation

**Issue 2: Maintenance Responsibility**
- Minor repairs: Lessee (Section 108(o))
- Major repairs: Lessor (Section 108(c))
- Ambiguous cases: Refer agreement OR general principle (structural = lessor)

**Issue 3: Subleasing Request**
- If agreement prohibits: Lessor can refuse arbitrarily
- If agreement silent: Lessee can sublet (Section 108(j))
- Lessor's consent: Should not be unreasonably withheld (if permitted)

### Phase 3: Termination of Lease

**Section 111: Determination of Lease**

**A. By Efflux of Time (Fixed-Term Lease)**
- Lease expires automatically on end date
- No notice required
- Lessee must vacate and return possession

**B. By Notice (Periodic Lease)**
- Section 106 notice requirements apply
- Month-to-month: 15 days notice
- Year-to-year: 6 months notice
- Notice must expire with end of period

**C. By Agreement**
- Mutual consent to terminate
- Surrender and acceptance
- Execute termination deed (advisable)

**D. By Forfeiture (Breach by Lessee)**
- Section 111(g): Lessor's right to forfeit
- Grounds: Non-payment, breach of covenant, unauthorized subletting
- Procedure: Notice to remedy breach → If not remedied, re-enter

**E. By Disclaimer (Lessee Renounces)**
- Lessee communicates intent to abandon lease
- Lessor accepts disclaimer
- Lease terminates

**F. By Merger**
- Lessee acquires lessor's interest (purchases property)
- Lease merges into ownership
- Terminates by operation of law

**G. By Destruction of Property**
- Property completely destroyed (fire, earthquake, flood)
- Lease frustrated (Section 108(e) exception)
- Terminates automatically

**H. By Operation of Law**
- Court decree
- Acquisition by government
- Insolvency of lessee

**Forfeiture Procedure (Section 111(g)):**

**Step 1: Breach Occurs**
- Lessee defaults (non-payment, unauthorized subletting, illegal use, waste)

**Step 2: Notice to Remedy** (Mandatory)
- Lessor gives written notice
- Specifying breach
- Demanding remedy within reasonable time (15-30 days)
- Warning of forfeiture if not remedied

**Step 3: Wait for Remedy Period**
- If lessee remedies breach → Lease continues
- If lessee does not remedy → Proceed to forfeiture

**Step 4: Re-Entry**
- Lessor re-enters property OR
- Files suit for possession (safer method)

**Step 5: Lessee's Relief (Section 114)**
- Lessee can apply to court for relief against forfeiture
- If breach remedied + compensation paid + court satisfied → Lease restored
- Discretionary remedy

**Notice Format (Sample):**

```
To: [Lessee Name and Address]

Date: [Date]

Subject: NOTICE UNDER SECTION 111(g) TPA - BREACH OF LEASE COVENANT

This is to inform you that you are in breach of the lease agreement dated [Date] for the property at [Address], on the following grounds:

1. Non-payment of rent for the months of [Months], totaling Rs. [Amount].
2. [Other breaches, if any]

You are hereby called upon to remedy the said breach by paying the arrears of rent and [remedy other breaches] within 15 days from the date of this notice.

Please note that if the breach is not remedied within the stipulated period, we shall be constrained to forfeit the lease and take possession of the property without further notice to you.

[Lessor Signature]
```

**Step 6: Ejectment Suit (If Lessee Refuses to Vacate)**

If lessee does not vacate after forfeiture:
- Lessor cannot forcibly evict (breach of peace)
- Must file ejectment suit

**Grounds for Ejectment (Typical):**
1. **Non-Payment of Rent** - Arrears beyond reasonable period
2. **Expiry of Lease** - Fixed term ended, notice given
3. **Breach of Covenant** - Subletting, illegal use, waste
4. **Bona Fide Need** - Lessor requires property for self-use (rent control jurisdictions)
5. **Unauthorized Construction** - Structural alterations without consent

### Phase 4: Ejectment Proceedings

**Route 1: Regular Civil Suit (If No Rent Control)**

**Court:** Civil Court having jurisdiction (where property situated)

**Procedure:**
1. File suit for ejectment + arrears + mesne profits
2. Plaint contents: Lease details, breach, forfeiture, demand for possession
3. Service of summons on lessee
4. Lessee files written statement (defense)
5. Framing of issues
6. Evidence (documentary + oral)
7. Arguments
8. Judgment
9. Decree for possession

**Timeline:** 1-3 years (depending on court backlog)

**Decree:**
- Ejectment of lessee
- Recovery of arrears
- Mesne profits (damages for wrongful possession)
- Costs

**Route 2: Rent Control Tribunal (If Rent Control Applicable)**

If property covered by Rent Control Act:
- Ejectment only on statutory grounds
- File application before Rent Controller/Tribunal
- Limited grounds: Non-payment, bona fide need, subletting, etc.
- Expedited procedure (theoretically)

**State-Specific Rent Control Acts:**
- Maharashtra: Maharashtra Rent Control Act 1999
- Delhi: Delhi Rent Control Act 1958
- West Bengal: West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act 1997
- Tamil Nadu: Tamil Nadu Buildings (Lease and Rent Control) Act 1960
- Karnataka: Karnataka Rent Control Act 1961

**Route 3: Summary Procedure (Section 14 Specific Relief Act - Suit for Injunction)**

If lessee creating nuisance/illegal use:
- File suit for injunction to restrain illegal activity
- Temporary injunction (Order XXXIX CPC)
- Quick interim relief

**Execution of Ejectment Decree:**

After obtaining decree:
1. **Execution Application** - Apply to court for execution
2. **Court Bailiff** - Court appoints bailiff to enforce possession
3. **Delivery of Possession** - Bailiff physically hands over possession to lessor
4. **Panchnama** - Report prepared by bailiff (possession delivered)

If lessee resists:
- Police assistance can be sought
- Contempt of court if willful disobedience

## Rights and Duties of Lessor and Lessee

### A. Rights of Lessee (Section 108)

**Right 1: Right to Possession (Section 108(a))**
- Lessee entitled to possession for lease period
- Lessor cannot disturb possession (quiet enjoyment)

**Right 2: Right to Fixtures (Section 108(k))**
- Fixtures attached by lessee remain lessee's property
- Removable at end of lease (unless agreed otherwise)
- Must not damage property during removal

**Fixtures vs Improvements:**
- **Fixtures:** Removable attachments (fans, ACs, shelves, curtains)
- **Improvements:** Permanent additions (cannot be removed without damage - built-in wardrobes, tiles)

**Right 3: Right to Sublet/Assign (Section 108(j))**
- Unless restricted by lease agreement
- If agreement silent: Lessee can transfer interest
- If prohibited: Subletting without consent = Forfeiture

**Right 4: Right to Emblements (Crops)**
- If agricultural lease
- Crops sown by lessee belong to lessee (even if harvested after lease ends)

**Right 5: Right to Compensation for Improvements (Section 108(l))**
- If lessee makes improvements with lessor's consent
- Entitled to compensation on termination
- Improvement increases property value

**Right 6: Right to Relief Against Forfeiture (Section 114)**
- If lease forfeited for breach
- Lessee can apply to court for relief
- If breach remedied + compensation paid → Court may restore lease

**Right 7: Right to Set Off (Section 108(m))**
- Lessee can set off lessor's breaches against rent
- If lessor fails major repairs → Lessee repairs and deducts from rent

**Right 8: Right to Defend Title (Section 108(h))**
- If third party claims property
- Lessee entitled to defend possession
- Lessor must indemnify if title defeated

### B. Duties of Lessee (Section 108)

**Duty 1: Pay Rent (Section 108(n))**
- Primary obligation
- On time and mode specified
- Failure → Forfeiture

**Duty 2: Disclose Defects (Section 108(b))**
- If lessee discovers material defects
- Inform lessor within reasonable time

**Duty 3: Maintain Property (Section 108(o))**
- Keep property in good condition
- Not commit waste (permissive or voluntary)
- Return property in good condition (wear and tear excepted)

**Duty 4: Give Notice of Transfer (Section 108(h))**
- If lessor sells property to third party
- Lessee must attorn to new owner (recognize as lessor)

**Duty 5: Allow Inspection**
- Lessor entitled to inspect (reasonable notice)
- Lessee must permit entry

**Duty 6: Deliver Possession on Termination (Section 108(q))**
- Vacate property
- Remove fixtures (without damage)
- Hand over keys

### C. Rights of Lessor (Section 109)

**Right 1: Right to Rent (Section 109(a))**
- Receive rent on due dates
- Sue for arrears
- Forfeit lease for non-payment

**Right 2: Right to Forfeiture (Section 111(g))**
- If lessee breaches covenant
- Follow notice procedure
- Re-enter and terminate lease

**Right 3: Right to Inspection**
- Inspect property to check condition
- Reasonable notice required (24-48 hours)

**Right 4: Right to Ejectment**
- On lease termination
- If lessee refuses to vacate → Sue for ejectment

**Right 5: Right to Mesne Profits (Section 109(e))**
- If lessee holds over after termination without consent
- Damages for wrongful possession (market rent OR double rent)

**Right 6: Right to Transfer Interest (Section 109(c))**
- Lessor can sell property
- Lease continues (buyer steps into lessor's shoes)
- Lessee's rights unaffected (Section 108(h))

### D. Duties of Lessor (Section 108)

**Duty 1: Deliver Possession (Section 108(a))**
- At commencement of lease
- Vacant possession (unless otherwise agreed)

**Duty 2: Quiet Enjoyment**
- Not interfere with lessee's possession
- Not disturb lessee's use

**Duty 3: Major Repairs (Section 108(c))**
- Structural repairs (roof, walls, foundation)
- Keep property fit for purpose

**Duty 4: Disclose Defects (Section 108(b))**
- Material defects known to lessor
- Inform lessee before lease

**Duty 5: Pay Property Tax** (Unless Otherwise Agreed)
- Statutory liability on owner
- Cannot recover from lessee (unless agreement permits)

---

## Special Situations

### 1. Holding Over (Section 116)

**Definition:** Lessee continues in possession after lease expiry without lessor's consent

**Legal Position:**
- Lessee becomes **Tenant at Sufferance**
- Lessor can:
  - Accept holding over → New lease created (same terms, month-to-month)
  - Refuse → Sue for ejectment + mesne profits

**Lessor Accepts Holding Over:**
- If lessor accepts rent after lease expiry
- Implies consent to continuation
- New lease created by implication
- Duration: Month-to-month OR year-to-year (as per previous rent payment cycle)

**Lessor Refuses Holding Over:**
- Demand possession
- If lessee refuses → Sue for ejectment + mesne profits
- Mesne profits: Market rent OR double the rent (whichever higher)

**Section 116 Effect:**
```
"If a lessee or sub-lessee of property remains in possession after determination 
of the lease granted to the lessee, and the lessor or his legal representative 
accepts rent from the lessee or sub-lessee, the lessor shall be deemed to have 
consented to the lessee's continuing in possession."
```

**Acceptance of Rent = Consent:**
- Even single acceptance creates new tenancy
- Lessor cannot claim arrears as mesne profits

**Avoiding Holding Over:**
- Lessor should:
  - Send termination notice well in advance
  - Demand vacation before expiry
  - Not accept rent after expiry (if wants to eject)
  - File ejectment suit immediately if lessee refuses

### 2. Subletting and Assignment

**Subletting (Section 108(j)):**
- Lessee leases property (or part) to third party (sub-lessee)
- Sub-lease period cannot exceed head-lease period
- Lessee remains liable to lessor for rent and covenants
- Sub-lessee liable to lessee (not lessor directly)

**Assignment:**
- Lessee transfers entire interest to assignee
- Assignee steps into lessee's shoes
- Assignee becomes tenant of lessor
- Original lessee may remain liable (depends on agreement)

**Difference:**

| Aspect | Subletting | Assignment |
|--------|------------|------------|
| **Transfer** | Part of interest | Entire interest |
| **Period** | Cannot exceed head-lease | Entire remaining period |
| **Relationship** | Lessee-Sub-lessee | Lessor-Assignee (Lessee drops out) |
| **Liability** | Lessee remains liable | Assignee liable (Lessee may be released) |

**Covenant Against Subletting:**
- Absolute prohibition: Cannot sublet at all
- Qualified prohibition: Cannot sublet without consent (consent not to be unreasonably withheld)

**Subletting Without Consent (If Prohibited):**
- Breach of covenant
- Lessor can forfeit lease
- Notice procedure (Section 111(g))

**Consent to Subletting:**
- Lessor should give written consent
- Specify conditions (identity of sub-lessee, rent, use)
- Consent to one subletting ≠ General permission (unless stated)

### 3. Fixtures and Improvements

**Section 108(k): Lessee's Fixtures Removable**

**Fixtures Defined:**
- Chattels attached to property for use/ornament
- Removable without substantial damage

**Examples:**
- Air conditioners (split/window)
- Fans, lights, chandeliers
- Curtain rods, blinds
- Kitchen cabinets (if removable)
- Shelves, racks

**Improvements Defined:**
- Permanent additions increasing property value
- Not removable without damage

**Examples:**
- False ceiling
- Built-in wardrobes
- Tiling, flooring (replacing existing)
- Plumbing/electrical upgrades (if embedded)

**Lessee's Rights:**
- Remove fixtures at lease end
- Not damage property during removal
- If damage caused → Compensate lessor

**Improvements Compensation (Section 108(l)):**
- Lessee makes improvements with lessor's consent
- On termination, lessee entitled to:
  - Take away improvements (if removable), OR
  - Compensation equal to increase in property value

**Lessor's Rights:**
- Refuse consent to improvements
- If consent given → May purchase improvements at fair value
- If no consent → Lessee not entitled to compensation

### 4. Transfer of Lessor's Interest

**Section 109(c) + Section 108(h): Sale of Leased Property**

**Effect of Sale:**
- Buyer (new owner) steps into lessor's shoes
- Lease continues with all terms
- Lessee's rights unaffected

**Lessee's Duties:**
- Recognize new owner as lessor (attornment)
- Pay rent to new owner
- If disputes old owner's sale → Lessor must defend title

**Protections for Lessee:**
- Lease deed registered → Notice to world (buyer cannot claim ignorance)
- Lease deed unregistered → If buyer has notice (actual/constructive), still bound
- If bona fide buyer without notice → May not be bound (but rare)

**Practical Tip:**
- Always register lease deed (if >12 months)
- Creates notice to subsequent buyers

### 5. Rent Control Legislation

**Purpose:**
- Protect tenants from unreasonable rent increases
- Prevent arbitrary eviction
- Housing affordability

**Key Features (Typical):**

**A. Rent Regulation:**
- Standard rent fixed (based on property value/construction cost)
- Cannot increase beyond ceiling
- Formula: 8-10% of construction cost OR historical benchmark

**B. Eviction Protection:**
- Landlord cannot evict except on statutory grounds:
  1. **Non-Payment** - Arrears beyond reasonable period (6+ months)
  2. **Bona Fide Need** - Landlord needs for self/family
  3. **Unauthorized Subletting** - Without consent
  4. **Illegal Use** - Using for unlawful purpose
  5. **Waste** - Damaging property
  6. **Denial of Title** - Tenant denies landlord's title
  7. **Nuisance** - Creating nuisance to neighbors

**C. Inheritance Rights:**
- Tenancy passes to family members (spouse, children)
- Protects from eviction on tenant's death

**D. Exemptions (Modern Acts):**
Many states exempt:
- New constructions (post-1995/2000)
- High-rent properties (>Rs. 25,000/50,000 pm)
- Commercial properties (in some states)
- Leave and license agreements (11 months)

**Applicability Examples:**

**Maharashtra Rent Control Act 1999:**
- Applies to premises let before 1999 (old tenancies)
- Exempts: New tenancies, high rent (>Rs. 5,000 pm in 1999 - adjusted upward)

**Delhi Rent Control Act 1958:**
- Applies to most premises
- Exempts: Government premises, hotels, dharamshalas

**Practical Impact:**
- Old tenancies (pre-rent control exemption) enjoy strong protection
- New tenancies (post-exemption) have minimal protection
- Landlords prefer 11-month leave and license to avoid rent control

---

## Common Scenarios and Solutions

### Scenario 1: Lessee Refuses to Vacate After Lease Expiry

**Facts:**
- 11-month lease expired
- Lessee refuses to vacate
- No renewal agreed
- Lessor wants possession

**Lessor's Options:**

**Option 1: Accept Rent and Create New Tenancy**
- If lessor accepts rent for months after expiry
- New periodic tenancy created (Section 116)
- Month-to-month tenancy
- Terminate by 15 days notice (Section 106)

**Option 2: Refuse Rent and Sue for Ejectment**
- Do not accept rent post-expiry
- Send legal notice demanding vacation
- If lessee refuses → File ejectment suit
- Claim mesne profits (double rent)

**Timeline:**
- Notice: 15-30 days
- Suit filing: Immediate
- Decree: 6-12 months
- Execution: 3-6 months
- Total: 9-18 months

**Lessee's Defense:**
- Claim renewal was agreed (difficult without writing)
- Offer to vacate if adequate time given
- Pay mesne profits to avoid adverse decree

---

### Scenario 2: Lessee Sublets Without Consent

**Facts:**
- Lease agreement prohibits subletting without consent
- Lessor discovers lessee has sublet to third party
- Rent being collected by lessee from sub-lessee

**Lessor's Remedies:**

**Remedy 1: Forfeit Lease (Section 111(g))**
- Send notice specifying breach (unauthorized subletting)
- Demand termination of sub-lease within 15 days
- If not complied → Forfeit lease and sue for possession

**Remedy 2: Seek Injunction**
- File suit to restrain lessee from subletting
- Temporary injunction (Order XXXIX CPC)

**Remedy 3: Damages**
- Claim damages for breach of covenant
- Loss = Difference between market rent and agreed rent (if sub-lease at higher rent)

**Lessee's Defense:**
- Argue subletting permitted (if agreement silent)
- Section 108(j): Lessee can transfer unless restricted
- If agreement ambiguous, interpret in favor of lessee

---

### Scenario 3: Lessor Fails to Do Major Repairs

**Facts:**
- Roof leaking, causing water seepage
- Lessee informed lessor multiple times
- Lessor refuses to repair
- Lessee's belongings damaged

**Lessee's Remedies:**

**Remedy 1: Self-Help and Set-Off (Section 108(m))**
- Lessee does repairs himself
- Deduct repair cost from rent
- Retain receipts and proof
- Inform lessor in writing

**Remedy 2: Sue for Specific Performance**
- File suit compelling lessor to do repairs
- Section 10 Specific Relief Act
- Obtain decree directing repair

**Remedy 3: Damages**
- Claim compensation for:
  - Damage to lessee's belongings
  - Mental agony (if severe)
  - Loss of use/enjoyment

**Remedy 4: Terminate Lease (If Substantial)**
- If lessor's breach frustrates lease purpose
- Treat as repudiation
- Vacate and claim damages

**Lessor's Defense:**
- Repairs are minor (lessee's responsibility)
- Lessee caused damage (not wear and tear)
- Lessee did not give reasonable notice

---

### Scenario 4: Rent Control Applicability Dispute

**Facts:**
- Property leased in 2000 (in Delhi)
- Monthly rent Rs. 40,000
- Landlord wants to increase rent to Rs. 60,000
- Tenant claims rent control protection

**Analysis:**

**Step 1: Check Rent Control Act Applicability**
- Delhi Rent Control Act 1958 applies to most premises
- Exemptions: Government, hotels, dharamshalas, certain new constructions

**Step 2: Determine Standard Rent**
- If property falls under DRC Act, standard rent fixed by Rent Controller
- Cannot exceed statutory ceiling

**Step 3: Rent Increase Procedure**
- Landlord can apply to Rent Controller for rent increase
- Based on property value, construction cost, market rate
- Tenant can contest

**Outcome:**
- If covered: Rent increase subject to Rent Controller approval
- If exempt: Rent as per agreement/market rate

**Practical Tip:**
- New leases often exempt from rent control (check state-specific exemptions)
- Landlords prefer 11-month leave and license to avoid rent control

---

### Scenario 5: Lessee Claims Ownership by Adverse Possession

**Facts:**
- Lessee in possession for 15 years
- Paid rent initially, then stopped
- Lessor did not pursue eviction
- Now lessee claims ownership by adverse possession

**Legal Position:**

**Adverse Possession Requirements:**
1. **Hostile Possession** - Against owner's title
2. **Actual Possession** - Physical occupation
3. **Open and Notorious** - Visible to owner
4. **Continuous** - Uninterrupted for 12 years (Article 65 Limitation Act)
5. **Exclusive** - To exclusion of owner

**Lessee's Challenge:**
- **Permissive Possession** - Lessee's possession is NOT hostile (it's permissive)
- Adverse possession does NOT apply to lessees (unless explicitly deny title)

**When Lessee Can Claim Adverse Possession:**
- If lessee explicitly denies lessor's title
- AND possesses adversely for 12 years thereafter
- Courts require clear, unequivocal denial

**Lessor's Defense:**
- Lessee cannot claim adverse possession (possession is permissive)
- Even if rent not paid, possession remains under lease
- Adverse possession clock does not start

**Outcome:**
- Lessor likely to succeed
- Lessee should be evicted (not become owner)

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Not Registering Lease (>12 Months)
**Consequence:** Lease inadmissible as evidence; difficult to prove terms
**Solution:** ALWAYS register lease exceeding 12 months

### 2. Accepting Rent After Lease Expiry (If Want to Eject)
**Consequence:** Creates new tenancy (Section 116); cannot eject easily
**Solution:** Refuse rent; send termination notice; file ejectment suit

### 3. Subletting Without Lessor's Consent (If Prohibited)
**Consequence:** Forfeiture of lease; eviction
**Solution:** Obtain written consent before subletting

### 4. Ignoring Rent Control Applicability
**Consequence:** Eviction grounds limited; rent increase restricted
**Solution:** Verify rent control applicability before leasing

### 5. Not Conducting Property Inspection Before Leasing
**Consequence:** Discover defects later; disputes over maintenance
**Solution:** Thorough inspection; document condition; take photographs

### 6. Vague Maintenance Clauses
**Consequence:** Disputes over repair responsibility
**Solution:** Clearly specify lessor vs lessee responsibilities (major vs minor repairs)

### 7. No Written Lease Agreement
**Consequence:** Difficult to prove terms; disputes likely
**Solution:** Always execute written lease agreement (even if not registrable)

### 8. Forfeiture Without Notice
**Consequence:** Court may grant relief against forfeiture (Section 114)
**Solution:** Always send Section 111(g) notice before forfeiting

### 9. Accepting "Leave and License" Label Blindly
**Consequence:** Agreement may actually be lease (if exclusive possession + rent)
**Solution:** Examine substance, not label; ensure true license characteristics

### 10. Not Taking Security Deposit Receipt
**Consequence:** Dispute over refund amount at termination
**Solution:** Obtain written receipt specifying deposit amount, refund conditions

# Indian Property Law: Mortgage of Immovable Property

## Purpose
This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on mortgage of immovable property under the Transfer of Property Act 1882 (Sections 58-104). It covers the 6 types of mortgages (simple, conditional sale, usufructuary, English, equitable, anomalous), essential elements, rights and duties of mortgagor and mortgagee, redemption rights, foreclosure, sale procedures, subrogation, marshalling, contribution, and practical considerations for mortgage transactions.

## Activation Triggers
Use this protocol when:
- Client wants to mortgage property for loan/debt security
- Analyzing mortgage deed validity or classification
- Mortgage default/breach by mortgagor (non-payment)
- Mortgagee wants to enforce security (sale/foreclosure)
- Redemption suit by mortgagor (paying off debt)
- Priority disputes between multiple mortgagees
- Subrogation claims by subsequent purchaser/mortgagee
- Marshalling of securities application
- Contribution between co-mortgagors
- Equitable mortgage creation (deposit of title deeds)
- Mortgage by deposit without written document
- Tacking of further advances to existing mortgage

## Core Principles

### 1. Section 58: Mortgage Defined

**"Mortgage is the transfer of an interest in specific immovable property for the purpose of securing the payment of money advanced or to be advanced by way of loan, an existing or future debt, or the performance of an engagement which may give rise to a pecuniary liability."**

Essential Elements of Mortgage:
1. **TRANSFER OF INTEREST** - In immovable property (not ownership)
2. **SPECIFIC PROPERTY** - Identified property (not general assets)
3. **PURPOSE** - Securing debt/loan/engagement
4. **DEBT** - Existing, future, or contingent
5. **TRANSFEROR** - Mortgagor (borrower/debtor)
6. **TRANSFEREE** - Mortgagee (lender/creditor)

**Key Distinction:**

| Aspect | Sale | Mortgage |
|--------|------|----------|
| **Transfer** | Ownership | Interest (security) |
| **Purpose** | Absolute transfer | Security for debt |
| **Redemption** | No right to get back | Right to redeem |
| **Price vs Debt** | Price paid | Debt secured |
| **Permanent** | Yes | Temporary (until debt repaid) |

**Mortgage vs Charge:**

| Aspect | Mortgage | Charge |
|--------|----------|--------|
| **Interest** | Transfer of interest | NO transfer |
| **Right** | Right in rem | Right in personam |
| **Remedy** | Sale of property | Court-ordered sale only |
| **Creation** | Transfer deed | Agreement/operation of law |

### 2. Section 58: Six Types of Mortgages

#### Type 1: SIMPLE MORTGAGE (Section 58(b))

**Definition**: Mortgagor binds himself personally to pay debt and agrees that in default, mortgagee can sell property, but WITHOUT delivering possession.

**Essential Features:**
1. Personal liability of mortgagor (covenant to pay)
2. Agreement that mortgagee can cause sale in default
3. NO delivery of possession
4. NO power of sale without court intervention

**Example**: A borrows Rs. 10 lakhs from B, executes simple mortgage deed creating charge on property. A retains possession. If A defaults, B must file suit for sale.

**Remedy for Mortgagee:**
- File suit for SALE of property (Section 67 TPA)
- Obtain preliminary decree
- Obtain final decree for sale
- Property sold through court

**Advantages:**
- Mortgagor retains possession and use of property
- No risk of extrajudicial foreclosure

**Disadvantages:**
- Lengthy court process for mortgagee
- Cannot take immediate possession

---

#### Type 2: MORTGAGE BY CONDITIONAL SALE (Section 58(c))

**Definition**: Mortgagor ostensibly sells property on condition that:
- On default, sale becomes absolute, OR
- On payment, sale becomes void, OR
- On payment, buyer re-transfers to seller

**Essential Features:**
1. Ostensible sale (appears to be sale)
2. Condition of re-transfer on payment
3. NO delivery of possession (usually)
4. Distinguishable from actual sale by redemption right

**Test to Distinguish from Sale:**
- If transaction intended as SECURITY for debt → Mortgage by conditional sale
- If transaction intended as ABSOLUTE transfer → Sale with repurchase condition

**Example**: A needs Rs. 20 lakhs urgently. Sells property to B for Rs. 20 lakhs with agreement that if A repays within 5 years, B will re-transfer property.

**Remedy for Mortgagee:**
- In default: Claim absolute ownership (no court sale needed)
- But courts apply Section 60 redemption right (mortgagor can redeem even after default)

**Advantages:**
- Appears simpler (like sale)
- Mortgagee claims ownership on default

**Disadvantages:**
- Courts scrutinize heavily (to protect mortgagor)
- Redemption right cannot be excluded
- Potential for dispute over nature (sale vs mortgage)

---

#### Type 3: USUFRUCTUARY MORTGAGE (Section 58(d))

**Definition**: Mortgagor delivers possession or expressly/impliedly binds himself to deliver possession, and mortgagee entitled to receive rents and profits in lieu of interest or in payment of debt or both.

**Essential Features:**
1. Delivery of POSSESSION to mortgagee
2. Mortgagee receives rents/profits
3. NO personal liability of mortgagor (usually)
4. NO power of sale to mortgagee
5. Debt extinguished when rents/profits = debt amount

**Example**: A borrows Rs. 5 lakhs from B, delivers possession of house. B collects rent of Rs. 25,000/month. Debt reduces by rental amount until fully paid.

**Duration:**
- Usually fixed period (e.g., 10 years)
- Or until debt paid from rents/profits

**Remedy for Mortgagee:**
- Possession and enjoyment
- Collect rents and profits
- If mortgagor obstructs possession: File suit for possession

**Remedy for Mortgagor:**
- Redeem mortgage after period/payment complete
- File suit for redemption (Section 60)

**Advantages:**
- Mortgagee gets possession and income
- No court process needed for possession

**Disadvantages:**
- Mortgagor loses possession and use
- Recovery slow (depends on rental income)

---

#### Type 4: ENGLISH MORTGAGE (Section 58(e))

**Definition**: Mortgagor binds himself to repay debt on certain date and transfers property absolutely to mortgagee, subject to proviso that mortgagee will re-transfer on payment.

**Essential Features:**
1. ABSOLUTE transfer of property to mortgagee
2. Condition of re-transfer on payment by certain date
3. Personal liability to repay
4. Mortgagee becomes owner (subject to redemption)
5. Possession may or may not be delivered

**Example**: A borrows Rs. 50 lakhs from Bank B, executes mortgage deed transferring ownership absolutely to Bank, with condition that on repayment within 15 years, Bank will re-transfer.

**Distinguishing Features:**
- Similar to mortgage by conditional sale
- BUT English mortgage has absolute transfer FIRST, then condition of re-transfer
- Conditional sale has apparent sale with condition making it void

**Remedy for Mortgagee:**
- On default: Mortgagee is already owner
- File suit for foreclosure (to extinguish redemption right)
- OR sell property and recover debt

**Advantages:**
- Strong security (mortgagee is owner)
- Easy enforcement

**Disadvantages:**
- Mortgagor loses ownership (only equity of redemption remains)
- Requires careful drafting to distinguish from sale

---

#### Type 5: MORTGAGE BY DEPOSIT OF TITLE DEEDS / EQUITABLE MORTGAGE (Section 58(f))

**Definition**: In certain towns (notified), delivery of title documents to creditor with intent to create security, without written mortgage deed.

**Essential Features:**
1. DEPOSIT of title deeds (original documents)
2. INTENT to create security (proved by circumstances)
3. NO written mortgage deed required
4. Applicable ONLY in notified towns (Presidency towns + notified areas)
5. Debt may be existing or future

**Notified Towns (Examples):**
- Calcutta (Kolkata)
- Madras (Chennai)
- Bombay (Mumbai)
- Delhi
- Pune
- Bangalore
- Hyderabad
- Ahmedabad
- + many others (check state notifications)

**Example**: A wants loan of Rs. 15 lakhs from Bank B in Mumbai. Deposits original sale deed, previous title documents with bank. Bank disburses loan. No mortgage deed executed.

**Proof of Intent:**
- Loan agreement mentioning deposit as security
- Receipt of title documents acknowledging security purpose
- Correspondence showing intent

**Registration:**
- NOT required (no written instrument)
- But bank may register memorandum of deposit

**Advantages:**
- Simple, quick, cost-effective
- No stamp duty on mortgage deed
- No registration fee
- Popular with banks

**Disadvantages:**
- Available only in notified towns
- Difficult to prove intent if documents missing
- Priority issues with registered mortgages

**Remedy for Mortgagee:**
- Same as simple mortgage: File suit for SALE (Section 67)
- Cannot take possession without court order

---

#### Type 6: ANOMALOUS MORTGAGE (Section 58(g))

**Definition**: Mortgage combining features of two or more types, OR not falling under any of the above five types.

**Essential Feature:**
- Hybrid nature (combination of features)

**Example 1**: Simple mortgage + Delivery of possession = Anomalous (combines simple + usufructuary features)

**Example 2**: Conditional sale + Power of sale without court = Anomalous

**Remedy:**
- Determined by mortgage deed terms
- Courts apply principles of all types involved

---

### 3. Rights and Duties of Mortgagor

#### A. Rights of Mortgagor

**Right 1: RIGHT TO REDEEM (Section 60) - EQUITY OF REDEMPTION**

Most Fundamental Right - CANNOT BE EXCLUDED by contract

```
Section 60: "At any time after the principal money has become due, 
the mortgagor has right to redeem the mortgage"
```

**Essential Features:**
1. Right arises when debt becomes due
2. Cannot be excluded by clog/fetter on equity
3. Exercisable by payment of entire debt + interest + costs
4. Available to mortgagor, his heirs, assignees

**Clog on Equity of Redemption (VOID):**
- Stipulation making redemption impossible/difficult
- Irredeemable mortgage (void after debt due)
- Unconscionable terms preventing redemption

**Example of Clog (VOID)**: Mortgage deed says "Redemption allowed only if paid in gold coins" - VOID

**Method of Redemption:**
- Tender of entire debt + interest + costs
- File suit for redemption (if mortgagee refuses)
- Obtain redemption decree and pay into court

**Right 2: RIGHT TO LEASE (Section 65A)**

During subsistence of mortgage:
- Simple mortgage: Mortgagor can lease (possession with him)
- Usufructuary/English mortgage: Mortgagee can lease (possession with mortgagee)

Restrictions:
- Lease should not prejudice mortgagee's interest
- Lease beyond mortgage term may be invalid

**Right 3: RIGHT TO ACCESSIONS (Section 63)**

Mortgagor entitled to:
- Accretions to property (alluvion, new land)
- Improvements made by mortgagor

**Right 4: RIGHT TO INSPECT DOCUMENTS (Section 60(c))**

Mortgagor can:
- Inspect title documents in mortgagee's custody
- Obtain certified copies at his cost

**Right 5: RIGHT TO REDEEM LATER MORTGAGES (Section 92)**

If property has multiple mortgages:
- Mortgagor can redeem later mortgage without redeeming earlier
- Prevents junior mortgagee from consolidating position

#### B. Duties of Mortgagor

**Duty 1: PAY DEBT (Primary Obligation)**

Mortgagor must:
- Pay principal amount on due date
- Pay interest as agreed
- Pay costs of mortgage

**Duty 2: NOT COMMIT WASTE (Section 65)**

Mortgagor cannot:
- Destroy/damage property
- Alter property substantially
- Commit acts reducing property value

**Duty 3: INSURE PROPERTY (If Agreed)**

If mortgage deed requires:
- Insure property against fire, earthquake, etc.
- Premium paid by mortgagor
- Policy assigned to mortgagee

**Duty 4: PAY TAXES (Unless Otherwise Agreed)**

Mortgagor responsible for:
- Property tax
- Land revenue
- Other statutory dues

**Duty 5: DEFEND TITLE (Section 62)**

If third party claims property:
- Mortgagor must defend title
- Indemnify mortgagee if title defeated

---

### 4. Rights and Duties of Mortgagee

#### A. Rights of Mortgagee

**Right 1: RIGHT TO SUE FOR DEBT (Section 67)**

If mortgagor defaults:
- File suit for recovery of debt (personal liability)
- Without disturbing mortgage security
- Limitation: 12 years from due date (Article 132)

**Right 2: RIGHT TO SUE FOR SALE (Section 69)**

Applicable to:
- Simple mortgage
- Equitable mortgage (deposit of title deeds)

Procedure:
1. File suit for sale (Section 67 TPA + Order XXXIV CPC)
2. Preliminary decree (declaring debt, ordering redemption in 6 months)
3. If not redeemed: Final decree for sale
4. Court-conducted sale
5. Sale proceeds applied to debt

**Right 3: RIGHT TO FORECLOSURE (Section 67)**

Applicable to:
- English mortgage
- Mortgage by conditional sale

**Foreclosure**: Extinguishing mortgagor's right to redeem

Procedure:
1. File suit for foreclosure
2. Preliminary decree (declaring debt, ordering redemption in 6 months)
3. If not redeemed: Final decree of foreclosure
4. Mortgagor's equity of redemption extinguished
5. Mortgagee becomes absolute owner

**Strict Foreclosure**: Property value > Debt
- Mortgagee keeps entire property (no refund of excess)
- Harsh remedy (courts reluctant)

**Right 4: RIGHT TO POSSESSION**

Depends on mortgage type:
- Usufructuary/English mortgage: Mortgagee entitled to possession
- Simple/Equitable mortgage: NO right to possession without court order

**Right 5: RIGHT TO SUBSEQUENT MORTGAGEE'S SALE PROCEEDS (Section 92)**

If property sold by subsequent mortgagee:
- Prior mortgagee entitled to payment from sale proceeds (priority)

**Right 6: RIGHT TO ACCESSIONS (Section 63)**

Mortgagee's security extends to:
- Accretions to property
- Improvements made by mortgagee

**Right 7: RIGHT TO INSURANCE MONEY**

If property destroyed and insured:
- Insurance proceeds applied to mortgage debt
- Mortgagee's security transfers to insurance money

**Right 8: RIGHT TO SALE OF MORTGAGED PROPERTY**

If mortgage deed confers power of sale:
- Sell property WITHOUT court intervention
- Common in bank mortgages, institutional loans

Requirements:
- Default by mortgagor
- Notice to mortgagor (30-60 days)
- Sell at fair market value
- Account for sale proceeds

**Right 9: RIGHT TO APPOINT RECEIVER**

If property income at risk:
- Court may appoint receiver to collect rents
- Receiver protects property and income for mortgagee

#### B. Duties of Mortgagee

**Duty 1: RETURN TITLE DOCUMENTS (Section 60(c))**

On redemption:
- Return all title documents to mortgagor
- Provide receipts, accounts

**Duty 2: RE-TRANSFER PROPERTY (Section 60(b))**

On redemption of English mortgage/conditional sale:
- Execute re-transfer deed (at mortgagor's cost)
- Deliver possession if taken

**Duty 3: ACCOUNT FOR RENTS/PROFITS**

In usufructuary mortgage:
- Account for rents and profits received
- Apply to debt reduction

**Duty 4: MANAGE PROPERTY PROPERLY**

If in possession:
- Maintain property
- Not commit waste
- Not make unauthorized alterations

**Duty 5: SELL AT FAIR VALUE (If Power of Sale)**

If selling under power of sale:
- Advertise properly
- Sell at fair market value
- Account for sale proceeds
- Return surplus to mortgagor

## Framework

### Phase 1: Creating Mortgage

**Step 1: Determine Mortgage Type**

Based on requirements:
- Need loan + retain possession? → Simple mortgage OR Equitable mortgage
- Lender wants possession + income? → Usufructuary mortgage
- Bank loan secured by title deposit? → Equitable mortgage
- Absolute security to lender? → English mortgage

**Step 2: Draft Mortgage Deed**

**Mandatory Contents:**

1. **PARTIES**
   - Mortgagor (borrower) - Full name, address, age
   - Mortgagee (lender) - Full name, address

2. **RECITALS**
   - Purpose of loan
   - Mortgagor's ownership of property

3. **PROPERTY DESCRIPTION**
   - Schedule with survey numbers, boundaries, area

4. **DEBT DETAILS**
   - Principal amount
   - Interest rate (simple/compound)
   - Repayment schedule
   - Due date

5. **MORTGAGE CLAUSE**
   - Type of mortgage (simple, English, etc.)
   - Security interest created
   - Personal liability (if applicable)

6. **COVENANTS**
   - Mortgagor's covenants (pay debt, maintain property, insure)
   - Mortgagee's covenants (return documents on redemption)

7. **DEFAULT PROVISIONS**
   - Events of default
   - Remedies on default (sale, foreclosure)
   - Notice period

8. **POWER OF SALE** (If Applicable)
   - Mortgagee's right to sell without court
   - Conditions for exercise

9. **INSURANCE**
   - Requirement to insure
   - Policy assignment

10. **ATTESTATION**
    - Two witnesses

**Step 3: Valuation and Loan-to-Value (LTV) Ratio**

Standard LTV:
- Residential property: 75-90% of market value
- Commercial property: 60-75%
- Agricultural land: 50-70%

Example:
- Property value: Rs. 1 crore
- LTV: 80%
- Maximum loan: Rs. 80 lakhs

**Step 4: Stamp Duty and Registration**

**Stamp Duty on Mortgage:**
- Varies by state (usually 0.1% to 1% of debt amount)
- Much lower than sale deed stamp duty

Examples:
- Maharashtra: 0.3% of debt
- Karnataka: 0.1% of debt
- Delhi: Rs. 5,000 fixed + 0.2% of debt

**Registration:**
- Section 17 Registration Act: Mortgage deed MUST be registered
- Unregistered mortgage void (Section 49 Registration Act)

Procedure:
- Execute mortgage deed
- Pay stamp duty
- Register at Sub-Registrar within 4 months
- Both parties + witnesses appear

**Step 5: Creation of Equitable Mortgage (Deposit of Title Deeds)**

Applicable: Notified towns only

Procedure:
1. **Loan Agreement** - Document loan terms, refer to deposit
2. **Deposit Title Deeds** - Hand over original sale deed, previous chain documents
3. **Receipt** - Mortgagee gives receipt acknowledging deposit for security
4. **Memorandum** - Draft memorandum of deposit (optional but advisable)
5. **Registration** - Not mandatory, but memorandum can be registered

No Stamp Duty on Deposit (Big Advantage)

**Step 6: Disbursement**

After mortgage created:
- Mortgagee disburses loan amount
- Usually to mortgagor's bank account
- Receipt obtained

### Phase 2: During Subsistence of Mortgage

**Step 1: Repayment**

Mortgagor makes:
- EMI payments (if amortized loan)
- Interest payments (if interest-only loan)
- Bullet payment at end (if structured)

**Step 2: Monitoring**

Mortgagee monitors:
- Timely payments
- Property condition (if possession with mortgagee)
- Tax payments
- Insurance renewal

**Step 3: Further Advances (Section 94 - Tacking)**

If mortgagee makes additional loans:
- Can "tack" to original mortgage (same priority)
- If no notice of intervening mortgage

**Tacking Conditions:**
1. Original mortgage reserves right to further advances, OR
2. No notice of subsequent mortgage at time of further advance

**Step 3: Second/Third Mortgage (Subsequent Mortgages)**

Mortgagor can create multiple mortgages:
- Must disclose prior mortgages
- Second mortgagee gets second priority
- Third mortgagee gets third priority

**Priority Rule**: First in time, first in right (if all registered)

### Phase 3: Redemption (Paying Off Mortgage)

**Step 1: Payment Tender**

Mortgagor:
- Calculates total dues (principal + interest + costs)
- Tenders payment to mortgagee
- Demands return of documents

**Step 2: Redemption Deed Execution**

For English mortgage/Conditional sale:
- Mortgagee executes reconveyance/re-transfer deed
- Property transferred back to mortgagor
- Registered at Sub-Registrar

**Step 3: Return of Documents**

Mortgagee returns:
- Original mortgage deed
- Title documents (if deposited)
- Sale deed, previous chain documents

**Step 4: Discharge Entry**

In Sub-Registrar records:
- Endorsement of satisfaction of mortgage
- Updates property records (mortgage discharged)

**Step 5: Mutation**

Revenue records updated:
- Mortgage charge removed
- Clear title restored

### Phase 4: Enforcement (Mortgagor Defaulted)

**Step 1: Notice of Default**

Mortgagee sends:
- Notice to mortgagor specifying default
- Demand for payment within 30-60 days
- Warning of enforcement action

**Step 2A: Suit for Sale (Simple/Equitable Mortgage)**

Procedure (Order XXXIV CPC):

1. **File Suit** - In court having jurisdiction (where property situated)
   - Plaint contents: Debt details, mortgage deed, default, prayer for sale
   
2. **Preliminary Decree** (Section 67 TPA)
   - Court declares debt due
   - Orders mortgagor to pay within 6 months (or court-fixed period)
   - If paid: Mortgage redeemed
   - If not paid: Proceed to final decree

3. **Final Decree for Sale**
   - Court orders sale of mortgaged property
   - Sale conducted by court-appointed officer

4. **Court Sale**
   - Advertisement (3 notices: 30 days, 15 days, 7 days before sale)
   - Auction on specified date
   - Sale to highest bidder
   - Sale requires court confirmation

5. **Application of Sale Proceeds:**
   ```
   Priority:
   1. Court costs and expenses
   2. Mortgagee's debt (principal + interest + costs)
   3. Subsequent mortgagees (in order of priority)
   4. Surplus to mortgagor
   ```

6. **Deficiency**
   - If sale proceeds < debt: Mortgagor liable for deficit
   - Mortgagee can file execution petition for deficit

**Limitation**: 12 years from due date (Article 132 Limitation Act)

**Step 2B: Foreclosure Suit (English Mortgage)**

Procedure:

1. **File Suit for Foreclosure**
   - Claim: Extinguish mortgagor's equity of redemption

2. **Preliminary Decree**
   - Court declares debt
   - Orders mortgagor to redeem within 6 months

3. **Final Decree of Foreclosure**
   - If not redeemed: Mortgagor's right extinguished
   - Mortgagee becomes ABSOLUTE OWNER
   - No surplus returned (even if property value > debt)

**Strict Foreclosure Problem:**
- Harsh remedy
- Mortgagee gains windfall if property valuable
- Courts may order sale instead (Section 67 TPA)

**Step 2C: Sale Under Power of Sale (If Mortgage Deed Confers)**

Common in bank mortgages, SARFAESI Act loans

Procedure:

1. **Notice** - 60 days notice to mortgagor (demand payment)

2. **Valuation** - Obtain property valuation

3. **Advertisement** - Public notice in newspapers (2 newspapers, 30 days)

4. **Auction** - Public auction OR private sale

5. **Sale Deed** - Execute sale deed to purchaser

6. **Account** - Apply proceeds to debt, return surplus

**Advantages:**
- Quick (no court process)
- Cost-effective

**Disadvantages:**
- Risk of challenge (if procedure not followed)
- Mortgagor can file suit to restrain sale

**SARFAESI Act 2002 (Special Statute):**
- Banks/Financial Institutions can enforce security under SARFAESI
- Section 13(2): Notice to borrower
- Section 13(4): Take possession after 60 days
- Sale under Section 13(4) without court intervention

## Special Doctrines in Mortgage Law

### 1. SUBROGATION (Section 92)

**Principle**: One who pays off mortgage steps into shoes of mortgagee

**Application:**

**Scenario 1**: Subsequent mortgagee pays off prior mortgage
- Second mortgagee pays first mortgagee's debt
- Second mortgagee gets first mortgagee's priority (subrogation)

**Scenario 2**: Buyer of mortgaged property pays mortgage debt
- Buyer pays mortgagee to get clear title
- Buyer subrogated to mortgagee's rights (can recover from mortgagor)

**Scenario 3**: Surety pays mortgage debt
- Surety who guaranteed loan pays mortgagee
- Surety subrogated to mortgagee's position

**Conditions for Subrogation:**
1. Payment made to protect own interest
2. Payment discharges debt
3. Not merely voluntary payment

**Effect:**
- Subrogated person gets all rights of original mortgagee
- Can enforce mortgage against mortgagor
- Gets same priority

---

### 2. MARSHALLING (Section 81)

**Principle**: Adjustment of rights where one creditor has multiple securities and another has single security in one of those properties

**Scenario:**
- Mortgagee A has mortgage on Property X + Property Y
- Mortgagee B has mortgage on Property X ONLY (subsequent)
- If A enforces against Property X, B's security destroyed

**Marshalling Rule:**
- B can compel A to enforce against Property Y first (where B has no interest)
- Preserves B's security in Property X

**Conditions:**
1. A must have TWO OR MORE securities
2. B must have security in ONE of those properties (junior to A)
3. B's security must be at risk
4. No prejudice to A

**Example:**
- Bank A: Mortgage on House + Factory (debt Rs. 50 lakhs)
- Bank B: Mortgage on House only (debt Rs. 20 lakhs, second mortgage)
- House value: Rs. 40 lakhs; Factory value: Rs. 30 lakhs

Without Marshalling:
- A sells House (Rs. 40 lakhs) + Factory (Rs. 30 lakhs) = Rs. 70 lakhs
- A recovers Rs. 50 lakhs (full debt)
- B gets Rs. 20 lakhs from surplus (full recovery)

If A Sells House First:
- A recovers Rs. 40 lakhs from House sale
- B gets nothing (House sold)
- A then sells Factory for Rs. 30 lakhs
- Total: A gets Rs. 70 lakhs (Rs. 20 lakhs surplus)
- B gets nothing

Marshalling Application:
- B can compel A to sell Factory FIRST (Rs. 30 lakhs)
- A gets Rs. 30 lakhs
- A then sells House for Rs. 40 lakhs (Rs. 10 lakhs more needed)
- A gets full Rs. 50 lakhs total
- House sale surplus: Rs. 30 lakhs (Rs. 40 lakhs - Rs. 10 lakhs to A)
- B recovers Rs. 20 lakhs from surplus

**Effect**: Protects junior mortgagee where senior has multiple securities

---

### 3. CONTRIBUTION (Section 82)

**Principle**: Where multiple properties mortgaged for single debt, each property contributes proportionately

**Scenario:**
- Single mortgage deed covering Property A + Property B + Property C
- Total debt: Rs. 60 lakhs
- Property A value: Rs. 30 lakhs
- Property B value: Rs. 20 lakhs
- Property C value: Rs. 10 lakhs
- Total value: Rs. 60 lakhs

**Contribution Rule:**
- Each property contributes in proportion to its value

Calculation:
- Property A: (30/60) × Rs. 60 lakhs = Rs. 30 lakhs liability
- Property B: (20/60) × Rs. 60 lakhs = Rs. 20 lakhs liability
- Property C: (10/60) × Rs. 60 lakhs = Rs. 10 lakhs liability

**Application:**
If mortgagee sells Property A for Rs. 30 lakhs and recovers full Rs. 30 lakhs:
- Property A owner can claim contribution from Property B and C owners
- B and C must contribute proportionately

**Conditions:**
1. Multiple properties mortgaged for SINGLE debt (not separate debts)
2. Properties owned by different persons OR different interests
3. Debt satisfied from one property disproportionately

---

### 4. CONSOLIDATION (Section 61)

**Principle**: Where same mortgagor and mortgagee in multiple mortgages, mortgagee can insist on redemption of ALL or NONE

**Scenario:**
- A mortgaged Property X to B (debt Rs. 10 lakhs)
- Later, A mortgaged Property Y to B (debt Rs. 5 lakhs)
- Now Property X value increased to Rs. 30 lakhs
- Property Y value declined to Rs. 3 lakhs
- A wants to redeem Property X only (valuable)

**Consolidation Right:**
- B can insist: "Redeem BOTH or NONE"
- Prevents cherry-picking by mortgagor

**Conditions (All Must Be Satisfied):**
1. Same mortgagor and mortgagee in both mortgages
2. Dates of mortgage different (not same transaction)
3. Legal redemption date passed for both
4. No contract excluding consolidation
5. Equities equal on both sides

**Effect:**
- Mortgagor must redeem all mortgages to B
- Cannot redeem selectively

**Modern Relevance:** Rarely applied (courts reluctant)

## Priority Between Mortgages

### General Rule: First in Time, First in Right

If Property has multiple mortgages:
1. First mortgage: First priority
2. Second mortgage: Second priority
3. Third mortgage: Third priority

**Priority Order for Sale Proceeds:**

Property sold for Rs. 80 lakhs:
- Court costs: Rs. 5 lakhs
- First mortgagee (debt Rs. 50 lakhs): Rs. 50 lakhs (full recovery)
- Second mortgagee (debt Rs. 30 lakhs): Rs. 25 lakhs (partial recovery)
- Third mortgagee (debt Rs. 10 lakhs): Rs. 0 (nothing)
- Mortgagor: Rs. 0 (nothing)

Second mortgagee can claim Rs. 5 lakhs deficit from mortgagor (personal liability)

### Exception 1: Registered vs Unregistered

**Section 27 Registration Act:**
- Registered document gets priority over unregistered
- Even if unregistered is earlier in time

**Example:**
- 1st June: Mortgage to A (NOT registered)
- 1st July: Mortgage to B (REGISTERED)
- Priority: B > A (registration prevails)

### Exception 2: Actual Notice vs Constructive Notice

**Constructive Notice:**
- Registration creates constructive notice to world
- Subsequent mortgagee deemed to have notice

**Actual Notice:**
- If subsequent mortgagee has actual knowledge of prior unregistered mortgage
- Cannot claim bona fide purchaser protection

### Exception 3: Tacking of Further Advances (Section 94)

**Scenario:**
- 1st January: Mortgage to Bank A (Rs. 20 lakhs)
- 1st June: Mortgage to Bank B (Rs. 10 lakhs)
- 1st December: Bank A gives further advance (Rs. 10 lakhs)

**Question**: Is Bank A's further Rs. 10 lakhs prior to Bank B OR subsequent?

**Tacking Rule (Section 94):**
Bank A's further advance gets priority over B IF:
1. Original mortgage to A reserved right to further advances, OR
2. A had NO NOTICE of B's mortgage when making further advance

If either condition satisfied:
- Bank A's total debt (Rs. 30 lakhs) ranks ahead of Bank B

If neither condition satisfied:
- Bank A's original Rs. 20 lakhs ranks first
- Bank B's Rs. 10 lakhs ranks second
- Bank A's further Rs. 10 lakhs ranks THIRD

## Common Scenarios and Solutions

### Scenario 1: Mortgagor Defaults - Simple Mortgage

**Facts:**
- A borrowed Rs. 50 lakhs from B
- Simple mortgage on property
- A defaulted after paying Rs. 10 lakhs
- Outstanding: Rs. 40 lakhs + Rs. 5 lakhs interest
- Property value: Rs. 60 lakhs

**B's Remedies:**

Option 1: **Sue for Debt** (Section 67 - Personal Action)
- File suit for Rs. 45 lakhs debt
- Decree against A personally
- Execute against A's ALL assets (not just mortgaged property)
- Limitation: 12 years from default

Option 2: **Sue for Sale** (Section 69 - Action Against Security)
- File suit for sale of mortgaged property
- Preliminary decree declaring debt
- Final decree for sale
- Property sold through court
- Limitation: 12 years from default

Option 3: **Both**
- Sue for debt + sale simultaneously
- Recover from property sale
- If deficit, recover from A's other assets

**Procedure:**
1. Send legal notice (demand payment within 30 days)
2. File suit in court (where property situated)
3. Obtain preliminary decree (usually 6-12 months)
4. Obtain final decree (6 months after preliminary decree)
5. Court sale conducted
6. Sale proceeds applied to debt

---

### Scenario 2: Equitable Mortgage - Title Deeds Deposited

**Facts:**
- A deposited title deeds with Bank B in Mumbai
- Loan Rs. 30 lakhs
- No written mortgage deed
- A defaulted

**Legal Position:**
- Valid equitable mortgage (Section 58(f))
- Mumbai is notified town
- Deposit with intent to secure = Mortgage

**Bank B's Rights:**
- Same as simple mortgage
- Sue for sale (Section 67)
- Cannot take possession without court order
- Cannot sell under power of sale (unless separate agreement)

**Bank B's Procedure:**
1. File suit for sale (Order XXXIV CPC)
2. Preliminary decree
3. Final decree
4. Court sale

**Defense by A:**
- Challenge intent (argue deposit not for security)
- Difficult if loan documents mention deposit

---

### Scenario 3: English Mortgage - Foreclosure vs Sale

**Facts:**
- A executed English mortgage to B
- Debt Rs. 40 lakhs
- Property value Rs. 80 lakhs
- A defaulted

**B's Options:**

Option 1: **Foreclosure**
- File suit for foreclosure
- Preliminary decree (6 months to redeem)
- Final decree: B becomes absolute owner
- B gets property worth Rs. 80 lakhs for Rs. 40 lakhs debt (windfall)
- A loses property + Rs. 40 lakhs equity

Option 2: **Sale**
- File suit for sale (Section 67)
- Property sold for Rs. 80 lakhs
- B recovers Rs. 40 lakhs
- A gets Rs. 40 lakhs surplus

**Court's Approach:**
- Courts prefer Sale over Foreclosure (Section 67 proviso)
- Foreclosure granted only if:
  - Property value = Debt (no surplus)
  - Special circumstances
  - Mortgagor consents

**A's Defense:**
- File redemption suit before foreclosure decree
- Deposit Rs. 40 lakhs + interest in court
- Redeem mortgage

---

### Scenario 4: Multiple Mortgages - Priority Dispute

**Facts:**
- Property value: Rs. 1 crore
- 1st Mortgage to Bank A: Rs. 60 lakhs (registered)
- 2nd Mortgage to Bank B: Rs. 30 lakhs (registered)
- 3rd Mortgage to C: Rs. 20 lakhs (registered)
- Total debt: Rs. 1.10 crores
- Property sold for Rs. 1 crore

**Distribution:**
1. Court costs: Rs. 2 lakhs
2. Bank A (1st mortgagee): Rs. 60 lakhs (FULL)
3. Bank B (2nd mortgagee): Rs. 30 lakhs (FULL)
4. C (3rd mortgagee): Rs. 8 lakhs (PARTIAL)
5. Mortgagor: Rs. 0

**C's Position:**
- Shortfall: Rs. 12 lakhs
- Can sue mortgagor for deficit (personal liability)
- Limitation: 12 years from sale

**Prevention for C:**
- Before taking 3rd mortgage, verify prior mortgages
- Ensure adequate security margin
- Demand mortgagor to partially discharge prior mortgages

---

### Scenario 5: Usufructuary Mortgage - Possession Issue

**Facts:**
- A executed usufructuary mortgage to B
- Debt Rs. 20 lakhs
- B entitled to possession and rents (Rs. 1 lakh/month)
- 10 years period
- After 3 years, A obstructs B's possession

**B's Remedies:**

Remedy 1: **Sue for Possession**
- File suit under Order XXI Rule 35 CPC
- Claim possession based on mortgage deed
- Obtain possession decree

Remedy 2: **Sue for Injunction**
- Restrain A from obstructing possession
- Claim damages for lost rents

**Calculation:**
- Debt: Rs. 20 lakhs
- Rent received (3 years): Rs. 36 lakhs
- Debt satisfied (Rs. 36 lakhs > Rs. 20 lakhs)
- Mortgage redeemed automatically

**If Debt Satisfied:**
- A can claim return of possession
- B must vacate and return possession
- File redemption suit if B refuses

---

### Scenario 6: Second Mortgage Without Notice of First

**Facts:**
- 1st March: A mortgaged property to Bank B (Rs. 30 lakhs) - NOT registered
- 1st June: A mortgaged same property to Bank C (Rs. 20 lakhs) - REGISTERED
- A did not disclose B's mortgage to C
- C had no notice of B's mortgage

**Priority:**
- Section 27 Registration Act: Registered prevails over unregistered
- C (registered) > B (unregistered)

**C's Position:**
- First priority despite being later in time
- If property sold, C gets Rs. 20 lakhs first

**B's Position:**
- Second priority
- Can challenge if proves C had actual notice

**B's Remedies Against A:**
- Sue A for fraud/misrepresentation
- Section 420 IPC (cheating)
- Claim damages

**Prevention:**
- B should have registered mortgage (priority protection)
- C should have verified encumbrances (due diligence)

## Integration with Other Laws

### SARFAESI Act 2002 (Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act)

**Applicability:**
- Banks, Financial Institutions, Asset Reconstruction Companies
- Debt Rs. 1 lakh or more (earlier Rs. 1 lakh, now increased periodically)

**Section 13 Enforcement:**

**Step 1: Notice** - Bank sends notice under Section 13(2)
- Demand full payment within 60 days

**Step 2: Possession** - If not paid, bank can take possession (Section 13(4))
- Without court intervention
- Take symbolic/actual possession

**Step 3: Sale** - Bank can sell property
- After taking possession
- Public auction or private sale
- Apply proceeds to debt

**Borrower's Remedies:**
- File application before Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT) within 45 days
- Section 17: DRT can stay SARFAESI action if prima facie case
- Appeal to Debt Recovery Appellate Tribunal (DRAT)

**Advantages of SARFAESI:**
- Quick enforcement (no court suit needed)
- Cost-effective
- 60-90 days timeline

**Limitations:**
- Not applicable to agricultural land
- Not applicable if debt < threshold
- DRT can stay action

### Code of Civil Procedure 1908 - Order XXXIV

**Special Procedure for Mortgage Suits:**

Order XXXIV Rules:
- Suit for sale/foreclosure follows special procedure
- Preliminary decree mandatory (declaring debt + redemption period)
- Final decree after redemption period expires
- Mortgagor can redeem even after preliminary decree (before final decree)

**Valuation for Court Fees:**
- Based on debt amount (not property value)
- Court fees on Rs. 50 lakhs debt: Calculate as per state rules

**Jurisdiction:**
- Where property situated (Section 16 CPC)
- Cannot be waived

### Registration Act 1908

**Section 17:** Mortgage deed must be registered
**Section 49:** Unregistered mortgage void
**Section 27:** Registered document prevails over unregistered

**Exception:** Equitable mortgage (deposit of title deeds)
- No written instrument
- Registration not required
- But memorandum of deposit CAN be registered (optional)

### Limitation Act 1963

**Article 132:** Suit on mortgage
- **Limitation:** 12 years from date when money sued for becomes due
- For sale suit: 12 years from due date
- For foreclosure: 12 years from due date
- For redemption: 30 years from accrual of right

**Redemption Limitation:**
- Equity of redemption continues for 30 years (Article 61)
- After 30 years, mortgagor's right barred

### Income Tax Act 1961

**Interest Deduction:**
- Section 24(b): Interest on home loan deductible (up to Rs. 2 lakhs for self-occupied)
- Section 80EE: Additional deduction for first-time home buyers

**Capital Gains on Sale:**
- Section 50C: Sale value = Higher of actual or stamp duty value
- Mortgagee's sale: Capital gains implications for mortgagor

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Not Registering Mortgage Deed
**Mistake:** Execute mortgage deed but don't register (thinking possession/payment sufficient)
**Consequence:** Mortgage void (Section 49 Registration Act); no security created
**Solution:** ALWAYS register mortgage deed within 4 months

### 2. Equitable Mortgage Outside Notified Towns
**Mistake:** Deposit title deeds in non-notified town thinking equitable mortgage created
**Consequence:** No mortgage (Section 58(f) limited to notified towns); only personal liability
**Solution:** Verify town is notified; if not, execute registered mortgage deed

### 3. Ignoring Prior Mortgages
**Mistake:** Take second mortgage without verifying encumbrances
**Consequence:** Discover first mortgage later; become junior mortgagee with second priority
**Solution:** Obtain encumbrance certificate; verify all prior mortgages before lending

### 4. Over-Valuation of Property
**Mistake:** Lend Rs. 80 lakhs on property worth Rs. 60 lakhs (thinking value will appreciate)
**Consequence:** On default, sale proceeds insufficient to recover debt
**Solution:** Maintain LTV ratio (75-90%); obtain professional valuation

### 5. Not Taking Title Insurance
**Mistake:** Lend large amount without title insurance/verification
**Consequence:** Discover title defect later; mortgagor's title invalid; cannot enforce mortgage
**Solution:** Conduct thorough title verification (30-40 years chain); take title insurance for high-value properties

### 6. Foreclosure Without Court Approval
**Mistake:** Mortgagee (English mortgage) takes possession and claims ownership without court decree
**Consequence:** Mortgagor files suit; court orders sale instead; mortgagee loses possession
**Solution:** Always obtain court decree for foreclosure (even if English mortgage)

### 7. Sale Under Power Without Notice
**Mistake:** Bank sells property under power of sale without proper notice/advertisement
**Consequence:** Sale voidable; mortgagor challenges sale; court sets aside
**Solution:** Follow procedure strictly: Notice (60 days) → Advertisement (30 days) → Auction → Account

### 8. Accepting Mortgaged Property Without Redemption
**Mistake:** Buyer purchases property without checking encumbrances; discovers mortgage later
**Consequence:** Mortgagee enforces security; buyer loses property
**Solution:** Obtain encumbrance certificate; insist on mortgage discharge before purchase

### 9. Mortgaging Agricultural Land
**Mistake:** Bank takes mortgage on agricultural land without verifying restrictions
**Consequence:** Cannot enforce under SARFAESI (agricultural land excluded); lengthy court process
**Solution:** Verify land classification; avoid agricultural land OR convert to non-agricultural first

### 10. Not Updating Mortgage Records
**Mistake:** Mortgage redeemed but not discharged in Sub-Registrar records
**Consequence:** Encumbrance certificate shows subsisting mortgage; buyer refuses to purchase
**Solution:** After redemption, obtain discharge certificate; update Sub-Registrar records

# Indian Property Law: Sale of Immovable Property

## Purpose
This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on sale of immovable property under the Transfer of Property Act 1882 (Sections 54-55) and Registration Act 1908 (Section 17). It covers the distinction between contract to sell vs sale, essential elements of valid sale, registration requirements, transfer of ownership and risk, doctrine of part performance (Section 53A), remedies for breach, and practical considerations for property transactions.

## Activation Triggers
Use this protocol when:
- Client wants to buy/sell immovable property
- Analyzing validity of property sale deed/agreement to sell
- Dispute over transfer of ownership or possession
- Registration issues in property transactions
- Breach of contract to sell (buyer/seller defaulted)
- Specific performance claim for property sale
- Applying Section 53A part performance doctrine
- Title verification and due diligence for property purchase
- Stamp duty and registration fee calculation
- Remedies evaluation for defective property transactions

## Core Principles

### 1. Section 54: Sale Defined

**"Sale is a transfer of ownership in exchange for a price paid or promised or part-paid and part-promised"**

Essential Elements of Sale:
1. **TRANSFER OF OWNERSHIP** - Title passes from seller to buyer
2. **IMMOVABLE PROPERTY** - Land, buildings, hereditary allowances, rights
3. **PRICE** - Money consideration (not barter = exchange)
4. **PAID OR PROMISED** - Present or future payment
5. **TWO PARTIES** - Seller (transferor) and Buyer (transferee)

### 2. Section 54 Registration Requirement

**Immovable Property of Value Rs. 100 or Upwards:**

**Registration is MANDATORY** (Section 17 Registration Act 1908)

```
Section 54 TPA:
"Sale of immovable property of the value of one hundred rupees and upwards 
can be made only by a REGISTERED INSTRUMENT."

Section 49 Registration Act:
"No document required to be registered shall affect any immovable property 
comprised therein, or confer any power to adopt, or be received as evidence 
of any transaction affecting such property, unless it has been REGISTERED."
```

**Effect of Non-Registration:**
- Sale deed is VOID (not voidable)
- No transfer of title
- Cannot be used as evidence
- Buyer gets NO rights in property
- Only remedy: Return of price + damages

**Below Rs. 100:**
- Registration NOT mandatory
- Delivery of possession sufficient

### 3. Contract to Sell vs Sale

| Aspect | Contract to Sell | Sale |
|--------|------------------|------|
| **Nature** | Executory contract | Executed transfer |
| **Ownership** | Remains with seller | Passes to buyer |
| **Risk** | With seller | With buyer |
| **Registration** | NOT required | MANDATORY (Rs. 100+) |
| **Breach Remedy** | Damages (not specific performance usually) | Specific performance available |
| **Rights Created** | Personal right (in personam) | Proprietary right (in rem) |
| **Time of Transfer** | Future date | Immediately on registration |

**Agreement to Sell** = Contract to Sell
- Creates obligation to execute sale deed in future
- Does NOT transfer ownership
- Usually not registrable (no transfer effected)
- Breach → Damages OR specific performance (if property unique)

**Sale Deed** = Sale
- Transfers ownership immediately on registration
- Buyer becomes owner
- Registrable and registration mandatory
- Breach after registration → Different remedies (possession, damages)

### 4. Transfer of Ownership and Risk

**Section 55(4)(b): Seller's Duty to Disclose Material Defects**

```
Seller must disclose:
- Material defects in property
- Material defects in title
- Encumbrances (mortgage, charge, lease)
- Litigation affecting property
```

**Non-Disclosure:**
- Buyer can rescind contract OR
- Claim compensation for loss

**Section 55(6)(a): Risk Passes with Ownership**

```
"The risk of loss from fire, tempest, or other inevitable accident, 
passes to the buyer as soon as the property is transferred"
```

**Unless otherwise agreed:**
- Before registration: Risk with SELLER
- After registration: Risk with BUYER

**Practical Implication:**
- Property damaged before registration → Seller bears loss
- Property damaged after registration → Buyer bears loss
- Insurance should transfer on registration

## Framework

### Phase 1: Pre-Transaction Due Diligence

**Step 1: Title Verification (30-40 Years Chain)**

Verify seller has:
1. **MARKETABLE TITLE** - Free from defects, litigation, encumbrances
2. **CHAIN OF TITLE** - Examine 30-40 years back (preferably to root of title)
3. **NO ADVERSE POSSESSION** - Verify actual possession

Documents to Examine:
- Sale deeds (entire chain)
- Gift deeds, partition deeds, will, succession certificate
- Encumbrance certificate (15 years minimum)
- Property tax receipts
- Possession certificate
- Building approval plans (if construction)
- No Objection Certificates (NOC) from society/authority

**Step 2: Legal Status Check**

1. **Seller's Capacity** - Section 7 TPA
   - Major (18+ years)
   - Sound mind
   - Entitled to transfer (owner, co-owner, authorized agent)

2. **Property Status**
   - NOT agricultural land (if buyer non-agriculturist - state laws)
   - NOT government/municipal land
   - NOT disputed property (pending litigation)
   - NOT subject to statutory restriction

3. **Encumbrances Check** - Encumbrance Certificate
   - Mortgage
   - Charge
   - Lease
   - Easement
   - Pending litigation

**Step 3: Physical Inspection**

1. **Site Visit** - Verify boundaries, possession, encroachment
2. **Survey** - Demarcation of property
3. **Construction Status** - Match approved plans with actual construction

### Phase 2: Agreement to Sell

**Step 1: Draft Agreement to Sell**

Mandatory Clauses:
1. **PARTIES** - Full name, address, age of seller and buyer
2. **PROPERTY DESCRIPTION** - Survey number, area, boundaries, address
3. **SALE PRICE** - Total consideration
4. **ADVANCE/EARNEST** - Amount paid (usually 10-20%)
5. **BALANCE PAYMENT** - Mode and timeline
6. **COMPLETION DATE** - Date for executing sale deed
7. **SELLER'S REPRESENTATIONS**
   - Owner of property
   - Marketable title
   - No encumbrances
   - Vacant possession
8. **BUYER'S OBLIGATIONS**
   - Pay balance on completion
   - Bear stamp duty and registration charges (unless otherwise agreed)
9. **DEFAULT CLAUSES**
   - Seller default: Return double earnest + damages
   - Buyer default: Forfeit earnest
10. **DISPUTE RESOLUTION** - Arbitration/jurisdiction clause

**Step 2: Execute Agreement (Optional Registration)**

Agreement to sell:
- Registration NOT mandatory (no transfer of ownership)
- But ADVISABLE to register if substantial advance paid
- Registered agreement: Admissible as evidence + notice to world

**Step 3: Token Money/Earnest**

Legal Nature:
- Part payment of price
- Secures performance of contract
- Forfeited if buyer defaults (unless otherwise agreed)
- Returned double if seller defaults (customary, not statutory)

Amount: Usually 10-20% of sale price

### Phase 3: Execution of Sale Deed

**Step 1: Draft Sale Deed**

Mandatory Contents:
1. **DATE AND PLACE** - Of execution
2. **PARTIES** - Seller (Vendor) and Buyer (Purchaser)
3. **RECITALS** - Background, title history
4. **PROPERTY DESCRIPTION** - Schedule with survey numbers, boundaries, area
5. **CONSIDERATION** - Total price
6. **RECEIPT CLAUSE** - Acknowledgment of payment
7. **TRANSFER CLAUSE** - "Sell, transfer, convey all rights, title, interest"
8. **POSSESSION CLAUSE** - Delivery of possession
9. **SELLER'S COVENANTS**
   - Marketable title
   - No encumbrances
   - Right to sell
   - Quiet enjoyment
10. **ATTESTATION** - Two witnesses

**Step 2: Stamp Duty Payment**

Governed by Indian Stamp Act 1899 + State Stamp Acts

**Stamp Duty Rate**: Varies by state (3-10% of consideration/market value, whichever higher)

Examples:
- Maharashtra: 5-6% + 1% metro cess
- Karnataka: 5% + 0.1% surcharge
- Delhi: 4-7%
- Tamil Nadu: 7%

**Market Value vs Consideration:**
- Stamp duty on HIGHER of:
  - Agreement value (consideration)
  - Circle rate/guidance value/ready reckoner rate

**Payment Mode:**
- E-stamping (preferred)
- Non-judicial stamp paper (being phased out)

**Penalty for Under-Stamping:**
- 2% penalty per month (maximum 200% of deficit)
- Document inadmissible as evidence

**Step 3: Registration (Section 17 Registration Act)**

**Mandatory Documents for Registration:**

Seller Must Produce:
1. **SALE DEED** - Original + 2 copies
2. **IDENTITY PROOF** - Aadhaar, PAN, passport
3. **TITLE DOCUMENTS** - Previous sale deed, chain documents
4. **ENCUMBRANCE CERTIFICATE** - 15 years
5. **TAX RECEIPTS** - Property tax paid up to date
6. **NOC** - Society NOC (if apartment/flat)
7. **POWER OF ATTORNEY** - If selling through agent

Buyer Must Produce:
1. **IDENTITY PROOF** - Aadhaar, PAN, passport
2. **ADDRESS PROOF**

**Registration Procedure:**

Step 1: **Book Appointment** - Online slot booking
Step 2: **Physical Appearance** - Seller, buyer, 2 witnesses at Sub-Registrar office
Step 3: **Document Verification** - Sub-Registrar verifies documents
Step 4: **Thumb Impression/Signature** - Parties sign sale deed
Step 5: **Registration Fee Payment** - 1% of consideration (usually)
Step 6: **Registration Completion** - Deed entered in register, serial number assigned
Step 7: **Certified Copies** - Collect registered sale deed (usually 1-7 days)

**Limitation for Registration:**
- Section 25 Registration Act: Within 4 MONTHS from execution
- Extension: Additional 4 months (with penalty)
- After 8 months: Registration refused

**Effect of Registration:**
- Transfer of ownership from seller to buyer
- Buyer becomes absolute owner
- Deed admissible as evidence
- Notice to world

### Phase 4: Post-Registration

**Step 1: Possession Delivery**

Methods:
1. **SYMBOLIC** - Delivery of keys
2. **ACTUAL** - Physical handover and vacation by seller
3. **CONSTRUCTIVE** - Attornment (acknowledgment by tenant/occupier)

**Step 2: Mutation of Records**

Update revenue records:
1. **Revenue Department** - Mutation in land records (7/12, 8A, RTC)
2. **Municipal Corporation** - Property tax records
3. **Society** - Transfer of membership (if apartment)
4. **Utility Providers** - Electricity, water connection transfer

**Step 3: Tax Implications**

**For Seller:**
- **Capital Gains Tax** - LTCG/STCG on profit
  - LTCG: 20% with indexation (holding >24 months)
  - STCG: As per income tax slab (holding <24 months)
- **Exemptions** - Section 54, 54EC, 54F Income Tax Act

**For Buyer:**
- **TDS Deduction** - Section 194IA: 1% TDS if consideration >Rs. 50 lakhs
- **Registration Fee** - 1% (approx) of consideration
- **Stamp Duty** - 3-10% (state-specific)

## Section 53A: Doctrine of Part Performance

**Section 53A TPA (Protection to Transferee)**

```
"Where any person contracts to transfer for consideration any immovable 
property by writing signed by him or on his behalf, and the transferee has, 
in part performance of the contract:
(a) Taken possession of the property, OR
(b) Being already in possession, continues in possession in part performance, 
    and has done some act in furtherance,

And the transferee has performed or is willing to perform his part of the 
contract, then the transferor CANNOT enforce against the transferee any 
right in respect of the property inconsistent with the contract."
```

**Purpose**: Prevent fraud/unjust enrichment where buyer acts on agreement but seller refuses to execute registered sale deed

**Essential Conditions (All Must Be Satisfied):**

1. **CONTRACT** - Valid contract to transfer immovable property
2. **CONSIDERATION** - For consideration (not gratuitous)
3. **WRITING** - Contract in WRITING signed by transferor/agent
4. **POSSESSION** - Transferee taken possession OR continues in possession
5. **ACT IN FURTHERANCE** - Transferee done some act in furtherance
6. **READINESS TO PERFORM** - Transferee ready and willing to perform his part

**What Section 53A DOES:**
- Prevents seller from evicting buyer
- Prevents seller from transferring to third party
- Protects buyer's possession

**What Section 53A DOES NOT DO:**
- Does NOT transfer ownership (only registered deed can)
- Does NOT create title in buyer
- Does NOT make unregistered deed valid

**Remedy**: Buyer must file suit for **SPECIFIC PERFORMANCE** to compel seller to execute registered sale deed

### Case Law on Section 53A

**Nathulal v. Phoolchand (SC 1969)**
Holding: Section 53A is shield, not sword - defensive, not offensive remedy

**Ramesh Hirachand v. Sonamal (SC 1988)**
Holding: "Act in furtherance" means substantial investment/alteration beyond mere possession

Examples of "Act in Furtherance":
- Construction on property
- Substantial repairs/renovations
- Payment of substantial part of price
- Payment of property tax, maintenance charges

NOT Sufficient:
- Mere possession alone
- Payment of token amount
- Temporary occupation

## Remedies for Breach

### A. Buyer's Remedies (Seller Defaulted)

**Scenario 1: Seller Refuses to Execute Sale Deed After Agreement**

Remedy 1: **SPECIFIC PERFORMANCE** (Section 10 Specific Relief Act 1963)

Conditions:
- Property is UNIQUE (land/building inherently unique)
- Damages inadequate compensation
- Contract valid and enforceable
- Buyer ready and willing to perform

Decree: Court orders seller to execute registered sale deed

Limitation: 3 YEARS from date fixed for performance (Article 54 Limitation Act)

**Remedy 2: DAMAGES** (Section 73 Contract Act)

If specific performance not granted:
- Return of earnest/advance
- Interest on amount paid
- Damages for loss of bargain (if market price increased)
- Compensation for expenses (stamp duty on agreement, legal fees)

**Remedy 3: COMPENSATION** (Section 55(5) TPA)

Seller wrongfully neglects/refuses to complete sale:
- Compensation for loss/damage from breach
- Return of deposit with interest

**Scenario 2: Seller's Title Defective (Discovered After Agreement)**

Section 55(1)(c) TPA: Seller's Duty to Disclose

Buyer's Options:
1. **RESCIND CONTRACT** - Get back deposit + expenses
2. **CLAIM COMPENSATION** - For diminution in value
3. **SPECIFIC PERFORMANCE WITH ABATEMENT** - Compel sale with price reduction

**Scenario 3: Seller Sells to Third Party (Double Sale)**

If Buyer's Agreement Registered:
- Agreement creates notice to world
- Third party purchaser is subsequent transferee
- Buyer can claim specific performance (priority)

If Buyer's Agreement NOT Registered:
- Third party bona fide purchaser for value gets priority (Section 27 Registration Act)
- Buyer's remedy: Damages against seller only

### B. Seller's Remedies (Buyer Defaulted)

**Scenario 1: Buyer Refuses to Pay Balance/Take Deed**

Remedy 1: **SPECIFIC PERFORMANCE** (Compel Buyer to Complete)

Conditions:
- Seller ready with marketable title
- Buyer without valid excuse
- Court's discretion (rarely granted against buyer)

Usually NOT granted because:
- Contract of sale is personal
- Cannot force buyer to purchase unwillingly
- Damages adequate remedy for seller

**Remedy 2: DAMAGES** (Section 73 Contract Act)

Seller can claim:
- Forfeit earnest (if contract permits)
- Damages for loss of bargain (if market price declined)
- Interest on unpaid price
- Expenses incurred (stamp duty on readiness deed)

**Remedy 3: FORFEIT DEPOSIT**

If contract contains forfeiture clause:
- Forfeit earnest/deposit
- Additional damages if actual loss exceeds deposit

Section 74 Contract Act Ceiling:
- Liquidated damages enforceable
- Court may reduce if unreasonable

**Scenario 2: Buyer Delays Payment**

Seller's Rights:
1. **CHARGE INTEREST** - As per agreement OR 6% p.a. if not specified
2. **RESCIND CONTRACT** - After reasonable notice
3. **FORFEIT DEPOSIT** - If contract permits

Time is of Essence:
- If contract says "time is of essence" → Strict timelines
- If not stated → Reasonable time allowed

## Common Scenarios and Solutions

### Scenario 1: Unregistered Sale Deed

**Facts**: 
- Buyer paid full price
- Seller executed sale deed
- Possession delivered
- But sale deed NOT registered

**Legal Position**:
- Sale is VOID (Section 54 + Section 49 Registration Act)
- No transfer of ownership
- Seller remains legal owner
- Buyer has NO title

**Buyer's Remedies**:
1. **File suit for specific performance** - Compel seller to register deed OR execute fresh registered deed
2. **Claim under Section 53A** - If conditions satisfied (possession + writing + consideration)
3. **Claim damages** - Return of price + interest + compensation

**Risk**: If seller sells to third party and registers deed, third party gets title (unless buyer's claim registered)

### Scenario 2: Agreement to Sell + Possession + Part Payment

**Facts**:
- Agreement to sell executed (not registered)
- Buyer paid 50% price
- Buyer in possession for 3 years
- Buyer constructed boundary wall
- Now seller refuses to execute sale deed

**Legal Position**:
- No ownership transferred (only agreement)
- Section 53A protection available (writing + possession + consideration + act in furtherance)

**Buyer's Remedies**:
1. **File suit for specific performance** - Compel registered sale deed
2. **Rely on Section 53A** - Protect possession against seller's eviction
3. **Claim damages** - If specific performance refused

**Limitation**: 3 years from date fixed for completion OR 3 years from date seller repudiated contract

### Scenario 3: Power of Attorney Sale

**Facts**:
- Seller gives Power of Attorney to Agent
- Agent executes "sale deed" on PoA basis
- Buyer pays full price and takes possession
- Sale deed registered
- But property NOT directly transferred (PoA holder sold)

**Legal Position**:
- **PoA Sale is NOT Sale** (Suraj Lamp & Industries v. State of Haryana - SC 2012)
- Power of Attorney does NOT transfer ownership
- Only principal (owner) can transfer property
- Registration of PoA deed does NOT validate transfer

**Risk**: 
- Seller (principal) can revoke PoA anytime
- Seller can sell property to third party
- Buyer has NO title

**Valid PoA Transaction**:
- PoA holder acts on behalf of principal
- Sale deed executed in NAME of principal (not PoA holder)
- Principal must sign sale deed (or PoA must be irrevocable)

**Buyer's Protection**:
- Insist on sale deed signed by OWNER (not PoA holder)
- Verify PoA is registered and irrevocable
- Take indemnity from principal

### Scenario 4: Circle Rate vs Agreement Value

**Facts**:
- Agreement value: Rs. 50 lakhs
- Circle rate/guidance value: Rs. 70 lakhs
- Buyer pays stamp duty on Rs. 50 lakhs only

**Legal Position**:
- Stamp duty payable on HIGHER of agreement value or circle rate
- Under-stamping attracts penalty

**Consequences**:
- Sub-Registrar may refuse registration OR
- Register with deficiency notice
- Penalty: 2% per month (max 200% of deficit)
- Document inadmissible as evidence until deficiency paid

**Solution**:
- Pay stamp duty on circle rate (Rs. 70 lakhs)
- Avoid litigation/penalty

### Scenario 5: Joint Property Sale by One Co-Owner

**Facts**:
- Property owned by A and B (co-owners)
- A sells entire property to C without B's consent
- C pays full price, gets registered sale deed from A
- B refuses to recognize sale

**Legal Position**:
- Section 7 TPA: Co-owner can transfer only his SHARE
- A can validly sell only his 50% share
- Transfer of B's share VOID (A not entitled to transfer)

**C's Rights**:
- Becomes co-owner with B (owns A's 50% share)
- Cannot claim exclusive ownership
- Can file partition suit to separate shares

**B's Rights**:
- Remains co-owner (50% share intact)
- Can file suit to cancel sale deed qua B's share

**Prevention**:
- Buyer must verify ALL co-owners consent
- All co-owners must execute sale deed

### Scenario 6: Sale During Pending Litigation

**Facts**:
- Property subject to pending partition suit
- During pendency, A sells property to B
- B gets registered sale deed
- Later, court orders partition

**Legal Position**:
- Section 52 TPA: Transfer pending litigation is subject to outcome
- B purchases subject to litigation risk
- If court rules against A, B's purchase fails

**Lis Pendens (Section 52 TPA)**:
```
"During pendency of any suit or proceeding relating to immovable property, 
transfer by any party to the suit does NOT affect rights of other party 
under decree or order."
```

**Effect**:
- B's purchase valid ONLY if A ultimately succeeds
- If A loses, B loses property

**Prevention**:
- Search for litigation (encumbrance certificate, court records)
- Avoid purchasing property under litigation
- If necessary, wait for litigation conclusion

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Relying on Unregistered Sale Deed
**Mistake**: Buyer pays price, takes possession, but does not register sale deed, thinking possession is sufficient

**Consequence**: No title transferred; seller can sell to third party

**Solution**: ALWAYS register sale deed (mandatory for property Rs. 100+)

### 2. Paying Without Title Verification
**Mistake**: Buyer pays full price based on seller's representation without verifying title documents

**Consequence**: Discovers defect in title later; cannot get refund if seller disappears

**Solution**: Conduct thorough due diligence BEFORE payment (30-40 years chain of title)

### 3. Ignoring Encumbrances
**Mistake**: Buyer does not obtain encumbrance certificate; later discovers mortgage/charge on property

**Consequence**: Property may be attached/sold by creditor

**Solution**: Obtain 15-year encumbrance certificate; verify no pending litigation/mortgage

### 4. PoA Sale Without Owner's Signature
**Mistake**: Buyer accepts sale deed signed by PoA holder (not owner) thinking PoA sale is valid

**Consequence**: Sale invalid (Suraj Lamp case); no title transferred

**Solution**: Insist on owner's signature OR irrevocable registered PoA with principal's acknowledgment

### 5. Under-Valuing to Save Stamp Duty
**Mistake**: Buyer and seller show lower consideration in sale deed to reduce stamp duty

**Consequence**: 
- Penalty for under-stamping (200% of deficit)
- Capital gains tax issue for seller (black money presumption)
- Registration may be refused

**Solution**: Show actual consideration OR circle rate (whichever higher); pay correct stamp duty

### 6. Not Registering Agreement to Sell
**Mistake**: Buyer pays substantial advance (30-50%) but does not register agreement to sell

**Consequence**: If seller sells to third party, third party gets priority (no notice of buyer's claim)

**Solution**: Register agreement to sell if substantial amount paid; creates notice to world

### 7. Accepting Possession Without Verification
**Mistake**: Buyer takes possession without verifying seller's actual possession rights (may be tenant, not owner)

**Consequence**: Discovers later that seller was tenant; no ownership rights

**Solution**: Verify seller's possession documents (title deeds, tax receipts, possession certificate)

### 8. Ignoring Building Approvals
**Mistake**: Buyer purchases property/flat without verifying building approvals, completion certificate

**Consequence**: 
- Illegal construction (may be demolished)
- Cannot get home loan
- Cannot register electricity/water connection

**Solution**: Verify building plan approval, completion certificate, occupancy certificate

### 9. Not Conducting Physical Survey
**Mistake**: Buyer relies on documents alone without physical survey/demarcation

**Consequence**: Discovers encroachment, boundary disputes after purchase

**Solution**: Conduct physical survey; verify boundaries match sale deed description

### 10. Delaying Registration Beyond 4 Months
**Mistake**: Buyer executes sale deed but delays registration beyond 4 months (thinking extension automatic)

**Consequence**: 
- Penalty for late registration (4-8 months)
- Registration refused after 8 months
- Must execute fresh deed

**Solution**: Register within 4 months of execution; if delayed, apply for extension immediately

## Case Law Digest

### 1. Specific Performance - Property is Unique

**Nathulal v. Phoolchand (SC 1969)**

**Facts**: Buyer sought specific performance for purchase of land. Seller argued damages adequate remedy.

**Holding**: 
- "Every piece of land or house property is unique, and no two pieces of land or houses are identical."
- Specific performance is normal remedy for land/building contracts
- Damages inadequate because property has special value to buyer

**Principle**: Specific performance readily granted for immovable property (presumed unique)

**Application**: Buyer can file specific performance suit; court will likely decree performance

---

### 2. Section 53A - Part Performance Doctrine

**Ramesh Hirachand Kundanmal v. Municipal Corporation (SC 1988)**

**Facts**: Buyer took possession under agreement to sell, paid substantial amount, and made constructions. Seller refused to execute sale deed.

**Holding**:
- Section 53A requires "act in furtherance" beyond mere possession
- Construction/substantial investment qualifies as act in furtherance
- Seller cannot evict buyer who has Section 53A protection

**Principle**: "Act in furtherance" means substantial alteration/investment, not mere possession

**Application**: Payment of price + construction = Section 53A protection; seller cannot evict

---

### 3. PoA Sale Invalid

**Suraj Lamp & Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana (SC 2012)**

**Facts**: Property sold through Power of Attorney without owner executing sale deed. Buyer claimed valid sale based on registered PoA deed.

**Holding**:
- "General Power of Attorney (GPA) sale is NOT a sale at all"
- Only registered sale deed by OWNER can transfer title
- PoA holder acts on behalf of principal; cannot transfer property in his own name
- Registration of PoA does not validate transfer

**Principle**: PoA sale does NOT transfer ownership; only registered sale deed by owner valid

**Application**: Insist on sale deed signed by owner (not PoA holder); PoA sale creates no title

---

### 4. Stamp Duty on Circle Rate

**Rajasthan High Court - Multiple Cases**

**Facts**: Sale deeds executed showing lower consideration than circle rate. Registrar demanded stamp duty on circle rate.

**Holding**:
- Stamp duty payable on HIGHER of actual consideration or circle rate/market value
- Under-stamping makes document inadmissible as evidence
- Penalty of 2% per month (max 200%) on deficit

**Principle**: Stamp duty based on circle rate, not agreement value (if circle rate higher)

**Application**: Pay stamp duty on circle rate even if agreement value lower; avoid penalty

---

### 5. Lis Pendens - Section 52 TPA

**Jayaram Mudaliar v. Ayyaswami (SC 1973)**

**Facts**: Property sold during pending partition suit. Buyer claimed bona fide purchaser protection.

**Holding**:
- Section 52 TPA: Transfer during pending litigation subject to outcome
- Doctrine of lis pendens applies from suit filing to decree
- Buyer's rights subject to court's final order

**Principle**: Purchase during litigation is at buyer's risk; subject to court decree

**Application**: Verify no pending litigation (encumbrance certificate); avoid purchasing litigated property

---

### 6. Co-Owner's Sale Invalid Qua Other Co-Owner

**Atma Ram v. Tek Singh (SC 1973)**

**Facts**: One co-owner sold entire property without consent of other co-owners. Buyer claimed entire property.

**Holding**:
- Co-owner can transfer only his UNDIVIDED SHARE
- Transfer of other co-owners' shares VOID (Section 7 TPA)
- Buyer becomes co-owner (with share of selling co-owner only)

**Principle**: Co-owner cannot transfer more than his share; consent of ALL co-owners required for entire property sale

**Application**: Verify ALL co-owners execute sale deed; single co-owner sale transfers only his share

---

### 7. Seller's Duty to Disclose Defects

**Gulam Abbas v. State of U.P. (Allahabad HC 1981)**

**Facts**: Seller sold property without disclosing pending acquisition proceedings. Buyer discovered later that property was being acquired.

**Holding**:
- Section 55(1)(c) TPA: Seller must disclose material defects in title
- Non-disclosure of pending acquisition is material defect
- Buyer entitled to rescind contract and claim damages

**Principle**: Seller has duty to disclose ALL material defects affecting property/title

**Application**: Seller must disclose litigation, acquisition, encumbrances; non-disclosure = rescission right

---

### 8. Time Not of Essence (Unless Specified)

**Saradamani Kandappan v. S. Rajalakshmi (SC 2011)**

**Facts**: Agreement to sell fixed completion date. Buyer delayed payment by 3 months. Seller rescinded contract, claiming time was of essence.

**Holding**:
- Time is NOT of essence in property contracts (unless expressly stated)
- Reasonable delay acceptable
- Seller must give notice and reasonable time before rescinding

**Principle**: Mere specification of date does not make time of essence; reasonable delay excusable

**Application**: If time not expressly of essence, buyer gets reasonable time even after deadline

---

### 9. Unregistered Sale Deed + Possession = No Title

**Mangat Ram v. Sardar Meharban Singh (SC 1987)**

**Facts**: Buyer paid full price, took possession, but sale deed not registered. Seller sold to third party and registered deed. First buyer claimed title based on possession.

**Holding**:
- Section 54 TPA + Section 49 Registration Act: Registration MANDATORY
- Unregistered sale deed creates NO title
- Possession without registration does not defeat registered transferee

**Principle**: Unregistered sale deed is void; possession alone insufficient to create title

**Application**: MUST register sale deed; unregistered deed + possession gives no protection against subsequent registered sale

---

### 10. Specific Performance - Discretionary Remedy

**Kailash Nath v. Prabhu Dayal (SC 2000)**

**Facts**: Buyer sought specific performance. Buyer had delayed payment and not performed his part of contract.

**Holding**:
- Specific performance is DISCRETIONARY remedy (Section 20 Specific Relief Act)
- Buyer must come with clean hands
- If buyer defaulted or not ready and willing to perform, court may refuse specific performance

**Principle**: Specific performance not automatic right; court's discretion based on plaintiff's conduct

**Application**: Buyer must show readiness and willingness to perform; delay/default may disentitle specific performance

---

# Protocol: Transfer of Property Act - Basics (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-PROPERTY-TRANSFER-BASICS  
**Domain**: Property Law  
**Subdomain**: Transfer of Property  
**Version**: 1.0.0  
**Last Updated**: 2025-12-06

## Purpose

This protocol provides foundational guidance on the Transfer of Property Act 1882, covering basic principles, types of transfers, competency to transfer, and essential requirements for valid property transfers in India.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "What is Transfer of Property Act?"
- Need to understand property transfer basics
- Competency to transfer questions
- Types of property transfers
- Valid transfer requirements
- Voidable vs void transfers

## Core Principles

### 1. Transfer of Property Defined (Section 5)

```
Section 5: "Transfer of property means an act by which a living person conveys 
property, in present or in future, to one or more other living persons, or to 
himself and one or more other living persons"

Essential Elements:

1. LIVING PERSON (Transferor):
   - Natural person OR juristic person (company, trust)
   - NOT testamentary disposition (will - governed by Succession Act)

2. CONVEYANCE:
   - Act of transferring rights
   - Voluntarily (not by operation of law)

3. PROPERTY:
   - Immovable (land, buildings, hereditary allowances, rights in land)
   - Movable (goods, actionable claims, money)

4. LIVING PERSON (Transferee):
   - Natural person OR juristic person
   - OR to himself + others (self-transfer + joint)

5. PRESENT OR FUTURE:
   - Immediate transfer (possession now)
   - Future transfer (possession later, but interest vests now)

NOT Transfer of Property:
✗ Will/testamentary disposition (Succession Act)
✗ Transfer by operation of law (inheritance, insolvency, escheat)
✗ Transfer of mere license to use (not property interest)
```

### 2. What Can Be Transferred (Section 6)

```
Section 6: Property of any kind may be transferred

Transferable Property:
✓ Immovable property (land, buildings)
✓ Movable property (goods)
✓ Actionable claims (debts, benefits of contracts)
✓ Contingent interests (Section 21)
✓ Leasehold interests
✓ Life estates
✓ Future interests (vested, contingent)

Section 6 Exceptions (Cannot Be Transferred):

(a) SPES SUCCESSIONIS (Expectancy of Succession):
    - Chance of inheritance (before death)
    - Example: "A's son B cannot transfer his expectancy to inherit A's property 
      while A is alive"
    - After inheritance vests (A dies) → B can transfer inherited property

(b) MERE RIGHT TO SUE:
    - Right to sue for damages (unliquidated)
    - Exception: Actionable claim (liquidated debt) CAN be transferred

(c) MERE RIGHT OF RE-ENTRY:
    - Landlord's right to re-enter leased property (cannot be transferred separately)
    - BUT lease with right of re-entry CAN be transferred

(d) EASEMENT SEPARATELY FROM DOMINANT HERITAGE:
    - Easement (right of way) attached to land
    - Cannot transfer easement without transferring land
    - Easement transfers WITH land automatically

(e) RESTRICTED INTEREST:
    - Property expressly forbidden from transfer by law
    - Example: Agricultural land (some states restrict transfer to non-agriculturists)

(f) RIGHT TO FUTURE MAINTENANCE:
    - Widow's right to maintenance (personal, not transferable)

(g) GOVERNMENT PENSIONS:
    - Non-transferable (except as permitted by government rules)
```

### 3. Who Can Transfer (Section 7)

```
Section 7: Every person competent to contract and entitled to transferable 
property, or authorized to dispose of transferable property not his own, is 
competent to transfer such property either wholly or in part

Competent Transferor:

1. COMPETENT TO CONTRACT (Section 11 Indian Contract Act):
   - Age of majority (18 years)
   - Sound mind
   - Not disqualified by law

2. ENTITLED TO PROPERTY:
   - Owner (absolute owner can transfer whole)
   - Limited owner (life estate holder can transfer his interest only)
   - Co-owner (can transfer his undivided share)

3. AUTHORIZED TO TRANSFER:
   - Agent (with power of attorney)
   - Guardian (with court permission for minor's property)
   - Manager (of HUF property, with consent of coparceners for permanent transfers)
   - Trustee (in accordance with trust deed)
   - Court-appointed receiver/administrator

NOT Competent:
✗ Minor (void transfer - Mohori Bibee principle)
✗ Person of unsound mind (void)
✗ Person not entitled (stranger to property)
✗ Unauthorized person (agent without power)
```

## TYPES OF TRANSFERS (Sections 54-123)

### Overview of Transfer Types

```
TRANSFER OF PROPERTY ACT 1882 COVERS:

1. SALE (Section 54):
   - Transfer for price (money consideration)
   - Absolute transfer of ownership

2. MORTGAGE (Section 58):
   - Transfer of interest in property as security for loan
   - Ownership reverts on repayment

3. LEASE (Section 105):
   - Transfer of right to enjoy property for specified time
   - Rent consideration
   - Ownership remains with lessor

4. EXCHANGE (Section 118):
   - Transfer of property for other property
   - Barter (not money)

5. GIFT (Section 122):
   - Transfer without consideration
   - Gratuitous transfer

6. ACTIONABLE CLAIM (Section 130):
   - Transfer of debt, benefit of contract
   - Assignment

NOT Covered (but related):
- Will: Succession Act (testamentary)
- Partition: Partition Act (division among co-owners)
- Easement creation: Easements Act
```

### 1. Sale (Section 54)

```
Section 54: "Sale is a transfer of ownership in exchange for price paid or 
promised or part-paid and part-promised"

Essential Elements:

1. TRANSFER OF OWNERSHIP:
   - Seller transfers all rights in property
   - Buyer becomes absolute owner

2. PRICE (Consideration):
   - Money (not goods - that's exchange)
   - Paid, promised, or part-paid & part-promised

3. TWO PARTIES:
   - Seller (transferor)
   - Buyer (transferee)

Contract of Sale vs Sale:
- Contract of Sale (Section 54): Agreement to sell (future transfer)
- Sale (Section 54): Immediate transfer of ownership

Registration Requirement (Section 54):

IMMOVABLE PROPERTY:

Value Rs. 100 or more:
- MUST be registered (Section 54 + Section 17(1)(b) Registration Act 1908)
- If not registered → VOID (no title passes)

Value less than Rs. 100:
- May be by registered instrument OR delivery of possession (rare)

MOVABLE PROPERTY:
- No registration required (delivery sufficient)

Writing Requirement:
- Immovable property sale: MUST be in writing + registered
- Movable property sale: Oral contract sufficient (but writing advisable)
```

### 2. Mortgage (Section 58)

```
Section 58: "Mortgage is the transfer of an interest in specific immovable 
property for the purpose of securing the payment of money advanced or to be 
advanced by way of loan, an existing or future debt, or the performance of an 
engagement which may give rise to a pecuniary liability"

Key Elements:

1. SECURITY (Not Sale):
   - Property interest transferred as security
   - Ownership reverts to mortgagor on repayment

2. LOAN/DEBT:
   - Money advanced or to be advanced
   - OR existing debt (mortgage to secure prior debt)

3. IMMOVABLE PROPERTY:
   - Mortgage only of immovable property (land, buildings)
   - Movable property: "Pledge" (not mortgage)

4. REDEMPTION RIGHT:
   - Mortgagor can redeem (get property back) by repaying loan

Types of Mortgages (Section 58):

(a) SIMPLE MORTGAGE:
    - No possession delivered
    - Mortgagee gets right to sell property if default

(b) MORTGAGE BY CONDITIONAL SALE:
    - Ostensible sale with condition to re-convey on repayment
    - Sale defeasible on payment

(c) USUFRUCTUARY MORTGAGE:
    - Mortgagee gets possession + right to receive rent/profits
    - Rent applied toward interest + principal (no separate payment)

(d) ENGLISH MORTGAGE:
    - Mortgagor binds to repay by certain date
    - On default, property absolutely mortgagee's
    - On payment, property re-conveyed

(e) MORTGAGE BY DEPOSIT OF TITLE DEEDS (Equitable Mortgage):
    - Delivery of title deeds to mortgagee
    - In certain towns (notified - Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, etc.)
    - Creates equitable mortgage (enforceable)

(f) ANOMALOUS MORTGAGE:
    - Combination of types
    - Hybrid features
```

### 3. Lease (Section 105)

```
Section 105: "Lease of immovable property is a transfer of a right to enjoy 
such property, made for a certain time, express or implied, or in perpetuity, 
in consideration of a price paid or promised, or of money, a share of crops, 
service or any other thing of value, to be rendered periodically or on 
specified occasions to the transferor by the transferee"

Essential Elements:

1. TRANSFER OF RIGHT TO ENJOY (Not Ownership):
   - Lessee gets possession + right to use property
   - Lessor retains ownership

2. CERTAIN TIME:
   - Fixed period (1 year, 5 years, 99 years)
   - OR perpetuity (rare - certain states allow)

3. CONSIDERATION (Rent):
   - Money (monthly, yearly)
   - OR share of crops (agricultural lease)
   - OR service
   - OR other thing of value

4. LESSOR and LESSEE:
   - Lessor: Owner transferring enjoyment
   - Lessee: Person receiving enjoyment

Registration Requirement (Section 107):

Lease for > 12 months (more than one year):
- MUST be registered (Section 107 + Registration Act)
- If not registered → Valid only as license (not lease)

Lease for ≤ 12 months:
- No registration required (oral or written)

Transfer of Leasehold:
- Lessee can transfer lease (unless prohibited in lease deed - Section 108)
- Sublease permitted (unless prohibited)
```

### 4. Exchange (Section 118)

```
Section 118: "When two persons mutually transfer the ownership of one thing for 
the ownership of another, neither thing nor both things being money only, the 
transaction is called an 'exchange'"

Essential Elements:

1. MUTUAL TRANSFER:
   - A transfers property X to B
   - B transfers property Y to A

2. OWNERSHIP TRANSFER:
   - Complete transfer (not lease/mortgage)

3. NOT MONEY:
   - At least one property must be non-money
   - If both money → Not exchange (two sales)

Example:
A owns land in Mumbai. B owns land in Delhi. A and B exchange properties.
→ Exchange (not sale)

Registration:
- If immovable property: MUST be registered (Section 118 applies Section 54)
```

### 5. Gift (Section 122)

```
Section 122: "Gift is the transfer of certain existing movable or immovable 
property made voluntarily and without consideration, by one person, called the 
donor, to another, called the donee, and accepted by or on behalf of the donee"

Essential Elements:

1. TRANSFER:
   - Voluntary (not forced)
   - Existing property (not future property - exception: Section 124 onerous gift)

2. WITHOUT CONSIDERATION:
   - No price paid
   - Gratuitous (love, affection, natural love)

3. ACCEPTANCE:
   - Donee must accept (express or implied)
   - During donor's lifetime (for immovable property gift)

4. DONOR and DONEE:
   - Living persons (not testamentary - that's will)

Registration Requirement (Section 123):

IMMOVABLE PROPERTY GIFT:
- MUST be registered (Section 123 + Registration Act)
- If not registered → VOID (no transfer)

MOVABLE PROPERTY GIFT:
- Delivery of possession sufficient (no registration)
- Writing advisable (evidence)

Revocability:
- Gift generally IRREVOCABLE (once completed)
- Exception: Onerous gift (Section 127 - can be repudiated if burden too heavy)
```

## ESSENTIAL REQUIREMENTS FOR VALID TRANSFER

### 1. Competency (Section 7)

```
Transferor Must Be:

1. Competent to Contract:
   - Major (18+ years)
   - Sound mind
   - Not disqualified

2. Entitled to Property:
   - Owner OR limited interest holder OR authorized person

Invalid Transfers:

Minor's Transfer:
- VOID (Mohori Bibee principle)
- Minor cannot transfer property
- Even with guardian consent (unless court permission for minor's benefit)

Unsound Mind:
- VOID (lacks capacity)

Unauthorized Person:
- VOID (nemo dat quod non habet - cannot give what you don't have)
```

### 2. Consideration (Where Required)

```
Sale (Section 54):
- Consideration REQUIRED (price)
- If no price → Gift (not sale)

Mortgage (Section 58):
- Loan/debt is consideration

Lease (Section 105):
- Rent/share of crops is consideration

Exchange (Section 118):
- Mutual transfer of properties (consideration in form of counter-property)

Gift (Section 122):
- NO consideration required (gratuitous)
```

### 3. Registration (Where Mandatory)

```
Section 17 Registration Act 1908:

MUST Be Registered:

1. Sale of immovable property (value Rs. 100+)
2. Mortgage of immovable property (except deposit of title deeds in notified towns)
3. Lease for > 12 months
4. Gift of immovable property
5. Exchange of immovable property
6. Transfer of actionable claim

Effect of Non-Registration (Section 49 Registration Act):
- Document VOID (does not pass title)
- Cannot be used as evidence of transfer
- Cannot be admitted in court

Exception - Deposit of Title Deeds (Equitable Mortgage):
- In notified towns (Mumbai, Delhi, Kolkata, Chennai, etc.)
- Delivery of title deeds creates equitable mortgage
- No registration required (Section 58(f) proviso)
```

### 4. Writing (Where Required)

```
Immovable Property Transfers:
- MUST be in writing (instrument executed)
- Signed by transferor
- Registered (if value Rs. 100+)

Oral Transfer:
- Immovable property: VOID (if value Rs. 100+)
- Movable property: Valid (delivery sufficient)

Exceptions:
- Movable property gifts: Delivery sufficient (oral valid)
- Leases ≤ 12 months: Oral valid
```

### 5. Delivery/Possession (Where Required)

```
Movable Property:
- Delivery of possession essential (for sale, gift)
- Symbolical delivery permitted (keys to warehouse)

Immovable Property:
- Delivery of possession NOT essential for sale (ownership passes by registration)
- BUT possession delivery essential for:
  * Lease (lessee must get possession)
  * Usufructuary mortgage (mortgagee gets possession)
  * Gift of movables (physical delivery)
```

## VOID VS VOIDABLE TRANSFERS

### Void Transfers (Section 7, 10, 11, 23-26)

```
VOID = No legal effect from beginning

Void Transfers:

1. By Minor (Mohori Bibee):
   - Minor's transfer VOID ab initio
   - Cannot be ratified on attaining majority

2. By Unauthorized Person:
   - Stranger transferring property he doesn't own
   - Nemo dat quod non habet

3. Not Registered (when mandatory):
   - Section 49 Registration Act - document void

4. Opposed to Public Policy (Section 23):
   - Transfer for illegal purpose
   - Transfer defeating provisions of law

5. Uncertain (Section 14):
   - Transfer of uncertain interest
   - Example: "To A, if he marries" (too uncertain)

6. Perpetual Restriction on Alienation (Section 10):
   - "To A, but A cannot transfer to anyone"
   - Absolute restriction void (property must be alienable)
   - Limited restriction valid (e.g., "Cannot transfer for 5 years")

Effect:
- No title passes
- Property remains with transferor
- Transferee cannot claim ownership
```

### Voidable Transfers (Section 19A, 39, 53, 64 Contract Act)

```
VOIDABLE = Valid until rescinded by aggrieved party

Voidable Transfers:

1. Coercion (Section 15 Contract Act):
   - Transfer obtained by threat/force
   - Transferor can rescind

2. Undue Influence (Section 16 Contract Act):
   - Dominant position used to obtain unfair advantage
   - Transferor can rescind

3. Fraud (Section 17 Contract Act):
   - False representation inducing transfer
   - Transferor can rescind

4. Misrepresentation (Section 18 Contract Act):
   - Innocent false statement
   - Transferor can rescind

5. Transfer by Person with Limited Interest (Section 43-55):
   - Person with limited interest (life estate) transferring absolute interest
   - Voidable at option of true owner (after limited interest expires)

Effect:
- Valid until rescinded
- Aggrieved party can:
  * Rescind (cancel transfer, restore status quo)
  * Affirm (continue with transfer)
- If rescinded → Property reverts to transferor
```

## TRANSFER BY UNAUTHORIZED PERSONS (Section 43-55)

### Section 43: Transfer by Unauthorized Person

```
Section 43: "Where, with the consent, express or implied, of the persons 
interested in immovable property, a person is the ostensible owner of such 
property and transfers the same for consideration, the transfer shall not be 
voidable on the ground that the transferor was not authorized to make it, 
provided the transferee, after taking reasonable care to ascertain that the 
transferor had power to make the transfer, has acted in good faith"

Doctrine of Ostensible Owner (Feeding the Estoppel):

Elements:

1. Consent of True Owner:
   - True owner allows unauthorized person to appear as owner
   - Express or implied consent

2. Ostensible Ownership:
   - Unauthorized person appears as owner
   - Documents, possession create appearance

3. Transfer for Consideration:
   - Bona fide purchaser pays value

4. Good Faith + Reasonable Care:
   - Purchaser believes transferor is owner
   - Makes reasonable inquiry

Effect:
- Transfer valid (binds true owner)
- True owner estopped from challenging

Example:
A (true owner) allows B (A's manager) to appear as owner (B's name on documents with A's consent). B sells property to C (bona fide purchaser). 
→ Sale valid (A estopped from claiming property)
```

### Section 41: Transfer by Tenant

```
Section 41: Transfer by person ostensibly owning but having limited interest

If lessee transfers as if owner (conceals lease), transferee gets lessee's 
interest only (not ownership).

Exception: If lessor estopped (allowed lessee to appear as owner) → Transfer 
binds lessor (Section 43)
```

## COMMON SCENARIOS

### Scenario 1: Minor's Transfer (Void)

```
Facts:
- A (17 years old, minor) owns land (inherited from father)
- A sells land to B for Rs. 50 lakhs
- Sale deed registered

Issue: Is sale valid?

Analysis:
- Section 7: Only persons competent to contract can transfer
- Section 11 Contract Act: Minor not competent
- Mohori Bibee: Minor's agreement VOID ab initio

Result:
- Sale VOID (no title passes to B)
- A can reclaim land (even after attaining majority)
- B cannot enforce sale deed
- B's remedy: Sue guardian/parents for fraud (if they facilitated sale)
```

### Scenario 2: Unregistered Sale of Immovable Property (Void)

```
Facts:
- A agrees to sell land to B for Rs. 10 lakhs
- B pays Rs. 10 lakhs
- A executes sale deed (not registered)
- A delivers possession to B

Issue: Is B the owner?

Analysis:
- Section 54: Sale of immovable property (value Rs. 100+) MUST be registered
- Section 49 Registration Act: Unregistered document VOID
- Possession delivery irrelevant (ownership passes by registered document, not possession)

Result:
- Sale VOID (B is NOT owner)
- A remains owner (title not passed)
- B's remedies:
  * Specific performance (compel A to register sale deed)
  * Damages for breach of contract
- B in possession as licensee/trespasser (not owner)
```

### Scenario 3: Ostensible Owner Transfer (Valid - Section 43)

```
Facts:
- A (true owner) allows B (A's manager) to manage property
- Property documents in B's name (with A's consent for convenience)
- B sells property to C for Rs. 1 crore
- C believes B is owner (checks documents, all in B's name)
- C pays value, acts in good faith

Issue: Can A reclaim property from C?

Analysis:
- Section 43: Transfer by ostensible owner
- Elements satisfied:
  * A's consent (allowed B to appear as owner)
  * B ostensible owner (documents in B's name)
  * C bona fide purchaser (reasonable care + good faith)
  * Consideration paid

Result:
- Sale VALID (C gets title)
- A estopped from claiming (Section 43)
- A's remedy: Sue B for fraud/breach of trust
- C protected (bona fide purchaser)
```

### Scenario 4: Gift Without Registration (Void)

```
Facts:
- A gifts land to B (daughter) out of love
- Gift deed executed (signed)
- NOT registered
- B takes possession

Issue: Is gift valid?

Analysis:
- Section 122-123: Gift of immovable property MUST be registered
- Section 49 Registration Act: Unregistered document VOID

Result:
- Gift VOID (B does NOT become owner)
- A remains owner (title not passed)
- B's possession does not confer ownership (mere licensee)
- Solution: Execute fresh gift deed + register
```

## CASE LAW

### 1. Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (PC 1903)
```
Facts: Minor's agreement to mortgage property
Held:
- Minor's agreement VOID ab initio (not voidable)
- Cannot be ratified on attaining majority
- Minor cannot transfer property validly
- Section 11 Contract Act: Minor not competent to contract
```

### 2. Ram Coomar Coondoo v. Chunder Canto Mookerjee (PC 1876)
```
Facts: Doctrine of feeding the estoppel (Section 43)
Held:
- True owner who allows unauthorized person to appear as owner → Estopped
- Bona fide purchaser from ostensible owner gets valid title
- Estoppel feeds the grant (subsequent acquisition by transferor enures to transferee)
```

### 3. Suraj Lamp & Industries v. State of Haryana (SC 2012)
```
Facts: Unregistered sale deed
Held:
- Section 54 + Section 17(1)(b) Registration Act: Sale of immovable property (Rs. 100+) MUST be registered
- Section 49: Unregistered document VOID (no title passes)
- Possession without registration does not confer ownership
```

### 4. Khwaja Muhammad Khan v. Husaini Begum (PC 1910)
```
Facts: Transfer by person with limited interest (life estate)
Held:
- Life estate holder transferring absolute interest → Transfer valid to extent of life estate only
- After life estate holder's death, property reverts to remainderman
- Purchaser gets only limited interest (life estate), not absolute ownership
```

# Indian Property Law: Registration of Documents (Registration Act 1908)

## Purpose
This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on the Registration Act 1908, covering mandatory registration requirements (Section 17), optional registration (Section 18), effects of registration/non-registration (Sections 47-49), priority between registered and unregistered documents (Section 27), time limits for registration (Section 23-25), procedure for registration (Section 32-35), and practical considerations for property document registration.

## Activation Triggers
Use this protocol when:
- Determining if document requires registration
- Sale deed, gift deed, mortgage deed, lease deed execution
- Understanding consequences of non-registration
- Priority disputes between registered/unregistered documents
- Late registration application (beyond 4 months)
- Registration procedure and requirements
- Refusal of registration by Sub-Registrar
- Presentment of document for registration
- Certified copies application
- Cancellation/rectification of registered document
- Title verification based on registered documents
- Enforcement of unregistered document

## Core Principles

### 1. Purpose of Registration

**Why Registration Required:**

1. **Publicity** - Notice to world about transactions
2. **Authentication** - Government verification of execution
3. **Preservation** - Permanent record maintenance
4. **Priority** - Determines priority between competing claims
5. **Evidence** - Admissibility in court
6. **Prevention of Fraud** - Reduces fraudulent transactions

**Section 47 - Registration Creates Constructive Notice:**
```
"Every registered document shall operate as notice to all persons of the 
contents thereof from the time of its registration."
```

**Effect:**
- Registered document gives notice to world
- Subsequent buyers/mortgagees deemed to have knowledge
- Cannot claim ignorance

### 2. Section 17: Documents of Which Registration is COMPULSORY

**Section 17(1) - Mandatory Registration:**

**Compulsorily Registrable Documents:**

**(a) Instruments of GIFT of Immovable Property**
- Gift of immovable property (any value)
- Must be registered
- Non-registration: Gift VOID

**(b) Other NON-TESTAMENTARY Instruments Transferring Immovable Property of Value Rs. 100+**
- **Sale deed** - Rs. 100 or more
- **Mortgage deed** - All mortgages (except deposit of title deeds)
- **Exchange deed** - Rs. 100 or more
- **Lease deed** - Period > 12 months OR reserving rent > 12 months

**(c) Non-Testamentary Instruments Transferring ANY INTEREST in Immovable Property Rs. 100+**
- Transfer of easement (perpetual OR > 1 year)
- Transfer of mining lease, water rights

**(d) Acknowledgment of Debt/Liability > Rs. 100 if Property Charged**
- Document acknowledging debt secured by immovable property

**(e) Powers of Attorney Authorizing Alienation of Immovable Property**
- Power of Attorney to sell, mortgage, gift property
- General/special power

**(f) Leases of Immovable Property - Period > 12 Months**
- Lease exceeding 12 months from execution date
- Lease reserving rent for period > 12 months

**(g) Certain Special Documents**
- Adoption deed (if immovable property involved)
- Award by arbitration (if immovable property)

**Section 17(2) - Additional Compulsory Documents:**

**(a) Documents Rectifying/Affecting Registered Document**
- Supplementary deed
- Variation deed
- Rectification deed

**Summary Table:**

| Document Type | Registration Required? | Value Threshold |
|---------------|------------------------|-----------------|
| **Sale deed** | YES | Rs. 100+ |
| **Gift deed** | YES | Any value |
| **Mortgage deed** | YES | All mortgages |
| **Equitable mortgage (deposit title)** | NO | NA |
| **Lease** | YES | >12 months |
| **Lease** | NO | ≤12 months |
| **Easement** | YES | >1 year OR perpetual |
| **Power of Attorney (sale/mortgage)** | YES | Any value |
| **Will** | NO | NA (probate required) |
| **Agreement to sell** | NO | Optional |
| **Partition deed** | NO | Optional (Section 18) |

### 3. Section 18: Documents of Which Registration is OPTIONAL

**Section 18 - Optionally Registrable:**

**(a) Instruments of No Value (Documents Not Required by Section 17)**
- Agreement to sell
- Memorandum of understanding
- Affidavit

**(b) Will (Testamentary Documents)**
- Optional to register
- Usually not registered (probate obtained after death)

**(c) Decree or Final Order of Court**
- Can be registered for creating notice

**(d) Instrument of Partition**
- Partition deed (co-owners dividing property)
- Registration optional but advisable

**Why Register Optional Documents:**
- Creates public notice
- Admissible as evidence
- Priority protection
- Preservation

**Example:**
- Agreement to sell: Optional
- But advisable if substantial advance paid
- Registration creates notice to prevent seller's double sale

### 4. Section 23-25: Time Limit for Registration

**Section 23 - General Rule:**

**Within 4 MONTHS** from date of execution

**Execution Date:**
- Date when document signed/executed
- NOT date when transaction completed

**Example:**
- Sale deed executed on 1st January 2025
- Must be registered by 1st May 2025 (4 months)

**Section 25 - Extension:**

**Additional 4 Months (With Penalty)**

- Application with late fee/penalty
- Discretion of Registrar
- Maximum total: 8 MONTHS from execution

**After 8 Months:**
- Registration REFUSED
- Document becomes unregistrable
- Effect: Void for purposes requiring registration

**Calculation:**
- Exclude day of execution
- Include day of presentment

**Section 24 - Special Cases (Extended Time):**

**(a) Document Executed Outside India:**
- 4 months from arrival in India

**(b) Document Detained for Stamp Duty Adjudication:**
- Time during adjudication excluded

**(c) Document Executed by Person Abroad:**
- 4 months from receipt in India by attorney

### 5. Section 47-49: Effect of Registration and Non-Registration

**Section 47 - Effect of REGISTRATION:**

```
"Every registered document shall operate as notice to all persons of the 
contents thereof from the time of its registration."
```

**Benefits of Registration:**

1. **Constructive Notice to World**
   - All persons deemed to have knowledge
   - Subsequent buyers cannot claim ignorance

2. **Admissible as Evidence (Section 47-A)**
   - Certified copy admissible in court
   - Presumption of genuineness

3. **Priority Protection (Section 27)**
   - Registered document prevails over unregistered

4. **Legal Effect (Transfer of Title)**
   - Ownership transferred (if sale/gift)
   - Interest created (if mortgage/lease)

**Section 49 - Effect of NON-REGISTRATION:**

```
"No document required by Section 17 to be registered shall:
(a) Affect any immovable property comprised therein, OR
(b) Confer any power to adopt, OR  
(c) Be received as evidence of any transaction affecting such property or 
    conferring such power,

UNLESS it has been REGISTERED."
```

**Consequences of Non-Registration (If Registration Mandatory):**

**Consequence 1: VOID as to Property Transfer**
- No effect on immovable property
- Ownership NOT transferred
- Interest NOT created

**Example:**
- Unregistered sale deed (Rs. 1 crore property)
- Buyer paid full price, took possession
- But ownership NOT transferred (seller still owner)
- Buyer has NO legal title

**Consequence 2: INADMISSIBLE as Evidence**
- Cannot be produced in court as proof
- Cannot rely on unregistered document

**Example:**
- Dispute over property ownership
- Plaintiff produces unregistered sale deed
- Court: Inadmissible (Section 49)
- Plaintiff loses

**Consequence 3: NO Power Conferred**
- Power of attorney (unregistered) confers NO power
- Sale by unregistered PoA holder = VOID

**Exception - Collateral Purpose:**
- Section 49 proviso: Unregistered document can be used for COLLATERAL purpose
- Not to prove transfer, but to prove other facts

**Example (Collateral Purpose):**
- Unregistered sale deed cannot prove transfer
- BUT can prove:
  - Defendant admitted debt (if recital mentions debt)
  - Parties' relationship
  - Part payment of consideration

### 6. Section 27: Priority Between Registered and Unregistered Documents

**Section 27:**

```
"Every document registered under this Act shall take effect as against any 
unregistered document relating to the same property and executed on or after 
the date of registration."
```

**Rule: REGISTERED PREVAILS over UNREGISTERED**

**Even if:**
- Unregistered document earlier in time
- Unregistered party paid consideration
- Unregistered party took possession

**Priority Order:**

1. **Registered document** (first priority)
2. **Unregistered document** (no priority)

**Example 1:**
- 1st January: A sells property to B (sale deed NOT registered)
- 1st March: A sells same property to C (sale deed REGISTERED)
- Priority: C > B (C gets property)
- B's only remedy: Sue A for fraud/damages

**Example 2:**
- 1st January: A mortgages property to Bank B (mortgage deed NOT registered)
- 1st June: A mortgages same property to Bank C (mortgage deed REGISTERED)
- Priority: C > B (C has first charge)

**Exception - Actual Notice:**

If subsequent registered buyer/mortgagee has ACTUAL NOTICE of prior unregistered transaction:
- Courts may not grant protection
- Bona fide requirement

**Bona Fide Purchaser/Mortgagee:**
- Paid consideration
- No actual notice of prior transaction
- Section 27 protection applicable

**Practical Implication:**
- ALWAYS register documents requiring registration
- Registration gives priority protection

## Framework

### Phase 1: Pre-Registration

**Step 1: Determine Registration Requirement**

**Check if Document Falls Under Section 17 (Compulsory):**

- Sale deed (Rs. 100+)? → YES
- Gift deed? → YES
- Mortgage deed? → YES
- Lease (>12 months)? → YES
- Power of Attorney (sell/mortgage)? → YES
- Agreement to sell? → NO (optional)

**Step 2: Calculate Stamp Duty**

**Stamp Duty Varies by State:**
- Based on: Property value OR Circle rate/guidance value (whichever higher)
- Rate: 3-8% (state-specific)

**Mode of Payment:**
- E-stamping (preferred - online)
- Non-judicial stamp paper (being phased out)
- Franking

**Under-Stamping Penalty:**
- Section 33 Stamp Act + Registration Act
- Penalty: 2% per month (maximum 200% of deficit)
- Document inadmissible until deficit paid

**Step 3: Prepare Documents**

**Documents Required for Registration:**

**For Seller/Transferor:**
1. Original document (sale deed, gift deed, etc.)
2. Two photocopies
3. Identity proof (Aadhaar, PAN, passport)
4. Address proof
5. Previous title documents (chain)
6. Encumbrance certificate (15 years)
7. Property tax receipts
8. NOC (if applicable - society, bank, municipality)

**For Buyer/Transferee:**
1. Identity proof (Aadhaar, PAN)
2. Address proof
3. PAN card (mandatory)

**For Witnesses:**
1. Identity proof (2 witnesses required)

**Step 4: Book Appointment (Online)**

- Visit state's registration department website
- Select Sub-Registrar office (jurisdiction: where property situated)
- Book slot (date and time)
- Pay online appointment fee (if applicable)

**Jurisdiction (Section 28):**
- Property location determines registering office
- If property in multiple sub-districts → Any of those offices

### Phase 2: Registration Procedure

**Section 32-35: Procedure**

**Step 1: Presentment (Section 32)**

**Who Must Appear:**
- ALL executants (parties signing document)
- Two witnesses (attesting witnesses)
- If any party absent: Special procedure (Section 32(c))

**When to Appear:**
- Within 4 months of execution
- During office hours (usually 10 AM - 5 PM)

**Where to Appear:**
- Sub-Registrar office having jurisdiction

**Section 32 - Personal Appearance:**
- Executants must appear personally
- Identification verified
- Acknowledge execution (admit signature)

**Exceptions to Personal Appearance:**
- Person abroad/sick: Attorney can appear (with notarized PoA)
- Purdahnashin woman: Special provisions (Section 40)

**Step 2: Examination by Sub-Registrar (Section 34-35)**

**Sub-Registrar Examines:**

1. **Identity of Parties** - Aadhaar, PAN, passport, physical appearance
2. **Execution** - Parties admit execution/signature
3. **Free Consent** - No coercion, fraud (Section 35)
4. **Stamp Duty** - Adequate duty paid
5. **Property Description** - Complete and accurate
6. **Witnesses** - Two witnesses present and identified

**Section 35 - Examination Questions:**
- "Did you execute this document?"
- "Is this your signature?"
- "Did you execute this voluntarily without force?"

**If Satisfied:**
- Proceed to registration
- If not satisfied: Refuse registration

**Step 3: Signature/Thumb Impression (Section 59)**

- Parties sign document before Sub-Registrar
- If illiterate: Thumb impression
- Witnesses attest

**Step 4: Payment of Registration Fee**

**Registration Fee:**
- Usually 1% of property value (varies by state)
- Example: Property Rs. 50 lakhs, registration fee Rs. 5,000

**Additional Charges:**
- Scanning charges
- Copying charges
- Miscellaneous fees

**Step 5: Registration Entry (Section 51-52)**

**Sub-Registrar:**
- Enters document details in Register
- Serial number assigned
- Date and time of registration recorded
- Document filed in office

**Section 51 - Register Maintenance:**
- Book No. 1: Details of documents registered
- Indexed by year, serial number, parties' names

**Step 6: Endorsement and Return (Section 60)**

**Endorsement on Document:**
- Registration certificate
- Serial number, date, Sub-Registrar's signature

**Return of Document:**
- Original returned to party
- Registered and endorsed

**Timeline:**
- Usually same day (if no issues)
- May take 1-7 days for certified copies

**Step 7: Collect Certified Copies (Section 57)**

**Certified Copy:**
- Exact copy of registered document
- Certified by Sub-Registrar
- Admissible as evidence (Section 47-A)

**Application:**
- Any person can apply for certified copy
- Fee payable (per page)

**Uses:**
- Property verification
- Court proceedings
- Mortgage applications

### Phase 3: Post-Registration

**Step 1: Mutation of Records**

Update revenue records:
- Revenue Department (land records - 7/12, RTC, property card)
- Municipal Corporation (property tax)
- Electricity Board, Water Department

**Step 2: Title Verification**

For future transactions:
- Encumbrance certificate (shows all registered documents)
- Search title chain (30-40 years)

## Special Provisions

### 1. Section 32(c) - Registration Without All Executants Present

**Scenario:** One party abroad/sick/unavailable

**Procedure:**

**Option 1: Agent/Attorney Appears**
- Executant executes document abroad
- Notarized by Indian Embassy/Consulate
- Attorney appears before Sub-Registrar with notarized PoA
- Attorney acknowledges execution on behalf of executant

**Option 2: Document Registered Based on Acknowledgment Before Consular Officer**
- Executant appears before Indian Consular Officer abroad
- Consular officer records acknowledgment
- Document sent to India with certificate
- Registered without executant's appearance

### 2. Section 40 - Purdahnashin Women

**Special Provision for Women Observing Purdah:**

- Sub-Registrar (or authorized officer) goes to woman's residence
- Identifies woman (through known persons)
- Takes acknowledgment
- Registers document

**Rationale:** Respect for cultural practices + ensure free consent

### 3. Section 69 - Refusal to Register

**Grounds for Refusal:**

1. **Stamp Duty Deficient** - Insufficiently stamped
2. **Not Executed by Parties** - Parties deny execution
3. **Consent Vitiated** - Coercion, fraud evident
4. **Jurisdiction** - Property not within jurisdiction
5. **Time Limit Expired** - Beyond 8 months
6. **Incomplete Document** - Missing essential clauses

**Appeal:**
- Section 72: Appeal to District Registrar
- Further appeal to High Court

**Mandamus:**
- If refusal illegal/arbitrary
- Writ of mandamus in High Court

### 4. Section 47-A - Admissibility of Certified Copies

**Section 47-A:**

```
"Certified copies of registered documents shall be admissible in evidence 
without production of original."
```

**Practical Importance:**
- Original document may be lost/destroyed
- Certified copy from Sub-Registrar office = Sufficient
- Court accepts certified copy

**Procedure to Obtain Certified Copy:**

1. Apply to Sub-Registrar (where registered)
2. Provide document details (serial number, year, parties)
3. Pay fee (per page)
4. Collect certified copy (usually 1-3 days)

**Any Person Can Apply:**
- Not restricted to parties
- Third parties (e.g., prospective buyers) can obtain

### 5. Section 77 - Cancellation/Rectification

**Cancellation of Document:**

**Grounds:**
- Document registered by fraud/mistake
- Consent vitiated
- Property description incorrect

**Procedure:**
- File suit in civil court
- Obtain decree canceling registration
- Decree registered (cancels original registration)

**Rectification:**
- Minor errors/omissions
- Supplementary deed executed and registered

## Common Scenarios and Solutions

### Scenario 1: Sale Deed Executed But Not Registered Within 4 Months

**Facts:**
- Sale deed executed on 1st January 2025
- Stamp duty paid
- Buyer took possession
- Now 1st July 2025 (6 months elapsed)
- Registration not done

**Legal Position:**

**Time Limit:**
- 4 months: 1st January to 1st May (expired)
- Extension available: Up to 8 months (1st September 2025)

**Remedy:**
- Apply for late registration (Section 25)
- Pay late fee/penalty
- Register by 1st September 2025

**After 8 Months:**
- If not registered by 1st September 2025:
  - Registration REFUSED
  - Sale deed VOID (Section 49)
  - Buyer has NO title
  - Must execute FRESH sale deed

**Prevention:**
- Register within 4 months
- Set reminders, appoint lawyer to follow up

---

### Scenario 2: Unregistered Sale Deed vs Registered Sale Deed (Same Property)

**Facts:**
- 1st March: A sells property to B (Rs. 80 lakhs, sale deed NOT registered)
- B pays full price, takes possession
- 1st August: A sells same property to C (Rs. 70 lakhs, sale deed REGISTERED)
- C has no knowledge of B's purchase

**Legal Position:**

**Section 27 - Priority Rule:**
- Registered document (C) prevails over unregistered (B)
- Even though B earlier in time and paid more

**Outcome:**
- C gets property (registered sale deed)
- B loses property (unregistered sale deed void)

**B's Remedies:**
- Sue A for:
  - Fraud (Section 420 IPC)
  - Breach of contract (damages - Section 73 Contract Act)
  - Return of Rs. 80 lakhs + interest
- Civil suit for damages
- Criminal complaint (cheating)

**B Cannot Get Property:**
- Section 49: Unregistered document void
- Section 27: C's registration creates priority

**Lesson:**
- ALWAYS register sale deed immediately
- Registration is not formality - it's ESSENTIAL

---

### Scenario 3: Agreement to Sell (Unregistered) vs Sale Deed (Registered)

**Facts:**
- 1st January: A agrees to sell property to B (agreement to sell, NOT registered)
- B pays advance Rs. 20 lakhs (out of Rs. 1 crore)
- 1st June: A sells property to C (registered sale deed)
- C had no notice of B's agreement

**Legal Position:**

**Agreement to Sell:**
- Optional registration (Section 18)
- B did not register
- No constructive notice to C

**Sale Deed to C:**
- Registered (Section 17 compulsory)
- C becomes owner

**Priority:**
- Section 27: Registered sale deed (C) prevails
- Unregistered agreement (B) creates only personal right (not proprietary)

**B's Remedies:**
- Sue A for:
  - Specific performance (compel A to complete sale to B)
  - If specific performance not granted: Damages
  - Return of advance Rs. 20 lakhs + interest

**But B Cannot Get Property:**
- C already registered owner
- B's remedy limited to damages from A

**Prevention for B:**
- Register agreement to sell (creates notice)
- Take possession (Section 53A part performance protection)
- Complete sale quickly

**Prevention for C:**
- Title verification (encumbrance certificate shows registered documents only)
- Physical inspection (check if anyone else in possession)
- Ask seller to warrant no prior agreements

---

### Scenario 4: Stamp Duty Deficiency Discovered During Registration

**Facts:**
- Property value: Rs. 1 crore (as per circle rate)
- Sale deed shows consideration: Rs. 60 lakhs
- Stamp duty paid: 5% of Rs. 60 lakhs = Rs. 3 lakhs
- Sub-Registrar discovers deficiency (should be on Rs. 1 crore)

**Legal Position:**

**Stamp Duty Payable:**
- On HIGHER of:
  - Agreement value (Rs. 60 lakhs), OR
  - Circle rate/guidance value (Rs. 1 crore)
- Correct stamp duty: 5% of Rs. 1 crore = Rs. 5 lakhs
- Deficiency: Rs. 2 lakhs

**Sub-Registrar Action:**
- Refuse registration until deficiency paid
- OR register with deficiency notice

**Penalty:**
- Section 33 Stamp Act: 2% per month (max 200% of deficit)
- Immediate payment: Rs. 2 lakhs
- If delayed (say 6 months): Rs. 2 lakhs + (Rs. 2 lakhs × 2% × 6) = Rs. 2.24 lakhs

**Parties' Options:**

**Option 1: Pay Deficiency Immediately**
- Pay Rs. 2 lakhs + penalty
- Complete registration

**Option 2: Contest Circle Rate**
- Challenge circle rate (if excessive)
- Adjudication by Collector
- Risk: Delay, registration refused if circle rate upheld

**Prevention:**
- Check circle rate before execution
- Pay stamp duty on circle rate (even if agreement value lower)

---

### Scenario 5: Power of Attorney Sale - Registration Issues

**Facts:**
- A gives General Power of Attorney to Agent B (to sell property)
- B executes sale deed in favor of C
- B signs as "Attorney for A"
- Sale deed presented for registration

**Legal Position:**

**Section 17(1)(e) - PoA Registration:**
- Power of Attorney for sale must be REGISTERED
- If PoA not registered → Agent has NO power
- Sale by unregistered PoA holder = VOID

**Registration Procedure:**

1. **PoA Must Be Registered First**
   - A (principal) registers PoA in favor of B
   - PoA gives B power to sell

2. **Sale Deed Signed by Agent**
   - B signs sale deed as "Attorney for A"
   - Sale deed must recite PoA details (registration number, date)

3. **Registration of Sale Deed**
   - Sale deed registered
   - Sub-Registrar verifies PoA registered
   - If PoA not registered: Refuse sale deed registration

**Suraj Lamp Principle (SC 2012):**
- PoA sale (without registered PoA + sale deed in principal's name) is NOT valid sale
- Agent cannot transfer property in his own name
- Only principal (A) can transfer property

**Valid PoA Sale Requirements:**
1. PoA registered
2. Sale deed executed in PRINCIPAL'S name (A), signed by agent (B as attorney)
3. Sale deed registered

### Scenario 6: Rectification of Registered Document (Typographical Error)

**Facts:**
- Sale deed registered
- Property description contains typographical error (Survey No. 123 written as 132)
- Parties want to rectify

**Options:**

**Option 1: Supplementary Deed (Section 17(2))**
- Execute supplementary deed correcting error
- Refer to original sale deed
- Recite correction
- Register supplementary deed (compulsory - Section 17(2))

**Option 2: Court Decree**
- File suit for rectification
- Obtain decree correcting error
- Register decree

**Option 3: Endorsement (Minor Errors)**
- Apply to Sub-Registrar
- If minor clerical error (typo)
- Sub-Registrar may endorse correction

**Preferred Method:**
- Supplementary deed (quickest, simplest)

**Sample Supplementary Deed Clause:**

```markdown
WHEREAS by a deed of sale dated [Date] and registered as Document No. [Number] 
at the office of Sub-Registrar, [Place], the Vendor sold to the Purchaser the 
property described therein;

AND WHEREAS due to a clerical error, the Survey Number of the said property 
was mentioned as 132 instead of the correct Survey Number 123;

NOW THIS SUPPLEMENTARY DEED WITNESSETH that the parties hereby agree that the 
correct Survey Number of the said property is 123, and the said sale deed is 
hereby rectified accordingly.
```

- Admissible without proof of execution

**Stamp Duty + Registration:**
- Both required for immovable property transfers
- Stamp duty paid BEFORE registration
- Under-stamping: Penalty + Inadmissibility

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Not Registering Compulsory Document (Section 17)
**Consequence:** Document VOID; no transfer of title
**Solution:** ALWAYS register documents listed in Section 17

### 2. Delaying Registration Beyond 4 Months
**Consequence:** Late fee/penalty; after 8 months, registration refused
**Solution:** Register within 4 months of execution

### 3. Under-Stamping (Paying Less Stamp Duty)
**Consequence:** Registration refused OR penalty (200% of deficit)
**Solution:** Pay stamp duty on circle rate (if higher than agreement value)

### 4. Assuming Unregistered Document Has Legal Effect
**Consequence:** No transfer, inadmissible as evidence
**Solution:** Understand Section 49 - unregistered document void

### 5. Relying on Possession Alone (Without Registration)
**Consequence:** Subsequent registered buyer gets priority
**Solution:** Register deed + take possession

### 6. Not Verifying Registered PoA Before Accepting PoA Sale
**Consequence:** PoA sale void if PoA not registered
**Solution:** Verify PoA registered before accepting sale by agent

### 7. Not Obtaining Certified Copies for Records
**Consequence:** Original lost/damaged, difficult to prove transaction
**Solution:** Obtain certified copies immediately after registration

### 8. Ignoring Registration of Supplementary/Rectification Deed
**Consequence:** Rectification not effective
**Solution:** Register supplementary deed (Section 17(2) compulsory)

### 9. Not Checking Encumbrance Certificate Before Purchase
**Consequence:** Discover prior registered mortgages/charges/litigation later
**Solution:** Obtain 15-year encumbrance certificate; verify all registered documents

### 10. Assuming Agreement to Sell Protects Against Double Sale
**Consequence:** Unregistered agreement gives no priority; registered buyer wins
**Solution:** Register agreement to sell if substantial advance paid
