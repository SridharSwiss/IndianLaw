# Protocol: Amendments and Withdrawal (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-CPC-AMENDMENTS-WITHDRAWAL  
**Domain**: Civil Procedure  
**Subdomain**: Pleadings and Initiation  
**Version**: 1.0.0  
**Last Updated**: 2025-12-06

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on amending pleadings (plaint, written statement, other proceedings) and withdrawing suits under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908, including strategic considerations and limitation effects.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "Can I amend my plaint after filing?"
- Need to correct errors in pleadings
- Want to add new parties or reliefs
- Change cause of action
- Withdraw suit partially or fully
- Need to re-file suit after withdrawal

## Core Principles

### 1. Amendment - Liberal Approach (Before Trial)

```
Order VI Rule 17: "The Court may at any stage of the proceedings allow either 
party to alter or amend his pleadings"

Objective:
- Determine REAL CONTROVERSY between parties
- Avoid multiplicity of litigation
- Advance substantial justice

Limitation:
- Cannot change cause of action fundamentally AFTER limitation
- Cannot prejudice opposite party beyond compensation by costs
- Cannot be used as delaying tactic
```

### 2. Withdrawal - Right with Conditions

```
Order XXIII Rule 1: Plaintiff may withdraw suit OR part of claim

Conditions:
- WITH court permission
- May be with/without liberty to file fresh suit
- Limitation implications

Strategic Use:
- Correct procedural defects
- Add necessary parties
- Increase claim
- File in proper court
```

## AMENDMENTS (Order VI Rule 17)

### Scope of Amendment Power

```
Court may allow amendment of:

1. Pleadings:
   - Plaint (Order VII)
   - Written Statement (Order VIII)
   - Replication (reply to written statement)
   - Rejoinder (reply to replication)

2. Other Proceedings:
   - Applications
   - Affidavits
   - Examination-in-chief/cross-examination (transcript)
   - Documents (to correct clerical errors)

3. At Any Stage:
   - Before trial commences
   - During trial
   - After judgment (very limited - only clerical/arithmetical errors)
   - In appeal (limited)
```

### Principles Governing Amendment

#### A. Before Trial Commences - LIBERAL APPROACH

```
Pirgonda Hongonda Patil v. Kalgonda Shidgonda Patil (SC 1957):

"The object of Order VI Rule 17 is that the court should try the merits of the 
case that come before it. Therefore, amendments should be allowed to facilitate 
a decision on merits, provided it does not cause such harm to the other side as 
cannot be compensated by costs and does not amount to allowing a new suit under 
the guise of amendment."

Allowed:
✓ Correcting party names
✓ Adding new facts (if relate to existing cause of action)
✓ Adding alternative reliefs
✓ Correcting valuation
✓ Adding necessary parties
✓ Clarifying existing allegations
✓ Correcting clerical/typographical errors

Generally NOT Allowed:
✗ Changing cause of action entirely (introducing time-barred claim)
✗ Adding new parties after limitation (unless relate back)
✗ Fundamentally altering nature of suit
✗ Introducing new case inconsistent with original
```

#### B. After Trial Commences - RESTRICTIVE APPROACH

```
Conditions for Amendment After Trial Starts:

1. NECESSARY for determining real controversy:
   - Amendment directly relates to issues being tried
   - Cannot be decided without amendment

2. NO PREJUDICE to other party (or compensable by costs/adjournment):
   - Other party has opportunity to meet new case
   - Costs/time can compensate any disadvantage

3. NOT fundamentally changing case:
   - Core cause of action remains same
   - Not introducing entirely new claim

4. NOT delaying tactic:
   - Genuine need (not to prolong litigation)
   - Sought without unreasonable delay

Virendra Kashinath v. Vinayak N. Joshi (SC 2019):
"Amendment after trial commenced should be allowed sparingly, only if essential 
for determination of real controversy and does not change character of suit."
```

#### C. After Decree/Judgment - VERY NARROW

```
Permitted ONLY for:
- Clerical errors (typographical, name spelling, date errors)
- Arithmetical errors (calculation mistakes in decree amount)
- Accidental slips/omissions

NOT Permitted:
✗ Substantive amendment (changing decree terms)
✗ Adding new relief not claimed
✗ Changing ratio decidendi (reasoning)

Review (Order 47) available for:
- Errors apparent on face of record
- New important evidence
- Other sufficient reason
(Amendment is NOT review)
```

### Procedure for Amendment

```
Step 1: File APPLICATION for Amendment
- Under Order VI Rule 17
- State grounds for amendment
- Annex proposed amended pleading (showing changes in RED/underlined)
- Explain necessity and no prejudice to other party

Step 2: Affidavit Supporting Application
- Deponent: Party seeking amendment (not advocate)
- State:
  * Why amendment necessary
  * When fact/ground came to knowledge
  * No intention to delay
  * Amendment does not prejudice opposite party

Step 3: Notice to Opposite Party
- Court issues notice
- Opposite party may:
  * Consent to amendment (file written consent)
  * Object (file reply stating prejudice/grounds)

Step 4: Hearing
- Court hears both parties
- Considers:
  * Stage of suit
  * Necessity of amendment
  * Prejudice to opposite party
  * Delay in seeking amendment
  * Limitation implications

Step 5: Court Order
Options:
(a) Allow amendment unconditionally
(b) Allow amendment with costs (impose costs on applicant)
(c) Allow amendment with conditions (time to opposite party to respond)
(d) Reject amendment application

Step 6: If Allowed
- File amended pleading within time granted
- Serve copy on opposite party
- Opposite party gets time to respond (if needed)
```

### Amendment and Limitation (Section 14 Limitation Act 1963)

```
Critical Question: Does amended claim relate back to original institution date?

Section 14 Limitation Act:
"In computing the period of limitation for any suit the time during which the 
plaintiff has been prosecuting with due diligence another civil proceeding, 
whether in a Court of first instance or in a Court of appeal... in good faith 
in a Court which, from defect of jurisdiction or other cause of a like nature, 
is unable to entertain it, shall be excluded."

Relation Back Doctrine:

Amendment RELATES BACK (limitation saved):
✓ Correction of party name (same person, wrongly named)
✓ Adding alternative relief (same cause of action)
✓ Clarifying existing claim
✓ Adding necessary party (connected to original cause)

Amendment DOES NOT RELATE BACK (limitation NOT saved):
✗ New cause of action (different facts giving rise to different right)
✗ New party (with independent cause of action, after limitation)
✗ Fundamentally different claim

Example 1 (Relates Back):
Original Plaint (01/01/2023): Plaintiff sues "Ram Kumar" for breach of contract
Amendment (01/06/2023): Correct name to "Ram Kumar S/o Shyam Kumar" (same person)
Limitation: 3 years from breach (01/01/2020 to 01/01/2023)
Effect: Amendment relates back to 01/01/2023 (original filing) → Within limitation

Example 2 (Does NOT Relate Back):
Original Plaint (01/01/2023): Plaintiff sues for breach of contract on 01/01/2020
Amendment (01/06/2024): Adds tort claim for fraud discovered in 2024
Limitation: Contract claim within 3 years (filed timely); Fraud claim 3 years from discovery (2024-2027)
Effect: Amendment does NOT relate back (new cause of action) → Fraud claim within limitation

Example 3 (Does NOT Relate Back):
Original Plaint (01/01/2023): Plaintiff sues Defendant 1 for property recovery (cause of action: 01/01/2020)
Amendment (01/06/2024): Adds Defendant 2 (claiming D2 also illegally occupies property)
Limitation: 12 years from dispossession (01/01/2020 to 01/01/2032)
Analysis: If D2's cause of action is same (dispossession in 2020) → Relates back (within limitation)
         If D2's cause of action is different (separate transaction) → Does NOT relate back
```

### Common Amendment Scenarios

#### Scenario 1: Correcting Party Name

```
Facts:
- Plaint names defendant as "Ramesh Enterprises"
- Correct legal name: "Ramesh Enterprises Private Limited"
- Discovery made during filing stage

Amendment Application:
- Substitute "Ramesh Enterprises Private Limited" for "Ramesh Enterprises"
- Relate back to original filing date (same entity, name error)

Court Likely to Allow:
✓ YES (correction of party description; no new party added)
```

#### Scenario 2: Adding New Relief

```
Facts:
- Original claim: Money decree for Rs. 10 lakhs (breach of contract)
- During trial, plaintiff wants to add: Specific performance of contract

Amendment Application:
- Add specific performance as alternative relief

Analysis:
If BEFORE evidence closes:
- Generally allowed (alternative relief, same cause of action)
- Opposite party gets time to respond

If AFTER evidence closes:
- Problematic (defendant may not have led evidence on specific performance)
- May be rejected OR allowed with re-opening of evidence (costs on plaintiff)

Court Likely to Allow (if before evidence):
✓ YES (with costs to compensate defendant for re-preparation)
```

#### Scenario 3: Increasing Suit Value

```
Facts:
- Original suit value: Rs. 5 lakhs
- Plaintiff discovers additional loss: Rs. 3 lakhs (total Rs. 8 lakhs)

Amendment Application:
- Increase claim from Rs. 5 lakhs to Rs. 8 lakhs
- Pay additional court fees

Court Considerations:
- When did plaintiff discover additional loss? (If from beginning, why not claimed?)
- Does increase change court's pecuniary jurisdiction?
- Prejudice to defendant?

If Genuine Discovery:
✓ Allowed (with additional court fees + costs to defendant)

If Attempt to Circumvent Limitation:
✗ Rejected (plaintiff should have claimed correctly initially)
```

#### Scenario 4: Adding New Party

```
Facts:
- Suit filed against Defendant 1 on 01/01/2023 (cause of action: 01/01/2020)
- Plaintiff wants to add Defendant 2 on 01/06/2024
- Limitation: 3 years from cause of action (expired 01/01/2023)

Amendment Application:
- Add Defendant 2 as additional defendant

Analysis:
If D2's cause of action SAME as D1 (joint liability, same transaction):
→ Relates back to 01/01/2023 (original filing)
→ Within limitation
✓ Allowed

If D2's cause of action DIFFERENT (separate transaction/liability):
→ Does NOT relate back
→ Barred by limitation (new suit against D2 would be time-barred)
✗ Rejected

Revajeetu Builders v. Narayanaswamy (SC 2009):
"Adding new party by amendment after limitation → Allowed ONLY if cause of action 
against new party same as existing parties (relates back)"
```

#### Scenario 5: Changing Cause of Action

```
Facts:
- Original claim: Breach of contract (contract dated 01/01/2020; breach 01/06/2020)
- Suit filed: 01/01/2023 (within 3 years)
- Amendment sought: 01/06/2024 (add fraud claim - discovered in 2023)

Amendment Application:
- Add fraud as separate cause of action

Analysis:
- Contract claim: Filed timely
- Fraud claim: Discovered in 2023 → Limitation 3 years from discovery (2023-2026)
- Fraud claim is NEW cause of action (different facts, different right)

Effect:
- Does NOT relate back to 01/01/2023
- Fraud claim treated as filed on 01/06/2024
- Within limitation (discovery in 2023)

✓ Allowed (if fraud cause within limitation from discovery)
✗ Rejected (if fraud cause time-barred)
```

## WITHDRAWAL OF SUIT (Order XXIII Rule 1)

### Right to Withdraw

```
Order XXIII Rule 1: "At any time after the institution of a suit, the plaintiff 
may as against all or any of the defendants abandon his suit or abandon a part 
of his claim"

Conditions:
1. WITH permission of court (not absolute right)
2. May be with LIBERTY to file fresh suit OR WITHOUT liberty

Effect on Limitation:
- If withdrawn WITHOUT liberty → Cannot file fresh suit on same cause
- If withdrawn WITH liberty → Can file fresh suit (subject to limitation)
```

### Types of Withdrawal

#### A. Withdrawal of Entire Suit (Rule 1(1))

```
Plaintiff withdraws whole suit against all defendants

Procedure:
1. File application for withdrawal
2. State reasons for withdrawal
3. Request: "With liberty to file fresh suit" OR "Without such liberty"

Court Considerations:
- Reason for withdrawal (procedural defect / wrong court / settlement / other)
- Whether fresh suit likely (if with liberty requested)
- Costs (may impose costs on plaintiff)

Orders:
(a) Allow withdrawal WITH liberty to file fresh suit
(b) Allow withdrawal WITHOUT liberty (bars fresh suit on same cause - res judicata)
(c) Refuse withdrawal (if found to be abuse of process)

Costs:
- Generally plaintiff pays costs to defendant (wasted time/expenses)
- Court discretion
```

#### B. Withdrawal of Suit Against Some Defendants (Rule 1(1) proviso)

```
Plaintiff withdraws suit against Defendant 2, continues against Defendant 1

Procedure:
1. Application stating withdrawal against specific defendant(s)
2. Suit continues against remaining defendants

Effect:
- Withdrawn defendant discharged
- Plaintiff may be liable for costs to withdrawn defendant
```

#### C. Withdrawal of Part of Claim (Rule 1(1))

```
Plaintiff abandons part of claim, continues with remaining claim

Example:
Original claim: Rs. 10 lakhs + Specific Performance
Withdrawal: Specific Performance claim
Continuation: Rs. 10 lakhs claim only

Procedure:
1. Application for partial withdrawal
2. Amend plaint to remove withdrawn relief
3. Pay court fees (if withdrawal reduces valuation, may get refund)

Effect:
- Suit continues for remaining claim
- Withdrawn claim cannot be claimed again (res judicata if judgment passed)
```

### Withdrawal WITH Liberty vs WITHOUT Liberty

#### WITH Liberty to File Fresh Suit

```
Effect:
- Suit withdrawn but plaintiff CAN file fresh suit on same cause of action
- Fresh suit subject to limitation (from original cause, NOT from withdrawal)

Section 14 Limitation Act:
"Time spent in earlier suit (withdrawn) shall be EXCLUDED in computing 
limitation for fresh suit"

Condition:
- Earlier suit was prosecuted with due diligence and in good faith
- Withdrawn due to:
  * Wrong court (jurisdiction issue)
  * Procedural defect
  * Need to add parties/amend claim beyond permissible
  * Other valid reason

Limitation Calculation:
Fresh Suit Limitation = Original Limitation Period - (Time spent in withdrawn suit)

Example:
Cause of Action: 01/01/2020
First Suit Filed: 01/01/2023 (within 3 years)
Withdrawn WITH Liberty: 01/06/2023 (after 5 months)
Fresh Suit Filed: 01/11/2023

Limitation Check:
Original Limitation: 3 years (01/01/2020 to 01/01/2023)
Time spent in first suit: 5 months (01/01/2023 to 01/06/2023) - EXCLUDED
Time available for fresh suit: 01/01/2023 + 5 months saved = 01/06/2023 to 01/01/2024
Fresh suit filed: 01/11/2023 → WITHIN LIMITATION
```

#### WITHOUT Liberty (Unconditional Withdrawal)

```
Effect:
- Suit withdrawn and plaintiff CANNOT file fresh suit on same cause
- Acts as adjudication on merits (res judicata under Section 11 CPC)

When Court Orders:
- Plaintiff requested without liberty
- OR plaintiff's conduct shows suit was frivolous/vexatious
- OR settlement reached

Example:
Plaintiff sues for Rs. 10 lakhs; withdraws unconditionally (settlement with defendant outside court)
Effect: Cannot file fresh suit for same Rs. 10 lakhs claim (res judicata bars)
```

### Procedure for Withdrawal

```
Step 1: File Application under Order XXIII Rule 1
- Title: "Application for Withdrawal of Suit (with/without liberty)"
- State reasons for withdrawal
- Request court permission

Step 2: Affidavit
- By plaintiff
- Stating:
  * Reasons for withdrawal
  * Whether settling with defendant (if applicable)
  * Request for liberty to file fresh suit (if desired)

Step 3: Notice to Defendant(s)
- Court issues notice
- Defendant may:
  * Consent (file written consent)
  * Object (seek costs/argue abuse of process)

Step 4: Hearing
- Court hears parties
- Considers:
  * Bona fides of withdrawal
  * Whether with/without liberty appropriate
  * Costs

Step 5: Court Order
Options:
(a) Allow withdrawal WITH liberty + impose costs on plaintiff
(b) Allow withdrawal WITHOUT liberty
(c) Refuse withdrawal (if abuse of process)

Step 6: If Allowed
- Suit stands withdrawn
- If with liberty → Plaintiff can file fresh suit (subject to Section 14 Limitation Act)
- If without liberty → Res judicata (cannot file fresh suit)
```

### Withdrawal After Evidence

```
If withdrawal sought AFTER evidence recorded:

Court considers:
- Why plaintiff wants to withdraw now? (after investing time/costs)
- Defendant's objection (wasted time, costs incurred)
- Whether plaintiff likely to succeed (if not, withdrawal may be allowed without liberty)

Costs:
- Heavier costs on plaintiff (compensate defendant for time/expense)
- May include advocate fees, witness expenses

Liberty:
- Generally WITHOUT liberty (after evidence, deemed adjudication on merits)
- WITH liberty only if valid reason (wrong forum, procedural irregularity discovered)
```

## Compromise and Settlement (Order XXIII Rule 3)

```
Rule 3: "Where it is proved to the satisfaction of the Court that a suit has 
been adjusted wholly or in part by any lawful agreement or compromise... the 
Court shall order such agreement or compromise to be recorded, and shall pass 
a decree in accordance therewith"

Distinguished from Withdrawal:
- Withdrawal: Plaintiff unilaterally abandons suit
- Compromise: Parties mutually settle dispute

Procedure:
1. Joint application by plaintiff and defendant
2. Terms of compromise stated
3. Court examines:
   - Whether parties understand terms
   - Whether compromise lawful (not opposed to public policy)
   - Whether parties have capacity to compromise
4. Court records compromise
5. Decree passed as per compromise terms (Consent Decree)

Effect of Consent Decree:
- Binding on parties
- Executable like regular decree
- Appeal limited (cannot challenge compromise terms, only if fraud/coercion/misrepresentation)
```

## Common Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Withdrawal Due to Wrong Court

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff files suit in Delhi court
- Defendant raises jurisdiction objection (property in Mumbai; Section 16 mandatory)
- Plaintiff wants to withdraw and file in Mumbai

Application:
- Withdraw suit WITH LIBERTY to file fresh suit
- Reason: Lack of jurisdiction (wrong forum)

Court Order:
✓ Allow withdrawal WITH liberty
✓ Section 14 Limitation Act: Time spent in Delhi suit excluded from limitation
✓ Costs: May impose on plaintiff (for filing in wrong court)

Fresh Suit:
- File in Mumbai court
- Limitation saved (time in Delhi suit excluded)
```

### Scenario 2: Withdrawal to Add Necessary Party

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff sues D1 for property recovery
- During trial, realizes D2 also necessary party (co-owner)
- Cannot add D2 by amendment (beyond limitation)

Application:
- Withdraw suit WITH LIBERTY
- Re-file suit against D1 + D2 (within limitation under Section 14)

Court Order:
✓ Allow withdrawal WITH liberty (if bona fide reason)
✓ Impose costs on plaintiff
✓ Section 14: Time spent in first suit excluded

Fresh Suit:
- File against D1 + D2
- Limitation: Original period - time in first suit = Time available for fresh suit
```

### Scenario 3: Settlement Outside Court

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff and defendant reach settlement during suit
- Agree: Defendant pays Rs. 7 lakhs (original claim Rs. 10 lakhs)

Options:

Option 1: Consent Decree (Order XXIII Rule 3)
- Joint application with settlement terms
- Court records compromise
- Consent decree passed (executable)

Option 2: Withdrawal WITHOUT Liberty
- Plaintiff withdraws suit (after receiving Rs. 7 lakhs)
- Cannot file fresh suit (settlement final)

Recommended: Option 1 (Consent Decree - ensures enforceability if defendant defaults)
```

### Scenario 4: Partial Withdrawal

```
Facts:
- Original claim: Rs. 10 lakhs + Specific Performance
- Plaintiff realizes specific performance not viable (limitation/jurisdiction issue)
- Wants to continue with money claim

Application:
- Withdraw Specific Performance claim
- Continue with Rs. 10 lakhs claim

Court Order:
✓ Allow partial withdrawal
✓ Amend plaint to remove specific performance prayer
✓ Adjust court fees (if needed)

Effect:
- Suit continues for Rs. 10 lakhs
- Specific performance claim abandoned (cannot be claimed later - res judicata)
```

## Case Law

### 1. Pirgonda Hongonda Patil v. Kalgonda Shidgonda Patil (SC 1957)
```
Facts: Amendment to plaint after trial commenced
Held:
- Order VI Rule 17: Liberal power to amend (to determine real controversy)
- Amendment allowed unless:
  * Causes prejudice to other party beyond compensation by costs
  * Amounts to new suit under guise of amendment
  * Introduces time-barred claim
```

### 2. Virendra Kashinath v. Vinayak N. Joshi (SC 2019)
```
Facts: Amendment sought to introduce new cause of action after limitation expired
Held:
- Amendment cannot introduce time-barred claim
- Relation back doctrine: Amendment relates back ONLY if same cause of action
- New cause of action beyond limitation → Amendment denied
```

### 3. Revajeetu Builders v. Narayanaswamy (SC 2009)
```
Facts: Amendment to add new party after limitation
Held:
- Adding new party by amendment → Allowed ONLY if:
  * Cause of action against new party SAME as existing parties
  * Amendment relates back to original institution date
- If new party has separate/independent cause beyond limitation → Amendment denied
```

### 4. Kaushalya Devi Bogra v. Land Acquisition Officer (SC 2011)
```
Facts: Withdrawal of suit; whether time spent in withdrawn suit excluded under Section 14
Held:
- Section 14 Limitation Act: Time spent in earlier suit excluded IF:
  * Prosecuted with due diligence
  * In good faith
  * Court unable to entertain (jurisdiction defect or like cause)
- If withdrawn due to lack of jurisdiction → Section 14 applies
```

### 5. Baldev Singh v. Manohar Singh (SC 2006)
```
Facts: Plaintiff withdrew suit without liberty; attempted to file fresh suit
Held:
- Withdrawal WITHOUT liberty → Acts as res judicata (Section 11 CPC)
- Fresh suit on same cause of action barred
- Plaintiff cannot circumvent by filing fresh suit
```

### 6. Vidyabai v. Padmalatha (SC 2009)
```
Facts: Partial withdrawal of claim; whether withdrawn claim can be re-agitated
Held:
- Order XXIII Rule 1: Partial withdrawal permitted
- Withdrawn claim cannot be claimed later (res judicata)
- Court has discretion to allow/refuse withdrawal based on circumstances
```

## Common Mistakes

```
Mistake 1: Seeking Amendment After Evidence Closes (Fundamental Change)
❌ After evidence closes, apply to change cause of action entirely
✓ Seek amendment BEFORE evidence closes; OR only for clerical/minor corrections after

Mistake 2: Not Showing Relation Back for New Party Amendment
❌ Add new party after limitation without showing same cause of action
✓ Show cause of action against new party is SAME as existing parties (relates back)

Mistake 3: Undervaluing Amendment's Limitation Impact
❌ Assume all amendments relate back to original filing
✓ Check if amendment introduces NEW cause of action (does NOT relate back if time-barred)

Mistake 4: Withdrawal Without Liberty When Fresh Suit Intended
❌ Withdraw "without liberty" thinking can still file fresh suit
✓ Request "WITH liberty" if intend to file fresh suit

Mistake 5: Not Excluding Time Under Section 14 for Fresh Suit
❌ File fresh suit without calculating exclusion of time spent in withdrawn suit
✓ Apply Section 14: Original limitation - time in withdrawn suit = Time available for fresh suit

Mistake 6: Partial Withdrawal Without Amending Plaint
❌ Withdraw specific relief but don't amend plaint to remove prayer
✓ After partial withdrawal allowed, amend plaint to delete withdrawn relief
```

# Protocol: Appeals and Revision (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-CPC-APPEALS-REVISION  
**Domain**: Civil Procedure  
**Subdomain**: Post-Judgment  
**Version**: 1.0.0  
**Last Updated**: 2025-12-06

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on appeals, second appeals, and revision petitions in civil matters under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908, including grounds, procedures, and strategic considerations.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "Can I appeal this judgment?"
- Need to file appeal from decree/order
- Second appeal to High Court questions
- Revision petition under Section 115
- Appeal limitation queries
- Stay of decree pending appeal

## Core Principles

### 1. Appeal = Right (Subject to Law)

```
Section 96: "Save where otherwise expressly provided, an appeal shall lie from 
every decree passed by any Court exercising original jurisdiction"

General Rule: APPEAL IS A RIGHT
→ From every decree (unless expressly barred)
→ Not from every order (only specified orders - Order 43)

Exceptions (No Appeal):
- Expressly barred by CPC or other statute
- Decree by consent (unless fraud/misrepresentation - limited grounds)
- Certain interlocutory orders (not in Order 43 Rule 1 list)
- Small value suits (if state notification bars appeal)
```

### 2. Hierarchy of Appeals

```
First Appeal (Section 96-99):
FROM: Original decree of any court
TO: Immediately higher court (District Court → High Court; Civil Judge → District Court)
SCOPE: Full rehearing on facts + law

Second Appeal (Section 100):
FROM: Appellate decree (first appeal decided)
TO: High Court
SCOPE: ONLY substantial questions of LAW (NOT facts)

Special Leave Petition (Article 136 Constitution):
FROM: High Court judgment/order
TO: Supreme Court
SCOPE: Discretionary (SC may refuse)
```

## FIRST APPEAL (Section 96-99, Order 41)

### Right of Appeal (Section 96)

```
Section 96: Appeal lies from every decree passed by court exercising original 
jurisdiction to court authorized to hear appeals

Appealable:
✓ Decrees (preliminary or final)
✓ Decrees in suits (not in execution or other proceedings)

NOT Appealable:
✗ Orders (unless specified in Order 43 Rule 1)
✗ Decrees expressly barred
✗ Decrees by consent (limited exceptions)

Who Can Appeal:
- Person aggrieved by decree
- Legal representative of deceased person aggrieved
- Assignee (if assignment before decree)
```

### Procedure for First Appeal (Order 41)

```
Step 1: File MEMORANDUM OF APPEAL
Within: 90 days from date of decree
Extension: Up to 90 MORE days if sufficient cause shown (Section 5 Limitation Act)

Contents (Order 41 Rule 1):
1. Name and description of appellant
2. Name and description of respondent
3. Decree appealed from (date, court, suit number)
4. Grounds of appeal (specific, not vague)
5. Relief sought
6. Valuation for court fees
7. Verification (by appellant)

Step 2: Pay COURT FEES
Ad valorem (on decree value) or fixed (as per state court fees rules)

Step 3: Serve NOTICE to Respondent
Memorandum of appeal + grounds

Step 4: Respondent Files VAKALATNAMA
(If contesting appeal)

Step 5: Hearing
Arguments on merits
Reference to evidence/documents from trial court record

Step 6: Judgment
Appellate court decides
Affirms, varies, or reverses decree
```

### Grounds of Appeal (Order 41 Rule 1)

```
Grounds must be SPECIFIC, stating:

1. Errors of Fact:
   "The learned trial court erred in holding that defendant paid Rs. 5 lakhs, 
   when evidence (PW1 testimony + Exhibit P-2) clearly shows no payment."

2. Errors of Law:
   "The learned trial court erred in applying Section 73 Indian Contract Act 
   when Section 74 (liquidated damages) was applicable."

3. Errors in Appreciation of Evidence:
   "The learned trial court erred in disbelieving plaintiff's witness PW1 
   without valid reasons, when defendant's witness DW1 was contradictory."

4. Procedural Errors:
   "The learned trial court erred in passing decree without giving defendant 
   opportunity to cross-examine plaintiff's witness PW2."

NOT Valid Grounds:
- Mere disagreement with findings (without showing error)
- Re-arguing facts without pointing out specific errors
- New facts not pleaded/proved at trial
```

### Powers of Appellate Court (Section 107, Order 41 Rule 33)

```
Section 107: Appellate Court Powers

Appellate court may:

(a) DETERMINE CASE FINALLY:
    - Decide on merits
    - Affirm, vary, or reverse decree

(b) REMAND CASE:
    - Send back to trial court for fresh decision
    - Reasons: Trial court decided on wrong issue, failed to frame/decide issue, 
      jurisdictional defect

(c) FRAME ISSUES AND REFER:
    - Frame specific issues
    - Send to trial court for findings
    - Return with findings for appellate court to decide

(d) TAKE ADDITIONAL EVIDENCE (Order 41 Rule 27):
    - If necessary for pronouncing judgment
    - If trial court improperly refused/admitted evidence
    - Conditions:
      * Court records reasons why additional evidence needed
      * Opportunity to opposite party

(e) PASS ANY OTHER ORDER:
    - As justice requires
    - Modify decree
    - Grant different relief (if within scope of pleadings)
```

### Scope of First Appeal

```
First Appeal = FULL REHEARING

Appellate court:
- Re-appreciates evidence
- Reconsiders findings of fact
- Reviews application of law
- Can reverse trial court on facts + law

Standard:
- Appellate court NOT bound by trial court's findings
- BUT trial court's findings entitled to weight (saw witnesses, judged credibility)
- Appellate court interferes only if:
  * Findings perverse (against evidence)
  * Material evidence ignored
  * Inadmissible evidence relied upon
  * Legal error in approach
```

### Remand (Order 41 Rule 23-25)

```
Rule 23: Appellate Court may remand if:
(a) Trial court decided suit on preliminary point without deciding all issues
(b) Trial court decided wrong issue
(c) Trial court failed to try suit on merits due to jurisdictional or procedural error
(d) Additional evidence necessary + cannot be taken by appellate court

Procedure on Remand:
1. Appellate court passes remand order (specifying issues for fresh decision)
2. Trial court re-hears on remanded issues
3. Records evidence, makes findings
4. Sends findings + evidence to appellate court
5. Appellate court decides appeal based on fresh findings

Effect of Remand:
- Original decree set aside
- Fresh trial on remanded issues
- Parties may adduce fresh evidence on remanded issues
- After fresh findings, appellate court decides

Limitation:
- Frequent remands discouraged (delays disposal)
- Remand only if absolutely necessary
```

## APPEAL FROM ORDERS (Section 104, Order 43)

```
Section 104: Appeals from orders

General Rule: ORDERS NOT APPEALABLE (only decrees appealable)

Exception: Order 43 Rule 1 lists SPECIFIC ORDERS from which appeal lies:

(a) Rejection of plaint (Order VII Rule 11)
(b) Rejection of set-off/counter-claim (Order VIII Rule 6C)
(c) Refusal to grant temporary injunction (Order XXXIX Rule 1-2)
(d) Granting/refusing to grant injunction (interim)
(e) Receiver appointment/removal
(f) Commission refusal/grant
(g) Setting aside ex parte decree (Order IX Rule 13) - refusal
(h) Rejection of application to set aside decree (Order IX Rule 13)
...and 14 other specified orders

Appeal Timeline: 30 DAYS from order
TO: Same court as first appeal from decree would lie

NOT Appealable Orders (Examples):
- Adjournment orders
- Cost orders (unless part of decree)
- Discovery orders
- Orders allowing/refusing amendment (generally)
- Most interlocutory/procedural orders
```

## SECOND APPEAL (Section 100, Order 42)

### Restricted Scope (Section 100 - Amended 1976)

```
Section 100: Second appeal to High Court on SUBSTANTIAL QUESTION OF LAW only

BEFORE 1976: Second appeal on facts + law (full rehearing)
AFTER 1976: ONLY on substantial questions of LAW

Second Appeal Conditions:

1. FROM: Appellate decree (first appeal decided)
2. TO: High Court
3. GROUND: Case involves SUBSTANTIAL QUESTION OF LAW

NOT Available for:
- Questions of fact
- Mixed questions of fact and law (unless substantial legal question can be extracted)
- Re-appreciation of evidence
- Perverse findings (remedy: First appeal, not second appeal)

What is "Substantial Question of Law"?

1. Substantial:
   - Important, material (not trivial/technical)
   - Affects rights of parties significantly
   - Not settled by precedent (or conflicting precedents)

2. Question of Law:
   - Legal principle/interpretation
   - Construction of statute/document
   - Applicability of legal provision
   
   NOT Question of Law:
   - Findings of fact (credibility of witnesses, weight of evidence)
   - Whether evidence sufficient (fact)
   - Appreciation of evidence (fact)
```

### Formulation of Substantial Question (Section 100)

```
Procedure:

Step 1: Appellant Formulates Substantial Question of Law
In memorandum of second appeal:
"This appeal raises the following substantial question of law:
Whether, on the facts and circumstances of this case, the first appellate court 
erred in law in holding that Section 74 Indian Contract Act applies when the 
contract clause is a genuine pre-estimate of damages and not a penalty?"

Step 2: High Court Decides Whether Substantial Question Exists
- Admission stage (preliminary hearing)
- HC examines: Is question substantial? Is it question of law?
- Options:
  (a) Admit appeal (if substantial question of law present)
  (b) Dismiss appeal (if no substantial question)

Step 3: If Admitted, HC Frames Substantial Question
HC may reframe or add questions

Step 4: Hearing Limited to Substantial Question
Arguments confined to framed question(s)
HC does NOT re-appreciate evidence or findings of fact

Step 5: HC Decides
Answers substantial question
Affirms, varies, or reverses appellate decree based on answer
```

### Examples of Substantial Questions of Law

```
✓ Substantial Questions (Second Appeal Admitted):

1. "Whether suit for specific performance is barred when contract does not 
   specify time as of the essence?" (Legal interpretation)

2. "Whether Section 27 Indian Contract Act bars a non-compete clause during 
   employment or only post-employment?" (Statutory construction)

3. "Whether partition can be ordered when one co-owner is minor?" 
   (Legal capacity issue)

✗ NOT Substantial Questions (Second Appeal Dismissed):

1. "Whether plaintiff proved payment by preponderance of evidence?" 
   (Question of fact - appreciation of evidence)

2. "Whether defendant's witness was credible?" 
   (Question of fact - credibility assessment)

3. "Whether first appellate court correctly weighed evidence?" 
   (Question of fact - re-appreciation)
```

### Powers in Second Appeal (Order 42)

```
High Court in second appeal may:

1. Reverse/Affirm/Modify first appellate decree (Rule 3)
2. Remand (if necessary to determine substantial question) (Rule 3)
3. Frame issues and refer to lower court (rare)
4. NOT re-appreciate evidence (limited to legal question)

Remand in Second Appeal:
Very rare (defeats purpose of restricting second appeal to law)
Allowed only if:
- Lower court decided on wrong legal principle
- Substantial question cannot be answered without fresh findings on specific issue
```

## REVISION (Section 115 CPC)

### Revisional Jurisdiction (Section 115 - AMENDED 2002)

```
Section 115 (Before 2002):
High Court could revise ANY order of subordinate court
Scope: Broad revisional power

Section 115 (After 2002):
HIGH COURT SHALL NOT revise unless:
(a) Subordinate court EXERCISED JURISDICTION NOT VESTED IN IT, OR
(b) Subordinate court FAILED TO EXERCISE JURISDICTION VESTED IN IT, OR
(c) Subordinate court ACTED ILLEGALLY OR WITH MATERIAL IRREGULARITY in 
    exercise of jurisdiction

Effect of Amendment:
- Revisional power severely curtailed
- NOT a regular appellate remedy (like pre-2002)
- Limited to jurisdictional errors, illegality, material irregularity

Note: Many High Courts' revisional jurisdiction under Section 115 restricted 
to orders NOT appealable (no revision if appeal lies)
```

### Grounds for Revision (Post-2002)

```
1. Excess of Jurisdiction:
   - Court exercised power it did not have
   - Example: Civil Judge (Junior Division) trying suit worth Rs. 10 lakhs 
     (beyond pecuniary jurisdiction)

2. Lack of Jurisdiction:
   - Court failed to exercise power it had
   - Example: Court refused to hear suit on ground it lacks jurisdiction, 
     when it actually had jurisdiction

3. Illegality:
   - Court acted contrary to law
   - Example: Passed decree without giving opportunity to defendant (violation 
     of natural justice)

4. Material Irregularity:
   - Procedural error materially affecting rights
   - Example: Framed wrong issues, leading to erroneous decision

NOT Grounds for Revision (Post-2002):
- Erroneous findings of fact (appeal remedy)
- Incorrect appreciation of evidence (appeal remedy)
- Wrong decision on merits (appeal remedy)
```

### Limitation for Revision

```
Section 115: 90 days from order (NOT expressly stated, but applied)

In Practice:
- No specific limitation in CPC for revision
- Courts apply 90 days (analogous to appeal limitation)
- Can be extended if sufficient cause
```

## APPEAL/REVISION - STRATEGIC CHOICE

```
Scenario: Subordinate court passed order refusing to set aside ex parte decree

Options:

1. APPEAL (Order 43 Rule 1(h)):
   - Order refusing to set aside ex parte decree is appealable
   - Timeline: 30 days
   - Full hearing on merits

2. REVISION (Section 115):
   - Argue jurisdictional error/illegality/material irregularity
   - Timeline: 90 days
   - Limited grounds

Recommendation: APPEAL (preferred - broader scope)
Revision only if appeal not available OR appeal time expired (within 90 days revision time)
```

## STAY OF DECREE PENDING APPEAL (Order 41 Rule 5)

```
Rule 5: Appellate court may stay execution of decree pending appeal

Conditions:
1. Application by appellant
2. Appellate court satisfied:
   - Prima facie case (substantial question involved)
   - Balance of convenience (appellant suffers greater hardship if no stay)
   - Irreparable injury (if decree executed, cannot be undone)

Procedure:

Step 1: File Application for Stay
With appeal OR separately after appeal admitted

Step 2: Affidavit
Stating:
- Decree executable
- If executed, appellant suffers irreparable loss
- Appellant willing to furnish security (deposit/bank guarantee)

Step 3: Hearing
Court examines:
- Merits of appeal (prima facie)
- Hardship to appellant vs respondent
- Public interest (if any)

Step 4: Order
(a) Grant stay (unconditional OR conditional - deposit/security)
(b) Refuse stay (decree executable despite appeal)

Conditions for Stay:
- Deposit decree amount in court, OR
- Furnish bank guarantee, OR
- Other security (as court directs)

Effect of Stay:
- Decree cannot be executed till appeal decided
- Respondent cannot recover/enforce decree
- Status quo maintained

Vacation of Stay:
- Respondent may apply to vacate stay (if circumstances change)
- Court may vacate if:
  * Appeal delayed without valid reason
  * Appellant abusing stay (delaying deliberately)
  * Security insufficient
```

## LIMITATION FOR APPEALS (Limitation Act 1963)

```
First Appeal from Decree (Section 96):
- 90 days from decree date
- Extension: Up to 90 MORE days (Section 5 - if sufficient cause)

Appeal from Order (Order 43):
- 30 days from order date
- Extension: Up to 30 MORE days (Section 5)

Second Appeal (Section 100):
- 90 days from first appellate decree
- Extension: Up to 90 MORE days (Section 5)

Revision (Section 115):
- 90 days from order (practice, not expressly stated)

Special Leave Petition (Article 136):
- 90 days from High Court order/judgment
- Extension: Discretionary (Supreme Court may condone any delay)

Counting of Days (Section 12 Limitation Act):
- Exclude date of decree/order
- Include last day
- If last day is holiday → Limit extends to next working day

Sufficient Cause (Section 5):
- Illness, accident, bona fide mistake
- Continuing cause (litigation in wrong forum)
- NOT mere negligence/laches
```

## CONDONATION OF DELAY (Section 5 Limitation Act)

```
Section 5: "Any appeal or application may be admitted after prescribed period 
if appellant/applicant satisfies court that there was sufficient cause for not 
preferring appeal/application within such period"

Burden: On appellant to show sufficient cause

What is "Sufficient Cause"?
- Illness preventing filing (medical certificate)
- Accident, natural calamity
- Bona fide mistake (filed in wrong court, promptly corrected)
- Continuing litigation in wrong forum (Section 14 exclusion)
- Fraud by opposite party preventing filing

NOT Sufficient Cause:
- Mere negligence/carelessness
- Advocate's mistake (unless extraordinary circumstances)
- Financial hardship (not filing fees)
- Busy schedule

Liberal Approach:
- Courts generally liberal in condoning delay (substantial justice over technicality)
- BUT gross negligence/deliberate inaction → No condonation

Maximum Condonation:
- 90 additional days for appeal from decree (total 180 days)
- 30 additional days for appeal from order (total 60 days)
- NO LIMIT for SLP (Supreme Court may condone any delay)
```

## CASE LAW

### 1. Surya Dev Rai v. Ram Chander Rai (SC 2003)
```
Facts: Second appeal on questions of fact
Held:
- Section 100 (post-1976): Second appeal ONLY on substantial questions of law
- Questions of fact NOT appealable in second appeal
- High Court must formulate substantial question before admitting second appeal
- If no substantial question → Dismiss appeal at admission stage
```

### 2. Santosh Hazari v. Purushottam Tiwari (SC 2001)
```
Facts: Condonation of delay in filing appeal
Held:
- Section 5 Limitation Act: Sufficient cause required
- Courts should adopt liberal approach in condoning delay (substantial justice)
- BUT gross negligence/deliberate inaction → No condonation
- Each case on its own facts
```

### 3. Ramesh Chandra Sankla v. Vikram Cement (SC 2008)
```
Facts: Scope of first appeal; whether appellate court can re-appreciate evidence
Held:
- First appeal is full rehearing on facts + law
- Appellate court can re-appreciate evidence
- BUT trial court's findings entitled to weight (saw witnesses, judged demeanor)
- Appellate court interferes only if findings perverse/against evidence
```

### 4. Keshavlal Khemchand v. State of Gujarat (SC 2015)
```
Facts: Revision under Section 115 CPC post-2002 amendment
Held:
- Section 115 (post-2002): Limited to jurisdictional errors, illegality, material irregularity
- NOT a regular appellate remedy
- Cannot revise on grounds of erroneous findings of fact/law (appeal remedy)
- Revisional power severely curtailed compared to pre-2002
```

### 5. Anil Kumar Singh v. Shivnath Mishra (SC 2011)
```
Facts: Stay of decree pending appeal
Held:
- Order 41 Rule 5: Stay discretionary
- Factors: Prima facie case, balance of convenience, irreparable injury
- Generally stay granted if appellant furnishes security (deposit/bank guarantee)
- Prevents miscarriage of justice if decree executed and later reversed
```

## COMMON MISTAKES

```
Mistake 1: Second Appeal on Questions of Fact
❌ File second appeal challenging findings of fact/appreciation of evidence
✓ Second appeal ONLY on substantial questions of LAW (Section 100 post-1976)

Mistake 2: Not Formulating Substantial Question in Second Appeal
❌ Memorandum of second appeal does not formulate specific legal question
✓ MUST formulate substantial question clearly (HC decides if substantial)

Mistake 3: Not Seeking Stay of Decree
❌ File appeal but don't apply for stay; respondent executes decree
✓ Apply for stay of decree pending appeal (Order 41 Rule 5) to prevent execution

Mistake 4: General/Vague Grounds of Appeal
❌ "Trial court erred in passing decree" (no specificity)
✓ "Trial court erred in [specific finding] when evidence (PW1 + Exhibit P-2) shows [contrary fact]"

Mistake 5: Missing Limitation
❌ File appeal after 90 days without condonation application
✓ File within 90 days OR with condonation application (Section 5) showing sufficient cause

Mistake 6: Attempting Revision When Appeal Lies
❌ File revision under Section 115 when order is appealable (Order 43)
✓ File appeal (if lies); revision only if no appeal OR appeal time expired

Mistake 7: Not Paying Security for Stay
❌ Court grants stay conditional on deposit; appellant doesn't deposit; stay vacated
✓ Comply with stay conditions immediately (deposit decree amount/furnish bank guarantee)
```

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on the hierarchy of civil courts in India, their powers, appellate structure, and the flow of civil litigation from trial to Supreme Court.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "What is the court hierarchy in India?"
- Need to understand appellate options after judgment
- Determining which court to appeal to
- Understanding powers of different courts
- Concurrent jurisdiction questions
- Original vs appellate jurisdiction queries

## Core Framework

### Constitutional Courts vs Subordinate Courts

```
CONSTITUTIONAL COURTS (Created by Constitution):
1. Supreme Court of India (Article 124-147)
2. High Courts (Article 214-231) - One or more per state

SUBORDINATE COURTS (Created by State Acts):
1. District Courts (District Judge/Additional District Judge)
2. Civil Courts (Civil Judge Senior Division / Junior Division)
3. Special Courts (Small Causes, Family, Consumer, etc.)

Key Distinction:
- Constitutional courts: Cannot be abolished (constitutional protection)
- Subordinate courts: Can be reorganized by state legislation
```

## Detailed Court Structure

### 1. Supreme Court of India

**Constitutional Provisions**: Articles 124-147

#### A. Original Jurisdiction (Article 131)

```
Limited to:
1. Disputes between Union and States
2. Disputes between States
3. Disputes involving enforcement of Fundamental Rights (Article 32)

Examples:
- State A vs State B over river water sharing
- Union of India vs State over constitutional validity
- Fundamental Rights violation (writ jurisdiction)

NOT available for:
- Private disputes between citizens
- Ordinary civil/criminal matters
```

#### B. Appellate Jurisdiction (Article 132-136)

**Civil Appeals**:

**1. Appeal as of Right (Article 133 - REPEALED 1973)**
```
Post-1973: No automatic right of appeal in civil matters
Exception: Must obtain certificate from High Court OR SLP under Article 136
```

**2. Certificate from High Court (Order XLV Rule 1-2)**:
```
High Court may grant certificate if:
- Case involves substantial question of law of general importance, AND
- Question needs to be decided by Supreme Court

Procedure:
- Party applies for certificate within 60 days of HC judgment
- HC decides if question of general importance exists
- If granted → Direct appeal to SC (no leave required)
```

**3. Special Leave Petition (Article 136)**:
```
Article 136: "Supreme Court may, in its discretion, grant special leave to 
appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any 
cause or matter passed by any court or tribunal."

Discretionary power:
- SC NOT bound to grant leave
- Generally granted if:
  * Substantial question of law
  * Perversity in HC findings
  * Gross injustice
  * Public importance

Limitation: 90 days from HC order/judgment (can be condoned)

Procedure:
- File SLP petition with leave application
- SC may:
  * Grant leave → Becomes regular appeal
  * Dismiss leave → No appeal
  * Issue notice → Hearing on merits
```

#### C. Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143)

```
President may refer questions of law/fact to SC for advisory opinion

SC's opinion:
- Advisory (not binding on President)
- BUT carries weight as SC's view

Examples:
- Constitutional interpretation questions
- International treaty obligations
- Public importance matters
```

#### D. Powers

```
1. Transfer Cases (Article 139A):
   - Transfer pending cases from one HC to another HC
   - Transfer subordinate court cases to itself

2. Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32):
   - Enforcement of Fundamental Rights
   - Can issue writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Quo Warranto, Certiorari

3. Review (Article 137):
   - Review own judgments
   - Limited grounds (error apparent on face of record)
   - No appeal against review dismissal

4. Curative Petition (Rupa Ashok Hurra 2002):
   - Beyond review (if review dismissed)
   - Extremely narrow grounds (abuse of process + miscarriage of justice)
```

### 2. High Courts

**Constitutional Provisions**: Articles 214-231

#### A. Original Jurisdiction (Article 225)

```
Only certain High Courts have original civil jurisdiction:
- Calcutta High Court (Ordinary Original Civil Jurisdiction - OOCJ)
- Bombay High Court (OOCJ)
- Madras High Court (OOCJ)
- Delhi High Court (limited)

Pecuniary limits:
Set by state notification (varies by HC)
Example (Bombay): Rs. 2 lakhs and above

Other High Courts:
NO original civil jurisdiction (only appellate + writ)
```

#### B. Appellate Jurisdiction (Article 227 + CPC)

**Appeals from District Courts** (Section 96 CPC):

```
Section 96: "Appeal from original decree"

Right of appeal from District Court to High Court:
- ALL decrees appealable UNLESS:
  * Expressly barred by CPC or other law
  * Suit value below prescribed limit (state notification)
  * Decree by consent (unless fraud/misrepresentation)

Examples barred:
- Section 104: Certain interlocutory orders not appealable
- Order 43 Rule 1: Limited list of appealable orders
- Provincial Small Cause Courts Act: No appeal on facts from Small Causes
```

**Appeals from Orders** (Section 104 + Order 43):

```
Section 104: "Appeals from orders"
General rule: Orders NOT appealable (only decrees appealable)

Exception - Order 43 Rule 1: Following orders appealable:
(a) Rejection of plaint (Order VII Rule 11)
(b) Rejection of set-off/counter-claim
(c) Refusal to grant temporary injunction (Order XXXIX)
(d) Receiver appointment/removal
(e) Issue of commission
(f) Rejection of application under Order 9 Rule 13 (set aside ex parte decree)
...and 22 other specified orders

Appeal time: 30 days from order
```

**Second Appeal** (Section 100 CPC):

```
Section 100: "Second appeal to High Court from subordinate courts"

From subordinate courts → High Court (if decree by District Court was appellate)

Grounds (RESTRICTED - amended 1976):
ONLY if case involves SUBSTANTIAL QUESTION OF LAW

NOT available for:
- Questions of fact
- Mixed questions of law and fact (unless substantial legal question extracted)
- Re-appreciation of evidence

Procedure:
1. Appellant must FORMULATE substantial question of law
2. HC decides if question is substantial
3. If NO substantial question → Dismiss at admission
4. If YES → Frame substantial question → Hear appeal ONLY on that question

Example:
First appeal: Civil Judge → District Court (decided)
Second appeal: District Court → High Court (only if substantial question of law)
```

#### C. Supervisory Jurisdiction (Article 227)

```
Article 227: "Power of superintendence over all courts and tribunals"

High Court can:
- Call for records of subordinate courts
- Satisfy itself as to correctness/legality/propriety of proceedings
- Issue directions if irregularity

NOT an appeal:
- Cannot re-appreciate evidence
- Cannot substitute own findings on facts
- Only corrects jurisdictional errors/legal errors

Grounds:
- Excess of jurisdiction
- Jurisdictional error
- Violation of natural justice
- Perversity

Limitation: Not specified (but generally within reasonable time)
```

#### D. Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226)

```
Article 226: High Court can issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights 
AND other rights

Wider than Article 32 (SC):
- SC: ONLY Fundamental Rights
- HC: Fundamental Rights + legal rights + statutory rights

Territorial jurisdiction:
- Where cause of action arose, OR
- Where respondent resides/carries business, OR
- Where government office situated

Writs available:
1. Habeas Corpus (illegal detention)
2. Mandamus (compel public duty)
3. Prohibition (prevent excess of jurisdiction)
4. Certiorari (quash unlawful order)
5. Quo Warranto (challenge illegal assumption of office)

Discretionary:
- HC may refuse writ if:
  * Alternative remedy available
  * Unclean hands
  * Laches (delay)
  * Not in public interest
```

#### E. Powers

```
1. Transfer Cases (Section 24-25 CPC):
   - Transfer from subordinate court to another subordinate court
   - Transfer from subordinate to HC (if substantial question of law)

2. Revision (Section 115 CPC - ABOLISHED for most HCs 2002):
   - Revisional jurisdiction limited post-2002 amendment
   - Only if subordinate court:
     * Exercised jurisdiction not vested in it, OR
     * Failed to exercise jurisdiction vested in it, OR
     * Acted illegally/with material irregularity

3. Reference (Section 113):
   - Subordinate court can refer question of law to HC
   - HC's decision binding on subordinate court

4. Review (Order 47 CPC):
   - Review own judgments
   - Grounds: Error apparent, new evidence, other sufficient reason
```

### 3. District Courts

**Statutory Basis**: State Civil Courts Acts (varies by state)

#### A. Original Jurisdiction

```
Pecuniary:
- Set by state notification
- Typical: Suits above Rs. 50 lakhs up to Rs. 3 crores (varies)
- Some states: Unlimited

Territorial:
- District where cause of action arose/defendant resides (Section 20 CPC)

Types of suits:
- Ordinary civil suits (money, property, specific performance, injunction, declaration)
- NOT exclusively assigned to tribunals
```

#### B. Appellate Jurisdiction (Section 96 CPC)

```
From subordinate courts in the district:
- Civil Judge (Senior Division) → District Court (first appeal)
- Civil Judge (Junior Division) → District Court (first appeal)

Decree appealable:
- ALL decrees UNLESS barred

Appeal time: 90 days from decree (can be condoned up to 90 more days)

Powers on appeal (Section 107 Order 41 Rule 33):
(a) Determine case finally (on merits)
(b) Remand case (for fresh decision on specific issues)
(c) Frame issues and refer to lower court (for finding)
(d) Take additional evidence (Order 41 Rule 27)
(e) Reverse, affirm, or vary decree
```

#### C. Revisional Jurisdiction

```
Varies by state legislation:
Some states: District Court can revise orders of subordinate courts
Others: No revisional power

If available:
- Similar to Section 115 CPC (pre-2002)
- Excess/lack of jurisdiction, illegality, material irregularity
```

#### D. Designation

```
District Judge = Principal Civil Judge of the district
Additional District Judges = Same powers as District Judge (for case load)

Administrative:
- District Judge is head of civil judiciary in district
- Supervises subordinate courts
- Transfers, inspections, administrative orders
```

### 4. Subordinate Civil Courts

#### A. Civil Judge (Senior Division)

```
Pecuniary:
- Typically: Rs. 5 lakhs to Rs. 50 lakhs (varies by state)
- Suits below District Court limit but above Junior Division

Powers:
- Try all civil suits within pecuniary limit
- Grant injunctions, specific performance, declarations
- Issue commissions, order discovery
```

#### B. Civil Judge (Junior Division)

```
Pecuniary:
- Typically: Up to Rs. 5 lakhs (varies by state)

Powers:
- Limited to pecuniary jurisdiction
- All civil remedies available (if within jurisdiction)
```

#### C. Small Causes Court (Provincial Small Cause Courts Act 1887)

```
Jurisdiction:
- Urban areas only (not rural)
- Small value disputes (typically up to Rs. 20,000-50,000)
- Specific categories: Rent, debt, account, partnership, contract

Procedure:
- Summary (speedy disposal)
- Informal pleadings
- Limited adjournments
- No decree, only "order" (BUT treated as decree for execution - Section 25)

Appeal:
- No appeal on FACTS (Section 25)
- Appeal to High Court on QUESTIONS OF LAW only
- Limitation: 30 days
```

### 5. Tribunals (Specialized)

```
Family Courts (Family Courts Act 1984):
- Matrimonial disputes (divorce, maintenance, custody)
- Guardianship under Guardians and Wards Act
- Appeal: High Court (within 90 days)

Debt Recovery Tribunals (SARFAESI Act 2002):
- Bank debt recovery (above Rs. 20 lakhs)
- Secured creditor enforcement
- Appeal: Debt Recovery Appellate Tribunal → High Court (Section 17)

National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) (Companies Act 2013):
- Company law disputes (oppression, winding up, mergers)
- Appeal: NCLAT → Supreme Court

Consumer Forums (Consumer Protection Act 2019):
- District: Up to Rs. 1 crore
- State: Rs. 1 crore to Rs. 10 crores
- National: Above Rs. 10 crores
- Appeal: State → National → Supreme Court

Motor Accidents Claims Tribunal (MACT) (Motor Vehicles Act 1988):
- Accident compensation claims
- Appeal: High Court (within 90 days)
```

## Appellate Flow Chart

### Scenario 1: Suit in Civil Judge (Junior Division)

```
Civil Judge (JD) → DECREE
          ↓
District Court (First Appeal under Section 96)
          ↓
High Court (Second Appeal under Section 100 - IF substantial question of law)
          ↓
Supreme Court (SLP under Article 136 OR certificate)
```

### Scenario 2: Suit in District Court (Original)

```
District Court → DECREE
          ↓
High Court (First Appeal under Section 96)
          ↓
Supreme Court (SLP under Article 136 OR certificate)

No second appeal (as District Court was original court, HC appeal is first appeal)
```

### Scenario 3: Suit in High Court (Original Side)

```
High Court (Original) → DECREE
          ↓
High Court (Appellate Side) - Division Bench (Letters Patent Appeal if available)
          ↓
Supreme Court (SLP under Article 136 OR certificate)

Note: Letters Patent Appeal exists only in Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, some other HCs
```

### Scenario 4: Small Causes Court

```
Small Causes Court → ORDER (treated as decree)
          ↓
High Court (Appeal ONLY on questions of law - Section 25 PSCCA)
          ↓
Supreme Court (SLP - rarely granted for small value disputes)
```

### Scenario 5: Family Court

```
Family Court → DECREE
          ↓
High Court (Appeal under Family Courts Act 1984)
          ↓
Supreme Court (SLP under Article 136)
```

### Scenario 6: Tribunal (NCLT/NCLAT)

```
NCLT → ORDER
          ↓
NCLAT (National Company Law Appellate Tribunal)
          ↓
Supreme Court (Direct appeal under Companies Act 2013 Section 423)
```

## Concurrent Jurisdiction

### When Multiple Courts Have Jurisdiction

```
Scenario:
Suit value: Rs. 30 lakhs
Cause of action: Delhi + Mumbai
Defendant: Resides in Delhi, business in Mumbai

Courts with jurisdiction:
1. Civil Judge (Senior Division) Delhi (pecuniary + territorial)
2. Civil Judge (Senior Division) Mumbai (pecuniary + territorial)
3. District Court Delhi (pecuniary + territorial)
4. District Court Mumbai (pecuniary + territorial)

Plaintiff's choice:
Can file in ANY of the above (concurrent jurisdiction)

Section 15 mandate:
SHOULD file in LOWEST competent court (Civil Judge SD)

Practical:
- Plaintiff often prefers higher court (District) for efficiency/expertise
- BUT if challenged, court may return plaint for filing in lower court (Order VII Rule 10)
```

## Transfer of Cases

### Section 24: Transfer from Lower Court to Higher Court

```
Grounds (Court may transfer on application OR suo motu):
1. Withdrawal of suit from court NOT competent → Transfer to competent court
2. Substantial question of law of general importance → Transfer to HC
3. Numerous persons interested → Transfer to prevent multiplicity
4. Ends of justice require

Procedure:
- Application by party OR court's own motion
- Notice to opposite party
- Hearing
- Order of transfer
- Case deemed filed in transferee court from original institution date

Effect on limitation:
Institution date in original court = Institution date in transferee court
(Benefit of original filing date preserved)
```

### Section 25: Transfer by Supreme Court/High Court

```
Supreme Court:
Can transfer any case pending in ANY court in India to:
- Any High Court, OR
- Any subordinate court

High Court:
Can transfer cases pending in subordinate courts within its jurisdiction to:
- Another subordinate court, OR
- Itself (if substantial question of law)

Grounds:
- Ends of justice
- Convenience of parties
- Important question of law
- Multiplicity of proceedings
```

## Powers of Courts on Appeal

### Section 107 Order 41 Rule 33 (Appellate Court Powers)

```
Appellate court may:

(a) Determine case finally:
    - Decide on merits
    - Pass decree affirming, varying, or reversing lower court decree

(b) Remand case:
    - Send back to lower court for fresh decision
    - Specific issues for reconsideration
    - Additional evidence if needed

(c) Frame issues and refer:
    - Frame specific issues
    - Send to lower court for finding on those issues
    - Return with findings for appellate court to decide

(d) Take additional evidence (Order 41 Rule 27):
    - If necessary for pronouncing judgment, OR
    - If lower court improperly refused/received evidence

(e) Reverse, affirm, vary:
    - Reverse: Set aside lower court decree
    - Affirm: Uphold lower court decree
    - Vary: Modify decree (increase/decrease relief)

(f) Set aside decree and remand:
    - If decree suffers from jurisdictional defect
    - If lower court decided on wrong grounds
    - Fresh decision needed

(g) Pass any other order:
    - As justice requires
```

## Review Jurisdiction

### Order 47 CPC (Review of Judgments and Orders)

```
Grounds for review (Rule 1):
1. Discovery of new and important matter/evidence which, after due diligence, 
   was not within knowledge or could not be produced at trial
2. Mistake or error apparent on face of record
3. Any other sufficient reason

Limitation: 30 days from date of decree/order (can be extended up to 30 more days)

Procedure:
- File review petition with grounds
- Court may dismiss at admission if no ground made out
- If admitted, hear parties and decide

Restrictions:
- Review NOT a re-hearing (cannot re-argue entire case)
- "Error apparent" = Obvious error visible on record (not debatable point)
- "Sufficient reason" = Narrow interpretation (fraud, natural justice violation)

Orders not reviewable:
- Order 47 Rule 1 proviso: Decree/order from which appeal preferred → Not reviewable
```

## Limitation Periods for Appeals

```
First Appeal from decree (Section 96):
- 90 days from date of decree
- Extension: Up to 90 more days (if sufficient cause - Section 5 Limitation Act)

Appeal from order (Section 104 + Order 43):
- 30 days from order
- Extension: Up to 30 more days

Second Appeal (Section 100):
- 90 days from decree of first appellate court
- Extension: Up to 90 more days

Appeal to Supreme Court (SLP - Article 136):
- 90 days from HC order/judgment
- Extension: Discretionary (SC may condone any delay)

Review (Order 47):
- 30 days from decree/order
- Extension: Up to 30 more days

Execution of decree:
- 12 years from date decree becomes final (Article 136 Limitation Act 1963)
```

## Common Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Which Court for Rs. 25 Lakh Suit?

```
Facts: Plaintiff wants to file Rs. 25 lakh money recovery suit

Pecuniary jurisdiction (assuming typical state limits):
- Civil Judge (Junior Division): Up to Rs. 5 lakhs → NO jurisdiction
- Civil Judge (Senior Division): Rs. 5-50 lakhs → HAS jurisdiction
- District Court: Above Rs. 50 lakhs OR unlimited → HAS jurisdiction

Section 15 mandate:
File in LOWEST competent court

Answer:
Civil Judge (Senior Division) is appropriate court
BUT District Court also has jurisdiction (if state notification permits)

Practical:
If plaintiff files in District Court, court MAY:
- Retain (if jurisdiction unlimited)
- Return plaint to file in Civil Judge SD (Order VII Rule 10)
```

### Scenario 2: Appeal Options After Decree

```
Facts:
- Suit filed in Civil Judge (Junior Division)
- Decree passed on DD/MM/YYYY
- Plaintiff/Defendant wants to appeal

First Appeal:
TO: District Court (Section 96)
TIME: 90 days from decree (extendable up to 90 more days)

If First Appeal dismissed/decreed in District Court:
Second Appeal:
TO: High Court (Section 100)
GROUND: ONLY if substantial question of law
TIME: 90 days from District Court decree (extendable up to 90 more days)

If Second Appeal dismissed/decreed in High Court:
Final Appeal:
TO: Supreme Court (Article 136 SLP)
TIME: 90 days from HC order (condonable)
DISCRETIONARY: SC not bound to grant leave
```

### Scenario 3: Concurrent Jurisdiction - Multiple Courts

```
Facts:
- Suit value: Rs. 40 lakhs
- Cause of action: Partly Mumbai, partly Delhi
- Defendant: Resides Mumbai, business in Delhi

Courts with jurisdiction:
1. Civil Judge (SD) Mumbai
2. Civil Judge (SD) Delhi
3. District Court Mumbai (if pecuniary jurisdiction permits)
4. District Court Delhi (if pecuniary jurisdiction permits)

Plaintiff files in: District Court Delhi

Can defendant object?
YES, on ground:
- Section 15: Should file in lowest competent court (Civil Judge SD)
- Court may return plaint (Order VII Rule 10)

Alternatively:
If defendant does not object → Submits to jurisdiction
Plaintiff can proceed in District Court
```

### Scenario 4: Transfer Due to Lack of Jurisdiction

```
Facts:
- Suit filed in Civil Judge (JD) Delhi
- Suit value later found to be Rs. 8 lakhs (above CJ JD limit)

Section 24 application:
- Plaintiff/Defendant applies for transfer
- Court finds pecuniary jurisdiction lacking
- Transfers to Civil Judge (SD) Delhi

Effect:
- Suit deemed filed in Civil Judge SD from original institution date
- Limitation preserved
- Evidence recorded in CJ JD → Valid in CJ SD
```

### Scenario 5: Revisional Jurisdiction Under Section 115

```
Facts:
- District Court passed order refusing to set aside ex parte decree
- Order appealable under Order 43? NO (not in Order 43 Rule 1 list)
- Can challenge in HC?

YES - Under Section 115 CPC (Revision):
Grounds:
- Subordinate court exercised jurisdiction not vested
- Failed to exercise jurisdiction vested
- Acted illegally or with material irregularity

HC can call for records and satisfy itself
BUT Section 115 limited post-2002 (not a regular appeal)
```

## Case Law

### 1. State of AP v. National Thermal Power Corporation (SC 2002)
```
Facts: Jurisdiction of Civil Court vs Arbitral Tribunal
Held:
- Court hierarchy must be respected
- Cannot bypass intermediate appellate forums
- Direct access to SC restricted (Article 136 discretionary)
```

### 2. Surya Dev Rai v. Ram Chander Rai (SC 2003)
```
Facts: Second appeal on questions of fact
Held:
- Section 100 (post-1976): ONLY substantial questions of law
- Questions of fact/mixed fact and law NOT appealable
- HC must formulate substantial question before admitting second appeal
```

### 3. Santosh Hazari v. Purushottam Tiwari (SC 2001)
```
Facts: Delay in filing appeal, condonation of delay
Held:
- Section 5 Limitation Act: Sufficient cause required
- Liberal approach in civil appeals (unlike criminal)
- BUT gross negligence/inaction → No condonation
- Maximum condonation: 90 days beyond 90 days for appeal from decree
```

### 4. Radhey Shyam v. Chhabi Nath (SC 2015)
```
Facts: Review petition, scope of "error apparent on face of record"
Held:
- Error apparent = Self-evident error; no elaborate argument needed
- Not debatable point of law
- Review NOT re-hearing
- Cannot re-argue case on merits
```

### 5. Shalini Shyam Shetty v. Rajendra Shankar Patil (SC 2010)
```
Facts: Letters Patent Appeal from Single Judge to Division Bench of HC
Held:
- LPA available only where Letters Patent exists (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, etc.)
- Not available in HCs without Letters Patent
- Intra-court appeal (within HC) limited to specific judgments
```

### 6. Laxminarayan v. Mst. Kamlabai (SC 1987)
```
Facts: Transfer of suit from one district to another for convenience
Held:
- Section 25: HC/SC can transfer for ends of justice
- Convenience of parties is relevant factor
- BUT not if amounts to forum shopping
- Balancing test: Plaintiff's choice vs defendant's convenience
```

## Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Filing Directly in High Court Original Side
```
❌ Wrong: Suit worth Rs. 50 lakhs filed in HC (where HC has original jurisdiction from Rs. 2 lakhs)
✓ Correct: File in District Court (Section 15 - lowest competent court)

Exception: HC retains if it accepts (discretionary)
```

### Mistake 2: Second Appeal on Questions of Fact
```
❌ Wrong: Appeal to HC from District Court decree challenging findings of fact
✓ Correct: Second appeal ONLY on substantial questions of LAW (Section 100 post-1976)

HC will dismiss at admission if no substantial legal question
```

### Mistake 3: Missing Appeal Limitation
```
❌ Wrong: File appeal after 90 days without condonation application
✓ Correct: File appeal within 90 days OR file with condonation application (up to 90 more days)

After 180 days → Decree becomes final (executable)
```

### Mistake 4: Review as Re-Hearing
```
❌ Wrong: File review petition re-arguing entire case on merits
✓ Correct: Review only for error apparent, new evidence, or sufficient reason (Order 47)

Review NOT a second appeal
```

### Mistake 5: Appealing Non-Appealable Order
```
❌ Wrong: Appeal from order dismissing application (not in Order 43 Rule 1 list)
✓ Correct: Only orders in Order 43 Rule 1 appealable; others challenge via revision (Section 115)

Check Order 43 list before filing appeal from order
```

# Protocol: Discovery and Inspection of Documents (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-CPC-DISCOVERY-INSPECTION  
**Domain**: Civil Procedure  
**Subdomain**: Evidence and Discovery  
**Version**: 1.0.0  
**Last Updated**: 2025-12-06

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on discovery of documents, inspection, admission/denial of documents, and production of documents under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908, ensuring fair disclosure and preventing surprise evidence.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "How to get documents from opposite party?"
- Need to discover what documents other party possesses
- Want to inspect documents mentioned in pleadings
- Opposing party not disclosing relevant documents
- Need to produce documents as evidence
- Challenge document's authenticity

## Core Principles

### 1. Discovery = Disclosure of Documents

```
Discovery (Order XI):
- Each party discloses documents in possession/power
- Related to matters in question
- Prevents "trial by ambush"
- Ensures fair trial (both parties know evidence)

Rule: "No document, no evidence"
→ Document not disclosed/admitted → Generally cannot be used at trial
→ Exception: Court's discretion (Order XIII Rule 2)
```

### 2. Purpose of Discovery and Inspection

```
1. Narrow Issues:
   - Know what documents exist
   - Focus trial on disputed documents

2. Prevent Surprise:
   - Opposite party not caught unaware
   - Time to prepare response

3. Fair Trial:
   - Both parties on equal footing
   - Access to relevant evidence

4. Judicial Efficiency:
   - Documents admitted → No need to prove
   - Documents denied → Only those need formal proof
```

## DISCOVERY OF DOCUMENTS (Order XI)

### Scope of Discovery (Order XI Rule 1)

```
Rule 1: "Court may, at any time, order any party to make discovery on oath of 
documents which are or have been in his possession or power relating to any 
matter in question in the suit"

Key Elements:

1. "Documents":
   - Deeds, contracts, letters, emails, SMS, WhatsApp chats
   - Invoices, receipts, vouchers
   - Photographs, videos, audio recordings (if reduced to tangible form)
   - Electronic records (under IT Act 2000)
   - Maps, plans, drawings

2. "In possession or power":
   - Currently in physical possession, OR
   - Legal right to obtain possession, OR
   - Practical ability to procure

3. "Relating to matter in question":
   - Relevant to issues in suit
   - Not privileged
   - Not oppressive to produce

NOT Discoverable:
- Privileged communications (lawyer-client, doctor-patient, priest-penitent)
- Self-incriminating documents (Article 20(3) Constitution)
- State secrets (Section 123 Evidence Act)
```

### Types of Discovery

#### A. Discovery by List (Order XI Rule 12)

```
Rule 12: Court may order party to file AFFIDAVIT OF DOCUMENTS

Affidavit shall contain:
1. Schedule 1: Documents in party's possession/power which he does not object to produce
2. Schedule 2: Documents in party's possession/power which he objects to produce (state grounds)
3. Schedule 3: Documents not in party's possession/power, but were at some time (state when parted with and current whereabouts)

Format of Affidavit of Documents:

"AFFIDAVIT OF DOCUMENTS

I, [Name], the [Plaintiff/Defendant] above named, make oath and say as follows:

1. I have in my possession or power the documents relating to the matters in 
   question in this suit set forth in Schedule 1 hereto, and I do not object 
   to produce the same.

2. I have in my possession or power the documents relating to the matters in 
   question in this suit set forth in Schedule 2 hereto, and I object to 
   produce the same on the following grounds: [State grounds - privilege, 
   self-incrimination, etc.]

3. I have had but have not now in my possession or power the documents relating 
   to the matters in question in this suit set forth in Schedule 3 hereto. 
   The said documents were last in my possession on [date] and are now in the 
   possession/power of [name/whereabouts unknown].

[Schedules attached]

Sworn before me this __ day of ____, 20__.

                                                          [Signature of Deponent]
```

**Schedule 1: Documents Party Will Produce**:
```
S.No. | Description of Document | Date | Remarks
------|-------------------------|------|--------
1. | Sale Agreement | 15/01/2024 | Between plaintiff and defendant
2. | Receipt for advance payment | 20/01/2024 | Rs. 1 lakh paid
3. | Legal Notice dated 05/03/2024 | 05/03/2024 | Calling upon defendant to perform
...
```

**Schedule 2: Documents Objected to Produce**:
```
S.No. | Description | Ground for Objection
------|-------------|--------------------
1. | Correspondence with lawyer dated 10/02/2024 | Legal professional privilege (Section 126 Evidence Act)
2. | Internal audit report | Not relevant to matters in question
...
```

**Schedule 3: Documents Not in Possession**:
```
S.No. | Description | When Parted With | Current Whereabouts
------|-------------|------------------|-------------------
1. | Original purchase deed | 01/06/2023 | Deposited with bank for loan
2. | Email correspondence with third party | Deleted | Unknown/not recoverable
...
```

#### B. Discovery by Interrogatories (Order XI Rule 1-10) - RARELY USED

```
Interrogatories = Written questions requiring written answers on oath

Rare in India (unlike common law jurisdictions):
- Not frequently ordered by courts
- Oral examination preferred (witness examination)

If Ordered:
1. Party files interrogatories (written questions)
2. Court allows specific interrogatories (not all)
3. Opposite party answers on oath
4. Answers admissible as evidence

Grounds for Interrogatories:
- Necessary for disposing fairly of suit
- Saving expense
- Opposite party has exclusive knowledge of facts
```

#### C. Discovery by Notice to Produce (Order XI Rule 15)

```
Rule 15: Either party may call on other to produce document referred to in 
pleadings or affidavits

Notice to Produce:
- Informal notice by advocate
- Requires opposite party to produce specific document(s) at trial/hearing
- If party fails to produce → Adverse inference (document would have been against him)

Format:
"NOTICE TO PRODUCE

TAKE NOTICE that you are hereby called upon to produce at the hearing of this 
suit on [date] the following documents referred to in your written statement/
affidavit:

1. [Description of document - e.g., \"Sale Agreement dated 15/01/2024 referred 
   to in para 3 of written statement\"]
2. [Description of document]

Dated: [Date]

                                                              [Advocate for Plaintiff]"

Effect:
- If document produced → Can be used as evidence
- If not produced → Court may presume document would have been unfavorable to party
```

### Privilege from Discovery (Order XI Rule 13)

```
Rule 13: No one shall be compelled to produce documents which:
(a) He could not be compelled to produce at trial (Section 123-129 Evidence Act)

Privileged Documents (Section 126-129 Evidence Act):

1. Legal Professional Privilege (Section 126):
   - Communications between lawyer and client
   - Purpose: Obtaining legal advice or for litigation
   - Client's privilege (not lawyer's)

2. State Secrets (Section 123):
   - Unpublished official records
   - Disclosure injurious to public interest
   - Court decides after examining document

3. Official Communications (Section 124):
   - Between public officers (if public interest harmed by disclosure)

4. Information about Commission of Offenses (Section 125):
   - Magistrate can forbid disclosure if public interest

5. Professional Communications (Section 126-129):
   - Lawyer-client (Section 126)
   - Interpreters, clerks (Section 127)
   - Production of title deeds (Section 128)
   - Suits for partition (Section 129 - title deeds need not be produced)

Self-Incrimination Privilege:
Article 20(3) Constitution + Section 132 Evidence Act:
- Witness cannot be compelled to answer if tends to expose to criminal charge
- Extends to production of documents (Sharma v. Satish Chandra)

Grounds for Objection to Production:
- Document privileged (state which privilege)
- Not relevant to matters in question
- Oppressive/vexatious (fishing expedition)
- Trade secret/confidential commercial information (court may allow inspection under confidentiality)
```

## INSPECTION OF DOCUMENTS (Order XI Rule 14)

```
Rule 14: After discovery, party entitled to INSPECT documents disclosed (Schedule 1)

Purpose:
- Verify existence of documents
- Examine contents
- Decide which to use as evidence
- Prepare case

Procedure:

Step 1: After Affidavit of Documents Filed
- Party files application for inspection of documents in Schedule 1

Step 2: Time and Place Fixed
- Court fixes time for inspection (usually at opposite party's advocate's office)
- Opposite party produces documents

Step 3: Inspection
- Applicant (or advocate) inspects documents
- Can take notes, photographs (with court permission)
- Can request certified copies

Step 4: Selection
- Party selects which documents to rely on
- Includes in own list of documents (if needs to use at trial)

Effect:
- Inspected document → Can be used at trial (if relevant)
- If party refuses inspection → Court may order production OR dismiss party's case
```

## PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS AT TRIAL (Order XIII)

### Documents to Be Produced (Order XIII Rule 1)

```
Rule 1: Party intending to rely on document must:
1. Annex document to plaint/written statement (if in possession)
   OR
2. Enter document in list of documents filed
   OR
3. Give notice to opposite party to produce (Order XI Rule 15)

Document NOT in Above Categories:
- Generally CANNOT be used at trial
- Exception: Court's discretion (Rule 2 - if sufficient cause shown)
```

### Admission and Denial of Documents (Order XIII Rule 2, 4)

```
Rule 2: Court may summon parties before trial to:
- Admit/deny documents
- Narrow issues for trial

Procedure:

Step 1: Notice to Admit Documents
- Party serves notice calling on opposite party to admit documents
- Opposite party must admit/deny within prescribed time (usually 7-10 days)

Step 2: Opposite Party's Response
Options:
(a) Admit: Document proved (no need for formal proof at trial)
(b) Deny: Document disputed (must be proved at trial by calling witness/certifying officer)
(c) Refuse to admit/deny: Deemed not admitted (must be proved)

Consequence of Denial:
- If document later proved at trial → Denying party may pay costs of proof (Section 35 Evidence Act)
- If genuine denial (document later not proved) → No costs penalty
```

### Proof of Documents If Not Admitted (Order XIII Rule 4)

```
If document denied → Must be proved at trial

Modes of Proof (Evidence Act):

1. Public Documents (Section 74-78):
   - Certified copy sufficient (Section 76)
   - Examples: Government gazette, registered deed, court judgment

2. Private Documents (Section 67-73):
   - Original produced + proved by:
     * Attesting witness (if attested) - Section 68
     * Execution admitted (Section 69)
     * Handwriting proved by expert/familiar witness (Section 47, 67)
     * Comparison of handwriting (Section 73)

3. Electronic Records (Section 65B IT Act 2000):
   - Certificate under Section 65B(4) required
   - Certifies: Computer used, data source, etc.
   - Without certificate → Inadmissible (Arjun Panditrao v. Kailash Kushanrao, SC 2020)

Adverse Inference (Section 114(g) Evidence Act):
"Court may presume that evidence which could be produced and is not produced 
would, if produced, be unfavorable to the person who withholds it"
```

### Impounding Documents (Order XIII Rule 9)

```
Rule 9: Court may impound document if:
- Appears to be forged, OR
- Materially altered, OR
- Inadmissible in evidence

Procedure:
1. Mark document "Impounded"
2. Send to police for investigation (if forgery suspected)
3. Await police report/chargesheet
4. Trial continues (impounded document not used)
```

## PRODUCTION BY THIRD PARTY (Order XVI Rule 6)

```
Rule 6: If document in possession of third party (not party to suit):

Procedure:

Step 1: Application for Summons to Produce
- Party applies to court
- Requests summons to third party

Step 2: Court Issues Summons
- Summons to third party to produce document on specified date
- OR deposit with court

Step 3: Third Party's Options
(a) Produce document
(b) Object to production (if privileged/not relevant)
(c) Seek costs/protection of confidentiality

Step 4: Court Orders Production or Rejects
- If third party fails to produce without valid excuse → Contempt of court
```

## ELECTRONIC DOCUMENTS (IT Act 2000 + Evidence Act)

### Discoverability

```
Section 65A Evidence Act:
"Electronic records admissible subject to Section 65B"

Section 65B: Certificate Required
For electronic record to be admissible:
- Certificate under Section 65B(4) stating:
  * Computer from which record produced
  * Computer regularly used for storing/processing information
  * Information fed into computer in ordinary course
  * Computer was operating properly (or defect did not affect record)

Arjun Panditrao v. Kailash Kushanrao (SC 2020):
"Section 65B certificate is MANDATORY; no exception unless original electronic 
record produced and proved under Section 67"

Discovery of Electronic Documents:
- Emails: Discoverable (party must produce printouts + Section 65B certificate)
- WhatsApp chats: Discoverable (screenshots + Section 65B certificate)
- SMS: Discoverable (call detail record + Section 65B certificate)
- Social media posts: Discoverable (printout/screenshot + Section 65B certificate)
```

### Procedure for Electronic Evidence

```
Step 1: Disclose in Affidavit of Documents
- "Email dated 15/01/2024 from defendant to plaintiff"

Step 2: Produce at Inspection
- Printout of email with headers (showing sender, recipient, date, time)

Step 3: At Trial
(a) Produce original electronic device (phone/laptop), OR
(b) Produce printout + Section 65B certificate

Section 65B Certificate Format:
"I, [Name], [Designation], certify that:
1. The electronic record containing [description] was produced by computer 
   [details].
2. The said computer was regularly used for storing and processing information 
   in the ordinary course of [business/activities].
3. During the material period, information was regularly fed into the computer.
4. The computer was operating properly at the material time, or if not, the 
   defect did not affect the electronic record or its accuracy.
5. The information in the electronic record reproduces or is derived from 
   information fed into the computer in the ordinary course.

[Signature]
[Date]"

Effect:
- With certificate → Electronic record admissible
- Without certificate → Inadmissible (unless original device produced + proved under Section 67)
```

## Common Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Discovering Contract in Defendant's Possession

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff claims contract exists
- Plaintiff lost his copy
- Defendant has original

Solution:

Step 1: In Plaint
- State: "Original contract in defendant's possession" (Order VII Rule 14)

Step 2: Apply for Discovery
- Application under Order XI Rule 12 for defendant to file affidavit of documents

Step 3: Defendant Files Affidavit
- Schedule 1: Includes contract (if willing to produce)
- OR Schedule 2: Objects to produce (state ground)

Step 4: Apply for Inspection
- Plaintiff applies to inspect documents in Schedule 1
- Inspect contract, verify existence

Step 5: Notice to Produce (Order XI Rule 15)
- Serve notice on defendant to produce contract at trial

Step 6: At Trial
- If defendant produces → Use as evidence
- If defendant refuses → Adverse inference (Section 114(g)) + Court may order production
```

### Scenario 2: Opponent Not Disclosing Relevant Documents

```
Facts:
- Defendant claims payment made by cheque
- Defendant not annexing cheque copy to written statement
- Plaintiff wants to see cheque

Solution:

Step 1: Notice to Admit Documents
- Plaintiff serves notice asking defendant to admit/deny existence of cheque

Step 2: Defendant's Response
- If admits → Cheque proved (no need to see)
- If denies → Plaintiff challenges at trial (defendant must prove payment)
- If ignores → Deemed not admitted

Step 3: Apply for Discovery
- Application under Order XI Rule 12 for defendant to file affidavit listing all payment documents

Step 4: Defendant Files Affidavit
- Schedule 1: Includes cheque copy (if exists and willing to produce)

Step 5: Apply for Inspection
- Inspect cheque, verify details (amount, date, payee)

Step 6: Notice to Produce
- Require defendant to produce original cheque at trial (for bank verification if needed)
```

### Scenario 3: Privileged Document (Lawyer-Client Communication)

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff applies for discovery
- Defendant has email to lawyer seeking legal advice

Defendant's Response:

Affidavit of Documents:
- Schedule 2: "Email dated 10/02/2024 to lawyer [Name] seeking legal advice"
- Ground for objection: Legal professional privilege (Section 126 Evidence Act)

Plaintiff's Options:
(a) Accept privilege claim (cannot access document)
(b) Challenge privilege (apply to court to inspect)

Court Decides:
- May inspect document in camera (private)
- Decide if truly privileged
- If privileged → No production
- If not privileged (e.g., email discussing facts, not seeking advice) → Order production
```

### Scenario 4: Electronic Evidence (WhatsApp Chat)

```
Facts:
- Defendant sent WhatsApp message admitting breach
- Plaintiff wants to use as evidence

Procedure:

Step 1: Disclose in Plaint
- State: "Defendant sent WhatsApp message dated 15/01/2024 admitting breach"

Step 2: Discovery
- Include in list of documents: "Screenshot of WhatsApp chat dated 15/01/2024"

Step 3: At Trial
- Produce screenshot (printout)
- Produce Section 65B certificate:
  * Computer/phone from which screenshot taken
  * WhatsApp regularly used for communication
  * Screenshot taken in ordinary course
  * Phone operating properly

Step 4: Proof
- With certificate → Admissible
- Without certificate → Inadmissible (Arjun Panditrao)

Alternative (if defendant admits):
- Serve notice to admit WhatsApp message
- If admitted → No need for Section 65B certificate
```

### Scenario 5: Document in Bank's Possession

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff needs bank statement to prove defendant's account transactions
- Bank not party to suit

Solution:

Step 1: Application for Summons (Order XVI Rule 6)
- Request court to summon bank to produce defendant's bank statement

Step 2: Bank's Response
- Objects: Banking secrecy (customer confidentiality)
- OR Produces: If defendant consents OR court orders

Step 3: Court Decision
- May order bank to produce (if relevant to suit and court overrides confidentiality in interest of justice)
- May reject (if privilege/confidentiality stronger than relevance)

Alternative:
- Defendant voluntarily produces bank statement (avoids third-party summons)
```

## Case Law

### 1. Arjun Panditrao Londhe v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (SC 2020)
```
Facts: Electronic evidence (call detail record) produced without Section 65B certificate
Held:
- Section 65B certificate MANDATORY for electronic evidence
- No exception to certificate requirement
- Without certificate → Electronic evidence inadmissible
- Overrules previous judgments allowing electronic evidence without certificate
```

### 2. Sharma v. Satish Chandra (SC 1970)
```
Facts: Compulsory production of documents tending to incriminate
Held:
- Article 20(3) protection against self-incrimination extends to documents
- Cannot compel production if document tends to expose to criminal charge
- Privilege to be claimed by party (not automatic)
```

### 3. Duncan v. Cammell Laird (UK 1942, persuasive in India)
```
Facts: State claiming privilege for official documents
Held:
- State can claim privilege if disclosure injurious to public interest
- BUT court examines document (in camera if needed) to decide privilege validity
- Blanket claim insufficient; must show specific injury to public interest
```

### 4. Ramesh Chandra Agrawal v. Regency Hospital (SC 2009)
```
Facts: Defendant not producing documents disclosed in affidavit
Held:
- After disclosure in affidavit (Schedule 1), party MUST produce on inspection
- Refusal → Court may draw adverse inference OR strike out defense
- Discovery imposes obligation to produce (not optional)
```

### 5. Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer (SC 2014) [OVERRULED by Arjun Panditrao]
```
Facts: Secondary electronic evidence admissibility
Held (at that time, now overruled):
- Section 65B certificate not mandatory if original electronic device produced
- Computer printout could be proved under Section 63 (secondary evidence)

Arjun Panditrao (2020) overruled this:
- Section 65B certificate ALWAYS required (no exception)
```

# Protocol: Evidence and Examination of Witnesses (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-CPC-EVIDENCE-EXAMINATION  
**Domain**: Civil Procedure  
**Subdomain**: Evidence and Discovery  
**Version**: 1.0.0  
**Last Updated**: 2025-12-06

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on examination of witnesses, recording of evidence, and evidence procedures in civil suits under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 and Evidence Act 1872.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "How to examine witnesses in civil court?"
- Need to prepare for witness examination
- Recording evidence in civil trial
- Cross-examination strategy needed
- Challenge to witness credibility
- Documentary evidence procedures

## Core Principles

### 1. Evidence = Proof of Facts

```
Evidence (Section 3 Evidence Act):
- All statements permitted by court (oral evidence)
- All documents produced for inspection (documentary evidence)

Purpose:
- Prove facts in issue
- Relevant facts
- Establish/disprove claim/defense

Rule: "He who asserts must prove" (Section 101-102 Evidence Act)
→ Burden of proof on party asserting fact
→ Standard: Preponderance of probabilities (civil); Beyond reasonable doubt (criminal)
```

### 2. Examination Stages (Order XVIII Rule 4)

```
Three-Stage Examination:

1. EXAMINATION-IN-CHIEF:
   - By party calling witness
   - Witness states facts in support of case
   - Leading questions NOT allowed (except formal/undisputed)

2. CROSS-EXAMINATION:
   - By opposite party
   - Test credibility, challenge testimony
   - Leading questions ALLOWED

3. RE-EXAMINATION:
   - By party who called witness
   - Clarify/explain matters arising in cross-examination
   - Leading questions NOT allowed
   - Limited to cross-examination scope
```

## WITNESS EXAMINATION PROCEDURE (Order XVIII)

### Summoning Witnesses (Order XVI)

```
Order XVI Rule 1: Summons to Witness

When Issued:
- Party applies for summons
- Court issues summons commanding witness to attend

Summons Contains:
- Name and address of witness
- Date, time, place of appearance
- Whether for examination OR to produce documents
- Consequence of non-attendance (warrant, fine, contempt)

Service (Rule 2):
- By court process server
- Registered post
- Witness must be tendered reasonable expenses (Batta) - Rule 3

Compulsion (Rule 10):
- If witness fails to attend → Warrant of arrest
- Fine up to Rs. 500
- Attachment of property
- Contempt proceedings

Exemptions (Rule 6):
- President, Governor: Not compelled to attend
- Other dignitaries: Commission to record evidence at their place
```

### Examination-in-Chief (Order XVIII Rule 4)

```
Purpose:
- Witness states facts supporting party's case
- Foundation of party's evidence

Procedure:

1. Oath/Affirmation (Section 136 Evidence Act):
   - Every witness sworn before examination
   - Form: "I swear in the name of God that I will state the truth, the whole 
     truth, and nothing but the truth"
   - Affirmation (for non-believers): "I solemnly affirm..."

2. Witness Identifies Himself:
   - Name, age, occupation, address
   - Relationship to parties (if any)

3. Examination by Calling Party:
   - Questions by advocate of party calling witness
   - Witness answers orally
   - Court records testimony

4. Leading Questions Prohibited (Section 141-143):
   - Leading Question = Suggests answer
   - Example (LEADING): "Did defendant pay you Rs. 5 lakhs?" → Suggests "yes"
   - Example (NON-LEADING): "What amount, if any, did defendant pay you?"

   Exceptions (Leading Allowed):
   - Formal matters (name, age, address)
   - Undisputed matters
   - Hostile witness (with court permission)
   - Contradicting witness with prior statement

5. Evidence Recorded (Rule 5):
   - Judge's dictation (verbatim or narrative form)
   - Typed/written
   - Read to witness for confirmation
   - Witness signs each page

Modern Practice:
- Examination-in-chief by AFFIDAVIT (allowed by many courts to save time)
- Witness files affidavit (pre-prepared testimony)
- At trial, witness affirms affidavit
- Straight to cross-examination
```

**Examination-in-Chief Affidavit Format** (Modern Practice):

```
IN THE COURT OF [COURT NAME]

Suit No. ___/20__

AFFIDAVIT OF [WITNESS NAME] (PLAINTIFF'S WITNESS No. __)

I, [Name], aged [__] years, S/o [Father's name], residing at [Address], do 
hereby solemnly affirm and state as follows:

1. I am the [plaintiff/defendant/witness] in the above suit and am well 
   acquainted with the facts and circumstances of the case.

2. [State facts in chronological narrative - para-wise]

3. I have perused the documents annexed to this affidavit marked as Exhibit 
   PW1/1, PW1/2, ... and state that the same are true copies and I am 
   conversant with the contents thereof.

4. I have read and understood the contents of this affidavit and the same are 
   true to my knowledge and belief.

DEPONENT

Verification:
I, the deponent above named, verify that the contents of paragraphs 1 to __ 
are true to my personal knowledge, and no part of it is false and nothing 
material has been concealed.

Verified at [Place] on this __ day of ____, 20__.

DEPONENT
```

### Cross-Examination (Order XVIII Rule 4, Section 137-146 Evidence Act)

```
Purpose:
- Test credibility of witness
- Elicit facts favorable to cross-examining party
- Discredit/impeach witness testimony
- Challenge version given in examination-in-chief

Rights of Cross-Examiner (Section 138):

1. Test Veracity (Credibility):
   - Previous inconsistent statements
   - Character for untruthfulness (with limitations)
   - Bias, interest, motive to lie

2. Shake Credit:
   - Prior convictions (Section 145)
   - Prior inconsistent statements (Section 145)
   - Imputation of bad character (with caution - may allow re-examination)

3. Elicit Facts Favorable to Cross-Examiner's Case:
   - Leading questions ALLOWED (Section 142)
   - Can put propositions (witness may deny)

Leading Questions Allowed:
- "Is it not true that you were not present at the scene?"
- "You received Rs. 10 lakhs from defendant, correct?"
- "Isn't it a fact that you have a dispute with plaintiff?"

Limitations:

1. Cannot Ask Irrelevant Questions:
   - Court may disallow scandalous/vexatious questions (Section 151)

2. Cannot Insult/Annoy Witness:
   - Questions must be pertinent
   - Cannot harass unnecessarily

3. Privilege Protections (Section 121-132):
   - Cannot ask about privileged communications (lawyer-client, state secrets)
   - Self-incrimination (Article 20(3), Section 132)

Techniques:

1. Prior Inconsistent Statements (Section 145):
   - Put: "In your police statement dated DD/MM/YYYY, you said [X]. Today you 
     say [Y]. Which is correct?"
   - If witness denies inconsistency → Prove by producing statement

2. Omissions:
   - "You mentioned [fact] today. But in your affidavit, you did not mention 
     this. Why?"

3. Improbabilities:
   - "You claim you saw accused's face from 100 meters at night without street 
     lights. How is that possible?"

4. Interest/Bias:
   - "You are plaintiff's brother, correct?"
   - "You have a pending lawsuit against defendant yourself, right?"

5. Contradictions with Documents:
   - "This receipt shows payment on 15/01. But you say payment was on 20/01. 
     Explain."
```

### Re-Examination (Order XVIII Rule 4, Section 137 Evidence Act)

```
Purpose:
- Clarify matters raised in cross-examination
- Rehabilitate witness (if credibility shaken)
- Explain apparent contradictions

Scope (Section 137):
"Re-examination may be directed to explanation of matters referred to in 
cross-examination; and if new matter is, by permission of Court, introduced 
in re-examination, opposite party may further cross-examine upon that matter."

Restrictions:

1. Limited to Cross-Examination Scope:
   - Cannot introduce new facts NOT touched in cross-examination
   - Can only clarify/explain cross-examination matters

2. Leading Questions NOT Allowed:
   - Same as examination-in-chief

3. Court Permission for New Matter:
   - If advocate wants to introduce new fact → Seek court permission
   - If allowed → Opposite party gets right to further cross-examine on that new matter

Example:

Cross-Examination:
Q: "You said payment was Rs. 5 lakhs. But receipt shows Rs. 3 lakhs. Explain."
A: "Receipt is for partial payment only."

Re-Examination (Clarifying):
Q: "You mentioned partial payment. Please explain fully."
A: "Rs. 3 lakhs by cheque (receipt given). Rs. 2 lakhs in cash (no receipt as 
    defendant insisted on cash)."

Result: Clarification of apparent contradiction raised in cross-examination.
```

### Recall of Witness (Order XVIII Rule 17)

```
Rule 17: Court may, at any stage, recall witness for further examination

Grounds:
- To clear doubt on any point
- New facts emerged requiring clarification
- Interests of justice

Procedure:
- Party applies OR court suo motu
- Witness re-summoned
- Further examination (may be by court itself)

Effect:
- Testimony continues (not fresh examination)
- Previous testimony stands unless contradicted/explained
```

## TYPES OF EVIDENCE

### A. Oral Evidence (Section 59-60 Evidence Act)

```
Section 59: All facts, except contents of documents, must be proved by oral evidence

Oral Evidence = Statements of witnesses in court

Must Be:
- DIRECT: Witness testifies about facts personally perceived (seen/heard/touched)
- NOT HEARSAY (generally): Witness cannot testify about what someone else told him
  (Exception: Section 32-33 - dying declaration, admissions, etc.)

Example (ADMISSIBLE Direct Evidence):
Witness: "I saw defendant sign the contract on 15/01/2024 in my presence"

Example (INADMISSIBLE Hearsay):
Witness: "My friend told me defendant signed contract" → Hearsay (unless friend is examined)
```

### B. Documentary Evidence (Section 61-90A Evidence Act)

```
Documentary Evidence = Documents produced for court inspection

Types:

1. Public Documents (Section 74-78):
   - Government records, gazettes, registered deeds, court judgments
   - Proved by certified copy (Section 76)

2. Private Documents (Section 67-73):
   - Contracts, letters, receipts, agreements
   - Proved by:
     * Original + attesting witness (Section 68)
     * OR handwriting comparison (Section 73)
     * OR admission of execution (Section 69)

3. Electronic Records (Section 65B IT Act):
   - Emails, digital signatures, computer printouts
   - Proved by Section 65B certificate (MANDATORY - Arjun Panditrao)

Primary Evidence (Section 62):
- Original document itself

Secondary Evidence (Section 63):
- Certified copy, photocopy, oral account
- Allowed ONLY if original:
  * In possession of opposite party (and not produced after notice)
  * Lost/destroyed
  * Cannot be moved (e.g., inscription on building)
  * Public document (certified copy permissible)
```

### C. Expert Evidence (Section 45-51 Evidence Act)

```
Section 45: Opinion of Expert Admissible

When Expert Opinion Relevant:
- Foreign law
- Science, art, handwriting, fingerprints
- Valuations (property, damages)
- Medical opinion

Who is Expert?
Person with specialized knowledge/skill beyond ordinary person

Examples:
- Handwriting expert (for signature verification)
- Medical expert (for injury causation)
- Engineer (for construction defects)
- Chartered Accountant (for financial analysis)

Procedure:
1. Party summons expert witness
2. Expert examines subject matter (document/property/records)
3. Expert prepares report
4. Expert testifies in court (examination-in-chief, cross, re-examination)
5. Court considers expert opinion (not binding, but persuasive)

Limitation (Section 45 proviso):
Expert opinion is OPINION, not fact
Court NOT bound to accept (may reject if finds expert unreliable/biased)
```

### D. Circumstantial Evidence

```
Circumstantial Evidence = Evidence of circumstances from which fact in issue can be inferred

Example:
Fact in Issue: Whether defendant was at scene on 15/01
Circumstantial Evidence:
- Defendant's car seen near scene at that time
- Defendant's fingerprints found at scene
- Witness saw defendant leaving scene

Standard (Sharad Birdhichand Sarda, SC 1984):
"Circumstances must be:
1. Conclusive in nature
2. Exclude every hypothesis except the one to be proved
3. Complete chain (no missing link)"

Civil Suits:
- Preponderance of probabilities sufficient (unlike criminal "beyond reasonable doubt")
```

## HOSTILE WITNESS (Section 154 Evidence Act)

```
Section 154: Court may permit leading questions if witness HOSTILE

Hostile Witness:
- Witness who, though called by party, gives evidence adverse to that party
- OR refuses to answer questions
- OR contradicts earlier statement

Procedure:

Step 1: Party Realizes Witness Hostile
- Witness giving contrary evidence than expected

Step 2: Apply to Court for Permission
- Request to treat witness as hostile
- Court grants permission (discretion)

Step 3: Leading Questions Allowed
- Party who called witness can ask leading questions
- Cross-examine own witness (to discredit)

Step 4: Contradict with Prior Statement (Section 145)
- Show previous statement (e.g., affidavit, police statement)
- Confront witness with inconsistency

Effect:
- Witness's testimony may be rejected/given less weight
- Party may adduce other evidence to prove fact
```

## RECORDING OF EVIDENCE (Order XVIII Rule 5)

### Judge's Dictation

```
Traditional Method (Rule 5(1)):
- Judge dictates evidence
- Stenographer/clerk types
- Read to witness for confirmation
- Witness signs

Modern Practice:
- Electronic recording (audio/video) - permitted by some courts
- Stenography (STENO)
- Transcript prepared and verified

Essential Requirements:
1. Evidence recorded in presence of parties/advocates
2. Witness confirms accuracy
3. Witness signs each page (Rule 5(2))
4. Judge signs record (Rule 5(2))

Language:
- English (in higher courts)
- Vernacular (in lower courts, if witness does not understand English)
- Interpreter used if needed
```

### Evidence by Affidavit (Order XIX Rule 1)

```
Modern Practice (to expedite trials):
Court may order evidence by AFFIDAVIT (instead of oral examination-in-chief)

Procedure:
1. Witness files affidavit (pre-prepared testimony)
2. Affidavit served on opposite party
3. At trial, witness called
4. Witness affirms affidavit contents
5. Straight to cross-examination (no examination-in-chief)

Advantages:
- Saves time (no lengthy examination-in-chief)
- Evidence clear and structured

Disadvantages:
- Witness may not remember all details (if affidavit prepared by advocate)
- Cross-examination more difficult (witness may rely on "as stated in affidavit")

Court's Power (Rule 2):
- Court may require witness to attend for cross-examination
- Court may require further clarification beyond affidavit
```

## COMMISSION EVIDENCE (Order XVI Rule 6-16)

```
When Ordered:
- Witness resides outside court's jurisdiction
- Witness unable to attend (illness, age, other sufficient cause)
- Dignitary (President, Governor, etc.)

Procedure:

Step 1: Application for Commission
- Party applies stating witness details and reason for commission

Step 2: Court Issues Commission
- Letter of request to court in witness's area
- OR appointing commissioner (advocate/officer)

Step 3: Commission Executed
- Commissioner examines witness (examination-in-chief, cross, re-examination)
- Evidence recorded
- Advocates of both parties may attend (if commission in India)

Step 4: Return of Commission
- Commissioner sends report and evidence to court
- Evidence read and treated as if recorded in court

Limitation:
- Less effective than in-person examination (cannot gauge demeanor)
- But necessary if witness genuinely cannot attend
```

## BURDEN OF PROOF (Section 101-106 Evidence Act)

```
Section 101: Burden of Proof on Party Asserting Fact

General Rule: "HE WHO ASSERTS MUST PROVE"

Plaintiff's Burden:
- Prove all facts necessary to establish claim
- If fails → Suit dismissed

Defendant's Burden:
- Prove affirmative defenses (payment, limitation, res judicata, etc.)
- If fails → Defense fails

Exceptions (Statutory Presumptions):

Section 79-85: Presumptions as to Documents
- Section 79: Certified copies of public documents presumed genuine
- Section 81-85: Gazettes, government acts, court proceedings presumed genuine

Section 111-114: Burden of Proof in Certain Cases
- Section 111: Burden on party claiming relationship (if possession with another)
- Section 114(g): Adverse inference if evidence withheld

Illustration:
Suit for money recovery:
- Plaintiff proves: (1) Loan advanced (2) Demand made (3) No payment
- Defendant claims: "I repaid"
  → Burden on DEFENDANT to prove payment (Section 106 - fact especially within knowledge)
```

## ADMISSIONS AND CONFESSIONS (Section 17-23 Evidence Act)

```
Section 17: Admission = Statement suggesting inference as to fact in issue or relevant fact

Types:

1. Judicial Admissions:
   - Made in pleadings, affidavits, court proceedings
   - Binding on party (cannot be contradicted without amendment)
   - Example: Defendant admits in written statement "Contract was signed"
     → No need for plaintiff to prove contract (admitted fact)

2. Extra-Judicial Admissions:
   - Made outside court (letters, conversations, emails)
   - NOT conclusive (can be explained/denied)
   - Admissible as evidence

Section 18: By Whom Admitted
- Party OR person whose position party represents (legal representative, partner, agent)

Section 21: Proof of Admissions
- Oral admissions proved by calling person to whom admission made
- Documentary admissions proved by producing document

Effect:
- Admission shifts burden (but not conclusive in civil suits)
- Can be explained/retracted with valid reason
```

## COMMON OBJECTIONS TO EVIDENCE

```
1. "Leading Question" (Section 141):
   - During examination-in-chief or re-examination
   - Court may disallow

2. "Irrelevant" (Section 5-55):
   - Question not related to facts in issue/relevant facts
   - Court may disallow

3. "Hearsay" (Section 60):
   - Witness testifying about what someone else said (not personally perceived)
   - Generally inadmissible (unless exception applies - Section 32-33)

4. "Privileged Communication" (Section 121-132):
   - Lawyer-client, state secret, self-incrimination
   - Court may sustain objection

5. "Asked and Answered":
   - Question already asked and answered (repetition)
   - Court may disallow

6. "Fishing Expedition":
   - Vague/speculative question to elicit information without basis
   - Court may disallow

7. "Argumentative":
   - Question arguing with witness (not seeking facts)
   - Court may disallow

8. "Compound Question":
   - Multiple questions in one
   - Court may ask to break into separate questions

9. "Assuming Facts Not in Evidence":
   - Question assumes fact not yet proved
   - Court may disallow
```

## Common Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Witness Turns Hostile

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff calls witness (expected to support case)
- Witness gives contrary evidence in examination-in-chief

Procedure:

Step 1: Apply to Court
- Advocate: "My Lord, I seek permission to treat this witness as hostile"

Step 2: Court Grants Permission
- Court: "Permission granted under Section 154 Evidence Act"

Step 3: Cross-Examine Own Witness
- Leading questions allowed
- Confront with prior statement:
  "In your affidavit dated DD/MM/YYYY, you stated [X]. Today you say [Y]. 
  Which is correct?"

Step 4: Prove Prior Inconsistent Statement
- Produce affidavit/police statement
- Mark as exhibit
- Witness may admit or deny prior statement

Effect:
- Witness's testimony may be disregarded
- Plaintiff may call other witnesses to prove fact
```

### Scenario 2: Objection to Leading Question

```
Examination-in-Chief:

Plaintiff's Advocate: "Did defendant pay you Rs. 5 lakhs on 15/01/2024?"

Defendant's Advocate: "Objection, My Lord. Leading question."

Court: "Sustained. Rephrase."

Plaintiff's Advocate: "What amount, if any, did defendant pay you?"

Witness: "Defendant paid me Rs. 5 lakhs."

Plaintiff's Advocate: "When?"

Witness: "On 15/01/2024."

Effect: Non-leading form elicits same facts without suggesting answer
```

### Scenario 3: Impeachment by Prior Inconsistent Statement

```
Cross-Examination:

Defendant's Advocate: "Mr. Witness, you testified that the contract was signed 
in Mumbai, correct?"

Witness: "Yes."

Defendant's Advocate: "I put it to you that in your police statement dated 
05/03/2024, you stated the contract was signed in Delhi. Which is correct?"

Witness: "I don't recall saying Delhi. It was Mumbai."

Defendant's Advocate: "My Lord, I wish to prove prior inconsistent statement 
under Section 145 Evidence Act. I produce the witness's police statement marked 
as Exhibit DW1/1."

Court: "Admitted."

Defendant's Advocate (reading): "In your police statement, paragraph 3, you 
stated: 'The contract was signed at my office in Delhi on 15/01/2024.' Do you 
deny this?"

Witness: "I may have been mistaken in the police statement."

Effect: Witness's credibility shaken; inconsistency noted by court
```

### Scenario 4: Expert Opinion (Handwriting)

```
Facts:
- Defendant denies signature on contract
- Plaintiff calls handwriting expert

Examination-in-Chief (Expert Witness):

Q: "What is your qualification and experience?"
A: "I am a certified handwriting expert with 20 years' experience. I have 
    examined over 5,000 documents for courts."

Q: "Did you examine the disputed signature on the contract?"
A: "Yes, I examined the signature on the contract dated 15/01/2024 marked as 
    Exhibit P-1."

Q: "Did you compare it with admitted signatures of defendant?"
A: "Yes, I compared with defendant's signatures on his passport, PAN card, and 
    bank documents provided to me."

Q: "What is your opinion?"
A: "In my expert opinion, the disputed signature on the contract is GENUINE and 
    was executed by the defendant. The slope, pressure, and unique features 
    match the admitted signatures."

Cross-Examination:

Q: "Mr. Expert, is handwriting analysis 100% accurate?"
A: "No field is 100% accurate. But handwriting analysis, when done properly with 
    adequate samples, has high accuracy."

Q: "Have you ever been wrong?"
A: "In my career, there have been instances where experts differed in opinion."

Q: "So your opinion is not conclusive?"
A: "It is my expert opinion based on my training and experience. Court is free 
    to accept or reject."

Court's Decision:
- Expert opinion considered (persuasive, not binding)
- If corroborated by other evidence → Signature held genuine
- If contradicted → Court may reject expert opinion
```

## Case Law

### 1. Suresh Chandra Bahri v. State of Bihar (SC 1995)
```
Facts: Examination-in-chief by affidavit challenged
Held:
- Court can permit examination-in-chief by affidavit (Order XIX Rule 1)
- Saves time and expedites trials
- Witness must be available for cross-examination
- If cross-examination reveals inconsistencies, affidavit evidence may be discredited
```

### 2. Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra (SC 1984)
```
Facts: Conviction based on circumstantial evidence
Held:
- Circumstantial evidence must form complete chain
- Each circumstance must point to guilt
- No other hypothesis except guilt should be possible
- (In civil suits, lower standard: preponderance of probabilities)
```

### 3. Bhagwati Prasad v. Chandramaul (SC 1966)
```
Facts: Leading questions in examination-in-chief
Held:
- Leading questions prohibited in examination-in-chief (Section 141)
- Exception: Undisputed facts, formal matters, hostile witness
- Court has discretion to allow/disallow
```

### 4. Ramesh Chandra Agrawal v. Regency Hospital (SC 2009)
```
Facts: Burden of proof in medical negligence case
Held:
- Plaintiff must prove: (1) Duty of care (2) Breach (3) Causation (4) Damage
- Burden on plaintiff to establish negligence
- If plaintiff proves prima facie case → Burden shifts to defendant to explain
```

## Common Mistakes

```
Mistake 1: Leading Questions in Examination-in-Chief
❌ "Did defendant sign the contract on 15/01/2024?" (Leading)
✓ "Who signed the contract and when?" (Non-leading)

Mistake 2: Not Objecting to Hearsay
❌ Allow witness to testify "My friend told me defendant said..."
✓ Object: "Hearsay, My Lord. Witness has no personal knowledge."

Mistake 3: Not Marking Documents as Exhibits
❌ Show document to witness but don't mark as exhibit
✓ After witness identifies document, request court to mark as Exhibit P-1/D-1

Mistake 4: Not Confronting Witness with Prior Inconsistent Statement
❌ Accept witness's changed testimony without challenge
✓ Produce prior statement under Section 145 and confront witness

Mistake 5: Failing to Lay Foundation for Expert Opinion
❌ Expert directly gives opinion without stating qualifications/examination
✓ First establish: Qualification → Examination conducted → Opinion

Mistake 6: Re-Examination Introducing New Matter Without Permission
❌ Introduce entirely new fact in re-examination
✓ Seek court permission if new matter; otherwise limit to cross-examination scope

Mistake 7: Not Applying for Hostile Witness Permission
❌ Continue examination-in-chief when witness clearly adverse
✓ Apply to court under Section 154 to treat as hostile → Cross-examine own witness
```

# Protocol: Execution of Decrees (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-CPC-EXECUTION  
**Domain**: Civil Procedure  
**Subdomain**: Post-Judgment  
**Version**: 1.0.0  
**Last Updated**: 2025-12-06

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on execution of decrees and orders under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908, covering procedures, modes of execution, objections, and remedies.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "How to execute a decree?"
- Decree holder wants to recover decree amount
- Need to attach property for decree satisfaction
- Judgment debtor not paying decree voluntarily
- Execution objections raised
- Cross-objections in execution

## Core Principles

### 1. Execution = Enforcement of Decree

```
Execution (Order XXI):
Process by which court enforces its decree
→ Judgment debtor compelled to satisfy decree
→ Coercive measures if voluntary compliance fails

Decree-Holder = Plaintiff (who obtained favorable decree)
Judgment-Debtor = Defendant (against whom decree passed)

Objective: Translate paper decree into actual relief (money/property/specific act)
```

### 2. Only Decrees Executable (Section 2(2) + Order XXI Rule 11)

```
Executable:
✓ Decrees (money, property, specific performance, injunction)
✓ FINAL decrees (not preliminary - unless expressly made executable)
✓ Orders expressly declared executable by CPC (e.g., Order XXXIX Rule 2A - contempt for injunction breach)

NOT Executable:
✗ Preliminary decrees (until final decree passed)
✗ Orders (generally - except those expressly executable)
✗ Declaratory decrees (no specific relief - only declaration of rights)
```

## WHEN EXECUTION LIES (Order XXI Rule 11)

### Conditions for Execution

```
Order XXI Rule 11: Execution on Application of Decree-Holder

Conditions:

1. DECREE must be FINAL (not preliminary)

2. Decree must have become FINAL:
   - No appeal filed within limitation (90 days expired), OR
   - Appeal filed and dismissed, OR
   - Appeal filed and withdrawn, OR
   - Appellate court modified decree (execute modified decree)

3. Decree must be for SPECIFIC RELIEF:
   - Money
   - Property (movable/immovable)
   - Specific act (specific performance, injunction)

4. Execution application filed within LIMITATION:
   - 12 years from date decree becomes final (Article 136 Limitation Act 1963)

5. Execution in PROPER COURT:
   - Court that passed decree (Rule 3), OR
   - Court to which decree transferred for execution (Rule 4-6)
```

### Courts Which May Execute Decree (Order XXI Rule 3-6)

```
Rule 3: Execution by Court Which Passed Decree (Primary)
Court that passed decree generally executes

Rule 4-6: Transfer for Execution (Secondary)

Transfer If:
(a) Judgment debtor resides outside jurisdiction of decree-passing court
(b) Judgment debtor's property situated outside jurisdiction
(c) Decree-holder requests transfer (for convenience)

Procedure:
1. Decree-passing court transfers decree to court where:
   - Judgment debtor resides, OR
   - Judgment debtor's property situated
2. Transferee court executes as if passed by itself
3. Return warrant after execution complete
```

## PROCEDURE FOR EXECUTION (Order XXI)

### Step 1: File Execution Application (Rule 11)

```
Rule 11: Execution Petition Format

Contents:
1. Decree-holder's name and description
2. Judgment debtor's name and description
3. Decree details (date, court, suit number, amount/relief)
4. Property/assets of judgment debtor to be attached (if known)
5. Mode of execution sought:
   - Attachment and sale of property
   - Arrest and detention
   - Delivery of property
   - Specific performance
6. Verification

Court Fees:
Ad valorem (on decree amount) or fixed (as per state rules)

Timeline:
Within 12 years from decree becoming final (Article 136 Limitation Act)
```

### Step 2: Notice to Judgment Debtor (Rule 22)

```
Rule 22: Notice of Execution

Court issues notice to judgment debtor:
- Show cause why execution should not proceed
- Opportunity to pay decree amount voluntarily
- Appear on specified date

Service:
- Personal service (primary)
- Substituted service (if personal impracticable)
- Proclamation + affixture (if whereabouts unknown)

Time:
- Usually 15-30 days from notice to appear
```

### Step 3: Judgment Debtor's Response

```
Options for Judgment Debtor:

1. PAY DECREE AMOUNT VOLUNTARILY:
   - Full satisfaction → Decree satisfied, execution closed
   - Partial payment → Execution for balance

2. FILE OBJECTION (Order XXI Rule 58, 101):
   - Challenge execution
   - Grounds: Decree satisfied, decree not executable, illegality

3. APPEAR AND REQUEST TIME:
   - Court may give installments (Order XXI Rule 1)
   - Conditional: Judgment debtor shows inability to pay in one go

4. NOT APPEAR / NOT PAY:
   - Court proceeds with execution (coercive measures)
```

### Step 4: Execution Proceedings (Modes of Execution)

```
Modes (depend on nature of decree):

A. Money Decree: Attachment and sale of property
B. Property Decree: Delivery of possession
C. Specific Performance: Compulsion to perform act
D. Injunction: Contempt for breach

Court selects mode based on:
- Nature of decree
- Availability of judgment debtor's assets
- Efficiency of recovery
```

## MODES OF EXECUTION - MONEY DECREES

### A. Attachment and Sale of Property (Rule 41-94)

```
Process:

1. ATTACHMENT (Rule 41-53):
   Court prohibits judgment debtor from transferring/dealing with property

   Procedure:
   - Court issues attachment order
   - Bailiff/process server serves attachment order on judgment debtor
   - Property attached (proclamation posted, if immovable)
   - Attachment prevents sale/transfer by judgment debtor

   Types of Property Attachable:
   ✓ Immovable property (land, buildings)
   ✓ Movable property (goods, vehicles, machinery)
   ✓ Debts owed to judgment debtor (attachment of debts - Rule 46)
   ✓ Shares, securities (Rule 48)
   ✓ Salary/pension (Rule 48 - subject to limits)

   Property EXEMPT from Attachment (Rule 60):
   ✗ Necessary wearing apparel, cooking vessels
   ✗ Tools of trade (up to Rs. 10,000 value)
   ✗ Books, instruments for profession (up to Rs. 10,000)
   ✗ Dwelling house + reasonable land (for agricultural family)
   ✗ Stipends, allowances
   ✗ Certain government pensions
   ✗ 2/3rd of salary (only 1/3rd attachable - Rule 48)

2. PROCLAMATION OF SALE (Rule 54-56):
   - Public notice of sale
   - 30 days minimum from proclamation to sale
   - Details: Property description, upset price, date/time/place of sale

3. SALE (Rule 66-94):
   - Public auction
   - Highest bidder
   - Sale confirmed by court
   - Sale certificate issued to purchaser
   - Judgment debtor dispossessed (if immovable property)

4. DISTRIBUTION OF SALE PROCEEDS (Rule 73):
   - Decree amount paid to decree-holder
   - Costs of execution paid
   - Surplus (if any) returned to judgment debtor

Time Limit for Sale:
- Movable property: Within 15 days of attachment (unless court extends)
- Immovable property: After 30 days from proclamation
```

### B. Arrest and Detention of Judgment Debtor (Rule 30-40)

```
Rule 30: Arrest and Detention for Non-Payment

Conditions:
1. Money decree
2. Judgment debtor has means to pay but refuses
3. Decree-holder applies for arrest
4. Court satisfied arrest necessary

NOT Allowed:
- Arrest of women (unless special circumstances)
- Arrest for civil debt if judgment debtor can show inability to pay

Procedure:
1. Warrant of arrest issued
2. Judgment debtor arrested and brought to court
3. Court examines: Can judgment debtor pay?
   - If YES but refuses → Detention ordered (up to 3 months)
   - If NO (genuinely unable) → Released, explore other modes

Detention Period:
- Maximum 3 months per warrant (Rule 31)
- Court may issue fresh warrant if decree still unsatisfied (but total detention limited)

Release:
- Payment of decree amount → Immediate release
- Inability to pay shown → Released
- Detention period expires → Released

Modern Practice:
- Arrest/detention RARELY used (last resort)
- Courts prefer attachment and sale of property
```

### C. Attachment of Salary/Pension (Rule 48)

```
Rule 48: Attachment of Salary

Limit:
- Maximum 1/3rd of salary attachable (2/3rd exempt)
- Ensures judgment debtor has minimum income for subsistence

Procedure:
1. Court issues order to judgment debtor's employer
2. Employer deducts 1/3rd of salary each month
3. Employer pays deducted amount to court
4. Court pays decree-holder
5. Continues till decree fully satisfied

Effect:
- Gradual satisfaction (installments)
- Judgment debtor continues working
- No immediate hardship

Similar: Pension attachment (with limits to protect minimum subsistence)
```

### D. Attachment of Debts (Rule 46)

```
Rule 46: Attachment of Debts Owed to Judgment Debtor

If third party owes money to judgment debtor:
→ Court attaches debt (prohibits third party from paying judgment debtor)

Procedure:
1. Decree-holder identifies third party debtor
2. Court issues order to third party (garnishee order)
3. Third party prohibited from paying judgment debtor
4. Third party pays court instead
5. Court pays decree-holder

Example:
Judgment debtor is landlord; tenant owes rent
→ Court attaches rent
→ Tenant pays rent to court (not to judgment debtor)
→ Court pays decree-holder

Third Party's Rights:
- Can dispute debt (claim debt not owed)
- Can show debt already paid
- Court decides (mini-trial on debt existence)
```

## MODES OF EXECUTION - PROPERTY DECREES

### Delivery of Immovable Property (Rule 95-96)

```
Decree for Possession of Immovable Property:

Procedure:
1. Decree-holder files execution application
2. Court issues order for delivery of possession
3. Bailiff/court officer delivers possession to decree-holder
4. If judgment debtor refuses to vacate:
   - Court officer removes judgment debtor's belongings
   - Hands over possession to decree-holder
5. Attachment if judgment debtor attempts re-entry (contempt)

Forcible Entry:
- Court officer may break open locks if necessary (with court permission)
- Police assistance sought if resistance expected

Mesne Profits (Rule 96):
- If decree includes mesne profits (rent for wrongful possession)
- Calculated by Commissioner OR court
- Recovered like money decree
```

### Delivery of Movable Property (Rule 97)

```
Decree for Specific Movable Property:

Procedure:
1. Court issues order for delivery
2. Bailiff seizes property from judgment debtor
3. Delivers to decree-holder
4. If judgment debtor hides/disposes property:
   - Court may order attachment of other property (value equivalent)
   - OR order payment of value + costs

Example:
Decree: "Defendant to return plaintiff's car (specific vehicle)"
Execution: Bailiff seizes car from defendant, delivers to plaintiff
If car sold/destroyed: Defendant pays market value of car
```

## MODES OF EXECUTION - SPECIFIC PERFORMANCE & INJUNCTION

### Specific Performance Decree (Rule 98-101)

```
Decree Directing Judgment Debtor to Perform Specific Act:

Example:
"Defendant to execute sale deed in favor of plaintiff within 30 days"

Procedure:
1. Decree-holder applies for execution
2. Court issues notice to judgment debtor
3. Options:
   (a) Judgment debtor performs act (executes sale deed)
       → Decree satisfied
   (b) Judgment debtor refuses to perform
       → Court appoints Commissioner/Registrar to execute on behalf of judgment debtor
       → Deemed as if judgment debtor executed (Section 34 Specific Relief Act)

Effect of Commissioner's Execution:
- Binding on judgment debtor
- Conveyance effective as if judgment debtor signed

Alternative:
- If performance impossible OR judgment debtor deliberately defeated
  → Decree-holder may apply for compensation in lieu
  → Court orders judgment debtor to pay value (like money decree)
```

### Injunction Decree (Order XXXIX Rule 2A, Section 94)

```
Decree Restraining Judgment Debtor from Doing Act:

Example:
"Defendant perpetually restrained from constructing on plaintiff's land"

Execution/Enforcement:
1. Injunction decree issued
2. If judgment debtor violates (does prohibited act):
   → Decree-holder files contempt application
   → Section 94 CPC / Order XXXIX Rule 2A: Contempt of court
   → Court may:
     (a) Fine judgment debtor
     (b) Imprison judgment debtor (up to 6 months)
     (c) Attach and sell judgment debtor's property

Contempt Procedure:
- Show cause notice to judgment debtor
- Judgment debtor explains violation (or denies)
- If violation proved → Contempt punishment

Prevention:
- If imminent violation → Decree-holder seeks attachment of property as preventive measure
```

## OBJECTIONS TO EXECUTION (Order XXI Rule 58, 101)

### Types of Objections

```
Rule 58: Objections to Execution BY JUDGMENT DEBTOR

Grounds:
1. Decree satisfied (payment already made)
2. Decree executed (in full or part) already
3. Decree stayed by appellate court (appeal pending + stay granted)
4. Decree set aside/modified
5. Decree not executable (preliminary decree, declaratory decree)
6. Decree-holder not entitled to execute (not party to decree)
7. Property sought to be attached exempt (Rule 60)
8. Execution barred by limitation (12 years expired)

Procedure:
- Judgment debtor files objection application
- Affidavit stating grounds
- Court hears parties
- Decides objection

Effect:
- If objection sustained → Execution dismissed/stayed
- If objection rejected → Execution proceeds
```

### Rule 101: Objections to Execution BY THIRD PARTY

```
Third Party = Person not party to decree, claiming interest in property attached

Example:
Decree-holder attaches property; third party claims "This is MY property, not 
judgment debtor's"

Grounds:
1. Property belongs to third party (not judgment debtor)
2. Third party has lien/charge on property
3. Third party is tenant/occupant with rights
4. Property jointly owned (third party co-owner)

Procedure:
1. Third party files objection (within prescribed time - usually 30 days from attachment)
2. Affidavit + documents proving ownership/interest
3. Court hears third party and decree-holder
4. Decides:
   (a) Third party objection valid → Attachment released (as to third party's share)
   (b) Third party objection invalid → Attachment continues, sale proceeds

Separate Suit:
If third party's claim involves detailed investigation:
→ Court may direct third party to file separate suit
→ Execution stayed/continued pending suit
```

### Cross-Objections

```
If judgment debtor or third party files objection AND decree-holder disputes:
→ Decree-holder files CROSS-OBJECTION
→ Supports execution, disputes objection grounds

Example:
Judgment Debtor's Objection: "Decree satisfied by payment on DD/MM/YYYY"
Decree-Holder's Cross-Objection: "No payment received; payment claim false"

Court decides objection + cross-objection together
```

## SATISFACTION OF DECREE (Rule 2)

### Decree Satisfaction

```
Rule 2: Satisfaction Entry

Decree Satisfied When:
1. Decree amount paid in full (voluntary OR through execution)
2. Property delivered (in property decree)
3. Specific act performed (in specific performance decree)
4. Parties compromise post-decree (satisfaction agreement)

Procedure:
1. Judgment debtor pays decree amount to court OR decree-holder
2. Decree-holder acknowledges satisfaction (filing application in court)
3. Court makes SATISFACTION ENTRY in decree register
4. Decree marked as "Satisfied"

Effect:
- Decree cannot be executed again
- Judgment debtor discharged from liability
- Execution proceedings closed

Partial Satisfaction:
- Payment of part of decree amount
- Court notes partial satisfaction
- Execution for balance continues

Certificate of Satisfaction:
- On request, court issues certificate stating decree satisfied
- Useful for judgment debtor (proof of satisfaction)
```

## PIECEMEAL EXECUTION

```
Piecemeal Execution = Executing decree in parts (multiple execution applications)

Permitted:
✓ Decree for multiple reliefs (can execute each separately)
✓ Decree for money + interest (can execute principal, then interest)
✓ Decree for multiple properties (can attach one, then another)

NOT Permitted:
✗ Unnecessarily splitting decree to harass judgment debtor
✗ Multiple executions for same relief (all at once preferred)

Example:
Decree: "Defendant to pay Rs. 10 lakhs + interest @ 9% p.a."
Execution 1: Attach property worth Rs. 5 lakhs, sell, recover Rs. 5 lakhs
Execution 2 (if needed): Attach another property, recover balance Rs. 5 lakhs + interest

Court discourages piecemeal if decree-holder can execute in one go
```

## LIMITATION FOR EXECUTION (Article 136 Limitation Act 1963)

```
Article 136: 12 YEARS from date decree becomes final

"Final" means:
- No appeal filed within limitation (90 days expired), OR
- Appeal decided

Calculation:
Decree date: 01/01/2020
No appeal filed (90 days expired): 01/04/2020
Execution limitation: 01/04/2020 + 12 years = 01/04/2032

If Appeal:
Decree date: 01/01/2020
Appeal filed: 15/02/2020
Appeal dismissed: 01/05/2021
Execution limitation: 01/05/2021 + 12 years = 01/05/2033

Extension:
- Limitation for execution can be extended (Section 5 Limitation Act) if:
  * Execution stayed by court order (period of stay excluded)
  * Bona fide mistake in execution
- BUT generally strict (12 years is long period; courts expect diligence)

Fresh Period (Article 136 Explanation):
Each execution application starts FRESH 12-year period
- First execution: 01/04/2020 to 01/04/2032
- If first execution dismissed (procedural defect): Fresh execution application within 12 years from dismissal
```

## COMMON SCENARIOS

### Scenario 1: Attachment and Sale of Immovable Property

```
Facts:
- Money decree for Rs. 50 lakhs
- Judgment debtor owns house (market value Rs. 70 lakhs)

Execution:
1. Decree-holder files execution application (attachment of house)
2. Court issues attachment order
3. Proclamation of sale (30 days' notice)
4. Sale date fixed
5. Auction held; highest bidder Rs. 68 lakhs
6. Sale confirmed by court
7. Sale certificate issued to purchaser
8. Sale proceeds distributed:
   - Decree amount: Rs. 50 lakhs to decree-holder
   - Execution costs: Rs. 3 lakhs
   - Surplus: Rs. 15 lakhs to judgment debtor
9. Purchaser gets possession

Effect:
- Decree satisfied
- Judgment debtor loses house but gets surplus
```

### Scenario 2: Third Party Objection

```
Facts:
- Decree-holder attaches property (land)
- Third party claims land belongs to him (not judgment debtor)

Objection:
1. Third party files objection under Order XXI Rule 101
2. Produces sale deed showing he purchased land from judgment debtor 2 years ago
3. Decree-holder disputes: "Sale was fraudulent to defeat decree"

Court Hearing:
- Third party proves bona fide purchase (paid value, registered sale deed)
- Decree-holder fails to prove fraud

Court Order:
- Third party objection sustained
- Attachment of land released
- Decree-holder must find other property of judgment debtor

Alternative (if fraud proved):
- Sale deemed fraudulent (to defeat creditors)
- Attachment continues
- Land sold, third party's "purchase" set aside
```

### Scenario 3: Decree Satisfied by Payment

```
Facts:
- Decree for Rs. 10 lakhs
- Judgment debtor pays Rs. 10 lakhs to decree-holder outside court

Satisfaction:
1. Decree-holder files application stating decree satisfied
2. Affidavit: Received Rs. 10 lakhs on DD/MM/YYYY
3. Court makes satisfaction entry
4. Decree marked "Satisfied"

Later:
- If judgment debtor wants proof: Apply for certificate of satisfaction
- Court issues certificate (useful if decree-holder later denies payment)

If Decree-Holder Denies Payment:
- Judgment debtor shows evidence (receipt, bank transfer, decree-holder's acknowledgment)
- Court decides (may direct decree-holder to file affidavit)
```

### Scenario 4: Execution of Specific Performance Decree

```
Facts:
- Decree: "Defendant to execute sale deed for property in favor of plaintiff within 30 days"
- Defendant refuses to execute

Execution:
1. Plaintiff files execution application
2. Court notices defendant
3. Defendant refuses
4. Court appoints Commissioner (or Registrar) to execute sale deed on behalf of defendant
5. Commissioner executes sale deed
6. Sale deed registered
7. Plaintiff becomes owner

Effect (Section 34 Specific Relief Act):
- Commissioner's execution deemed as defendant's execution
- Binding on defendant
- Property transferred to plaintiff

Alternative (if defendant destroyed property/made performance impossible):
- Plaintiff applies for compensation in lieu
- Court orders defendant to pay market value of property + costs
```

## CASE LAW

### 1. Sardar Govindrao Mahadik v. Devi Sahai (SC 1982)
```
Facts: Property exempt from attachment under Order XXI Rule 60
Held:
- Dwelling house of agricultural family exempt (reasonable area)
- Purpose: Protect judgment debtor's basic subsistence
- Decree-holder must attach other property (if available)
```

### 2. Manoharlal v. Shantabai (SC 1969)
```
Facts: Execution barred by limitation (12 years expired)
Held:
- Article 136 Limitation Act: 12 years from decree becoming final
- Decree becomes final when appeal time expires OR appeal decided
- After 12 years → Execution barred (decree unenforceable)
```

### 3. Rameshwar Dayal v. Suit Property (SC 1977)
```
Facts: Third party objection to execution; property claimed by third party
Held:
- Third party (not party to decree) can object under Order XXI Rule 101
- Burden on third party to prove ownership/interest in property
- If third party proves → Attachment released (as to third party's share)
- If third party fails → Attachment continues
```

### 4. Popat v. Shiv Kumar (SC 2007)
```
Facts: Piecemeal execution; decree-holder filing multiple execution applications
Held:
- Piecemeal execution permitted (if bona fide - decree cannot be executed in one go)
- BUT court discourages if appears to harass judgment debtor
- Each execution application starts fresh limitation period (Article 136)
```

# Protocol: Judgments and Decrees (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-CPC-JUDGMENTS-DECREES  
**Domain**: Civil Procedure  
**Subdomain**: Trial and Judgment  
**Version**: 1.0.0  
**Last Updated**: 2025-12-06

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on civil judgments and decrees under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908, including types, requirements, rectification, and enforcement distinctions.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "What is difference between judgment and decree?"
- Need to draft/understand judgment
- Decree preparation required
- Rectification of clerical errors needed
- Judgment interpretation questions
- Execution vs appeal timeline queries

## Core Principles

### 1. Judgment vs Decree Distinction

```
JUDGMENT (Section 2(9)):
"The statement given by the Judge on the grounds of a decree or order"

= Reasons, findings, analysis, decision-making process
= WHY court decided the way it did
= Narrative form (discussion, reasoning)

DECREE (Section 2(2)):
"The formal expression of an adjudication which, so far as regards the Court 
expressing it, conclusively determines the rights of the parties with regard 
to all or any of the matters in controversy in the suit"

= WHAT court decided
= Operative part (relief granted/denied)
= Executable (can be enforced through execution proceedings)

Relationship:
Judgment → Process → Decree
(Reasoning) → (Court decides) → (Formal order)

Example:
Judgment: 50-page document explaining why plaintiff proved case
Decree: "Defendant shall pay Rs. 10 lakhs to plaintiff"
```

### 2. Decree Characteristics (Section 2(2))

```
For an order to be "DECREE", it must:

1. Be formal expression of adjudication
2. Conclusively determine rights of parties
3. Relate to matters in controversy in the SUIT
4. Be by court

NOT Decrees:
- Orders (interim/interlocutory)
- Orders in execution proceedings
- Orders in appeals (unless finally disposing appeal - then becomes "decree")
```

## JUDGMENT REQUIREMENTS (Order XX)

### Mandatory Contents (Order XX Rule 4)

```
Rule 4: Judgment shall contain:

1. CONCISE STATEMENT OF CASE

Brief summary:
- Parties (plaintiff, defendant)
- Nature of claim (money, property, injunction, etc.)
- Nature of defense (denial, affirmative defense)

2. POINTS FOR DETERMINATION

List of issues framed:
- Issue No. 1: [Question]
- Issue No. 2: [Question]
- ...

3. DECISION ON EACH ISSUE WITH REASONS

For each issue:
- Finding (affirmative/negative)
- Reasons (analysis of evidence, law, precedents)

Example:
"Issue No. 1: Whether plaintiff proves that defendant owes Rs. 10 lakhs?

EVIDENCE: Plaintiff examined 2 witnesses... Defendant examined 1 witness...
ANALYSIS: Contract dated 15/01/2024 (Exhibit P-1) is admitted. Delivery receipts 
(Exhibit P-2) prove supply. Defendant claims payment by cheque, but cheque 
(Exhibit D-1) is for different transaction as per bank statement...
FINDING: Issue No. 1 is answered in the AFFIRMATIVE. Plaintiff has proved 
defendant owes Rs. 10 lakhs."

4. FINAL ORDER

Conclusion:
- Suit decreed/dismissed
- Relief granted/denied
- Costs

5. SIGNATURE AND DATE

- Judge's signature
- Date of pronouncement

OPTIONAL (but recommended):
- Summary of pleadings
- Summary of evidence (witness-wise)
- Discussion of legal provisions and case law
```

### Judgment Types

#### A. Final Judgment

```
Judgment that finally disposes suit
→ Nothing remains except execution (or appeal)

Example:
Judgment in money suit: "Suit decreed. Defendant to pay Rs. 10 lakhs to plaintiff."
→ Final (nothing more to adjudicate)
```

#### B. Judgment Declaring Rights (Preliminary)

```
Judgment that declares rights but does not finally dispose suit
→ Requires further proceedings (final decree application)

Example:
Partition suit judgment: "Plaintiff and defendant each entitled to 1/2 share"
→ Preliminary (actual partition/division still to be done)
→ After partition, final decree passed
```

### Judgment Pronouncement (Order XX Rule 1-3)

```
Rule 1: Judgment pronounced in OPEN COURT

Two Modes:

1. IMMEDIATE PRONOUNCEMENT:
   - After final arguments
   - Judge delivers judgment orally
   - Typed/written transcript prepared later (within few days)

2. RESERVED JUDGMENT:
   - Judge reserves judgment for detailed consideration
   - Date of pronouncement fixed (parties informed)
   - On fixed date, judgment read/handed down in court

Presence of Parties:
- Not mandatory (judgment pronounced whether parties present or not)
- Parties/advocates informed of date
- Copy given to parties on application

Operative Date:
- Judgment takes effect from DATE OF PRONOUNCEMENT
- Limitation for appeal runs from pronouncement date (not date judgment signed/typed)
```

### Judgment Correction (Order XX Rule 6, Section 152 CPC)

```
Section 152: Clerical or Arithmetical Errors Amendable

Court may amend:
- Clerical errors (typing mistakes, name misspellings)
- Arithmetical errors (calculation mistakes in decree amount)
- Errors arising from accidental slip or omission

Procedure:
1. Application by party OR court suo motu
2. Notice to opposite party
3. Hearing (if objection)
4. Order correcting error

Examples of Amendable Errors:

✓ Plaintiff's name typed as "Ramesh Gupta" instead of "Ramesh Kumar" (clerical)
✓ Decree amount calculated as Rs. 9 lakhs instead of Rs. 10 lakhs (interest miscalculation) (arithmetical)
✓ Judgment says "suit decreed" but decree mistakenly drawn as "suit dismissed" (clerical)

NOT Amendable (Substantive Errors):

✗ Changing finding on issue (from affirmative to negative)
✗ Granting different relief than awarded in judgment
✗ Altering reasoning or ratio decidendi

Remedy for Substantive Errors:
- Review (Order 47) - if error apparent on face of record
- Appeal (Section 96-100)
```

## DECREE REQUIREMENTS (Order XX Rule 6-7)

### Decree Contents (Order XX Rule 6)

```
Decree shall contain:

1. NUMBER OF SUIT
2. NAMES OF PARTIES (full description as in plaint)
3. PARTICULARS OF CLAIM (brief)
4. RELIEF GRANTED OR REFUSED (clear and specific)
5. COSTS (amount or direction for assessment)
6. DATE (of pronouncement)
7. SIGNATURE (of judge)
8. SEAL (of court)

Clarity Requirement:
Decree must be CLEAR and SPECIFIC
→ Can be executed without reference to judgment
→ No ambiguity in what defendant must do

Example of CLEAR Decree:
"The Defendant shall pay to the Plaintiff:
(a) Rs. 10,00,000/- (Principal)
(b) Interest @ 9% per annum on Rs. 10,00,000/- from 01/03/2023 till realization
(c) Costs of suit assessed at Rs. 50,000/-"

Example of AMBIGUOUS Decree (DEFECTIVE):
"The Defendant shall pay reasonable amount to Plaintiff"
→ What is "reasonable"? Decree not executable.
```

### Decree Preparation Timeline (Order XX Rule 7)

```
Rule 7: Decree drawn up within 15 DAYS of pronouncement of judgment 
(if practicable)

Procedure:
1. Judgment pronounced
2. Court staff prepares decree draft (based on judgment)
3. Judge signs decree
4. Seal affixed
5. Decree filed in court records

Certified Copy:
- Parties apply for certified copy of decree
- Required for execution or appeal
- Fee payable (as per court fee rules)
```

## TYPES OF DECREES

### 1. Preliminary Decree (Order XX Rule 2(1))

```
Definition (Section 2(2)): Decree passed in suit but does not completely dispose 
the suit, requiring further proceedings to be taken

Characteristics:
- Declares rights
- But does not finally conclude suit
- Further application/proceedings needed

When Passed:

A. Partition Suits:
   Preliminary Decree: Declares shares of co-owners
   Further Proceedings: Actual partition (division of property)
   Final Decree: After partition complete, allocating specific portions

B. Suits for Account:
   Preliminary Decree: Directs defendant to file account
   Further Proceedings: Account filed, objections, Commissioner examines
   Final Decree: After account settled, for balance amount due

C. Redemption of Mortgage:
   Preliminary Decree: Right to redeem established, amount to be paid determined
   Further Proceedings: Payment deadline given
   Final Decree: After payment, property conveyed to plaintiff OR if no payment, 
                 foreclosed

D. Specific Performance with Alternatives:
   Preliminary Decree: Directs defendant to perform within time
   Further Proceedings: If defendant performs → Suit disposed
   Final Decree: If defendant fails to perform → Decree for alternative relief 
                 (compensation)

Effect:
- Preliminary decree ALONE not executable
- Only FINAL decree executable
- Appeal lies from preliminary decree (but court may wait for final decree)
```

### 2. Final Decree (Order XX Rule 2(2))

```
Definition: Decree that finally disposes suit (nothing remains except execution)

Characteristics:
- Suit completely disposed
- All issues decided
- Relief granted/denied
- Executable

Examples:
- Money decree in loan recovery suit
- Injunction decree
- Declaration decree
- Final decree in partition (after preliminary decree and partition complete)

Effect:
- Executable (can be enforced through execution proceedings)
- Appeal lies (within limitation)
```

### 3. Partly Preliminary, Partly Final Decree (Order XX Rule 2(3))

```
Decree that is:
- Final as to some reliefs
- Preliminary as to other reliefs

Example:
Suit for possession + mesne profits (rent for wrongful possession)

Decree:
- Possession granted → FINAL (executable immediately)
- Mesne profits to be ascertained → PRELIMINARY (Commissioner appointed to calculate)
- After calculation, further decree for mesne profits amount → FINAL

Effect:
- Final part executable immediately
- Preliminary part requires further proceedings
```

### 4. Consent Decree (Order XXIII Rule 3)

```
Decree based on compromise/settlement between parties

Procedure:
1. Parties reach settlement
2. Joint application/memo of compromise filed
3. Court examines:
   - Whether parties understand terms
   - Whether compromise lawful (not opposed to public policy, not fraud on statute)
   - Whether parties have capacity to compromise
4. Court records compromise
5. Decree passed as per compromise terms

Characteristics:
- Binding on parties (contract + court order)
- Executable like regular decree
- Appeal limited:
  * Can appeal if compromise vitiated by fraud/coercion/misrepresentation/undue influence
  * CANNOT appeal challenging compromise terms themselves (parties voluntarily agreed)

Advantages:
- Quick disposal
- No lengthy trial
- Parties control outcome
- Costs saved

Disadvantages (for third parties):
- If fraud on creditors/statute → Third parties can challenge
- Example: Debtor and creditor collude in consent decree to defeat other creditors
```

### 5. Ex Parte Decree (Order IX Rule 6)

```
Decree passed when defendant does not appear despite service

Conditions:
1. Summons duly served on defendant
2. Defendant fails to appear on hearing date
3. Plaintiff proves claim (evidence)
4. Court satisfied plaintiff entitled to relief

Procedure:
1. Defendant absent on hearing date
2. Plaintiff produces proof of service (affidavit + acknowledgment)
3. Plaintiff examines witnesses and proves case
4. Court passes ex parte decree

Setting Aside (Order IX Rule 13):
Defendant can apply within 30 days from knowledge of decree:
Grounds:
- Summons not duly served, OR
- Prevented by sufficient cause from appearing

If set aside:
- Suit restored
- Defendant files written statement
- Trial proceeds from that stage
```

### 6. Conditional Decree

```
Decree granting relief subject to condition

Examples:

A. Money Decree with Time to Pay:
"Defendant shall pay Rs. 10 lakhs to plaintiff within 6 months. In default, 
decree executable immediately."

B. Specific Performance with Option:
"Defendant shall execute sale deed within 3 months. If fails, plaintiff 
entitled to Rs. 5 lakhs compensation in lieu."

C. Injunction with Undertaking:
"Defendant restrained from construction, provided plaintiff deposits Rs. 1 lakh 
as security (to compensate defendant if ultimately suit dismissed on appeal)."

Effect:
- Defendant gets opportunity to comply
- If complies → Suit disposed
- If fails → Decree executable for default consequence
```

## JUDGMENT INTERPRETATION

### Ratio Decidendi vs Obiter Dicta

```
RATIO DECIDENDI:
- Reason for decision
- Legal principle on which decision based
- BINDING as precedent (on lower courts)

Example:
Issue: Whether suit barred by limitation?
Finding: Suit filed within 3 years from cause of action (01/01/2020 to 01/01/2023)
Ratio: Suit within limitation under Article 113 Limitation Act
→ This ratio binds lower courts in similar cases

OBITER DICTA:
- Remarks made in passing
- Not necessary for decision
- NOT binding (only persuasive)

Example:
Judge remarks: "Even if suit were barred by limitation, Section 5 condonation 
would apply as delay was only 10 days due to plaintiff's illness"
→ Obiter (hypothetical observation, not necessary for actual decision)
→ Not binding precedent
```

### Operative Part (Decretal Portion)

```
Operative Part of Judgment:
- Conclusion section
- "IT IS ORDERED AND DECREED..."
- What court directs parties to do

This is what gets executed
- Not reasoning or analysis
- Only operative order

Example:
Judgment has 40 pages of reasoning
Operative Part (last paragraph):
"Suit decreed. Defendant to pay Rs. 10 lakhs + interest + costs to plaintiff."

Execution based on: Operative part (decree)
Appeal challenging: Reasoning (if erroneous) leading to operative part
```

## DECREE EXECUTION IMPLICATIONS

### When Decree Executable (Order XXI)

```
Conditions for Execution:

1. Decree must be FINAL (not preliminary)
2. Decree must have become FINAL:
   - No appeal filed within limitation (90 days), OR
   - Appeal filed and dismissed, OR
   - Appeal filed and withdrawn
3. Decree must be for specific relief (money/property/specific act)

NOT Executable:
- Preliminary decree (until final decree passed)
- Decree pending appeal (unless court grants stay)
- Declaratory decree (no specific relief - only declaration of rights)

Example:
Money Decree:
"Defendant to pay Rs. 10 lakhs to plaintiff"
→ Executable (specific relief - money)

Declaratory Decree:
"Plaintiff declared owner of property"
→ NOT executable (only declaration, no specific relief like possession)
→ Plaintiff can file fresh suit for possession based on declaration
```

### Decree Satisfaction

```
Decree Satisfied:
- Judgment debtor performs decree
- Pays money/delivers property/performs act

Certificate of Satisfaction:
- Decree-holder (plaintiff) files application
- States decree fully satisfied
- Court issues certificate
- Decree cannot be executed again

Partial Satisfaction:
- Decree-holder receives part payment
- Files statement of adjustment
- Decree executable for balance amount only
```

## COMMON SCENARIOS

### Scenario 1: Preliminary vs Final Decree (Partition)

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff and defendant co-own ancestral property
- Plaintiff sues for partition

Judgment:
- Court holds plaintiff entitled to 1/2 share, defendant 1/2 share

Preliminary Decree:
"It is declared that plaintiff and defendant are each entitled to 1/2 share 
in the suit property. Matter referred to Commissioner to effect partition."

Further Proceedings:
- Commissioner surveys property
- Prepares partition plan
- Divides into two equal portions (Portion A, Portion B)
- Files report

Final Decree (after partition):
"Portion A (as per Commissioner's report) allotted to plaintiff. Portion B 
allotted to defendant. Plaintiff and defendant shall execute mutual deeds 
conveying respective portions."

Effect:
- Only final decree executable (for possession if required)
- Preliminary decree not executable (only declares shares)
```

### Scenario 2: Consent Decree

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff sues defendant for Rs. 10 lakhs
- During trial, parties reach settlement: Defendant pays Rs. 7 lakhs

Compromise Application:
Joint application filed:
"The parties have amicably settled the dispute. Defendant agrees to pay 
Rs. 7,00,000/- to plaintiff in full and final settlement. Plaintiff waives 
remaining Rs. 3,00,000/-."

Court Examination:
- Parties appear before court
- Court confirms understanding of terms
- Court records compromise

Consent Decree:
"Suit decreed on the basis of compromise between parties. Defendant shall 
pay Rs. 7,00,000/- to plaintiff within 30 days. In default, decree executable. 
No order as to costs."

Effect:
- Binding contract + court order
- Executable if defendant defaults
- Limited grounds for appeal (fraud/coercion, not challenging terms themselves)
```

### Scenario 3: Correction under Section 152

```
Judgment:
"Suit decreed for Rs. 10,00,000/- (principal) + interest @ 9% per annum from 
01/03/2023 till realization."

Decree (Typed by Court Staff):
"Defendant shall pay Rs. 9,00,000/- + interest @ 9% per annum..."

Error: Rs. 9 lakhs instead of Rs. 10 lakhs (arithmetical/clerical error)

Application under Section 152:
- Plaintiff applies to correct error
- Shows judgment clearly states Rs. 10 lakhs
- Decree incorrectly typed as Rs. 9 lakhs

Court Order:
"Error in decree corrected. Rs. 9,00,000/- substituted with Rs. 10,00,000/-."

Effect:
- Decree amended to match judgment
- No appeal needed (mere correction of clerical error)
```

### Scenario 4: Ex Parte Decree Setting Aside

```
Facts:
- Suit filed; summons served on defendant
- Defendant does not appear
- Ex parte decree passed for Rs. 10 lakhs

Defendant's Application (Order IX Rule 13):
Filed within 30 days of knowledge of decree
Grounds:
- Summons not properly served (affixed on door, but defendant was out of station)
- Defendant has valid defense on merits (payment made)

Court Hearing:
- Defendant shows summons not properly served (service defective)
- OR shows prevented by sufficient cause (illness with medical certificate)
- Defendant shows prima facie defense (not frivolous)

Court Order:
"Ex parte decree set aside. Defendant shall file written statement within 
30 days. Suit restored to board. Costs of Rs. 10,000/- imposed on defendant 
(to compensate plaintiff for wasted time)."

Effect:
- Decree vacated
- Suit restored from point where defendant should have appeared
- Trial proceeds with defendant's participation
```

## CASE LAW

### 1. Madan Mohan Pathak v. Chander Bhan Kailashpati (SC 1992)
```
Facts: Distinction between judgment and decree
Held:
- Judgment = Reasons (process of deciding)
- Decree = Formal order (what is decided)
- Decree must follow judgment
- Appeal lies from decree, not judgment alone
```

### 2. Radhey Shyam v. Chhabi Nath (SC 2015)
```
Facts: Correction under Section 152; scope limited to clerical errors
Held:
- Section 152 allows correction of clerical/arithmetical errors only
- NOT for substantive errors (changing findings, reasoning)
- Substantive errors: Review (Order 47) or Appeal
```

### 3. Tek Bahadur Bhujel v. Debi Singh Bhujel (SC 2005)
```
Facts: Consent decree challenged on ground of fraud
Held:
- Consent decree is contract + court order
- Appeal limited: Can challenge if fraud/coercion/misrepresentation/undue influence
- Cannot appeal challenging terms of compromise themselves (parties agreed voluntarily)
- Burden on appellant to prove fraud/coercion
```

### 4. Ram Charan Das v. Girja Nandini Devi (SC 1966)
```
Facts: Preliminary decree in partition; whether executable
Held:
- Preliminary decree NOT executable (Order XX Rule 2)
- Only declares rights, does not finally dispose suit
- Execution only after final decree (after actual partition)
```

### 5. Ajit Singh v. Gopal Singh (SC 1993)
```
Facts: Judgment without reasons for findings on issues
Held:
- Order XX Rule 4: Judgment MUST contain reasons for decision on each issue
- Judgment without reasons violates CPC
- Such judgment liable to be set aside
- Matter remanded for fresh judgment with proper reasons
```

### 6. K.K. Modi v. K.N. Modi (SC 1998)
```
Facts: Decree ambiguous; not clear what defendant must do
Held:
- Decree must be clear and specific (Order XX Rule 6)
- Ambiguous decree not executable
- Decree must be executable without referring to judgment
- Vague decree liable to be struck down
```

# Protocol: Jurisdiction of Civil Courts (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-CPC-JURISDICTION  
**Domain**: Civil Procedure  
**Subdomain**: Jurisdiction and Court Hierarchy  
**Version**: 1.0.0  
**Last Updated**: 2025-12-06

## Purpose

This protocol provides systematic guidance on determining which civil court has jurisdiction to entertain a suit, covering pecuniary, territorial, and subject matter jurisdiction under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 (CPC).

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "Which court has jurisdiction to file my suit?"
- Need to determine appropriate court for civil litigation
- Jurisdiction challenge or objection raised
- Transfer of case requested
- Multiple defendants in different jurisdictions
- Property located in different jurisdictions
- Cause of action arose in multiple places

## Core Principles

### 1. Three Types of Jurisdiction (All Must Be Satisfied)

```
For a court to entertain a suit, it must have:

1. PECUNIARY Jurisdiction (value-based)
   → Can this court try suits of this value?

2. TERRITORIAL Jurisdiction (geography-based)
   → Does this court have jurisdiction over the area where defendant/property/cause of action is?

3. SUBJECT MATTER Jurisdiction (topic-based)
   → Is this court competent to try this type of case?

Rule: ALL THREE must be satisfied. If even one lacking → Court CANNOT try suit.
```

### 2. Fundamental Rule - Section 15

```
Section 15: Every suit shall be instituted in the court of the lowest grade 
competent to try it.

Meaning:
- Start at LOWEST court that has all three jurisdictions
- Cannot directly file in High Court if District Court competent
- Prevents forum shopping to higher courts

Example:
Suit value: Rs. 5 lakhs
District Court: Can try suits up to Rs. 3 crores
High Court: Original jurisdiction unlimited

→ File in District Court (lowest competent court)
✗ Cannot directly file in High Court (though it has jurisdiction)
```

## Jurisdiction Framework

### I. PECUNIARY JURISDICTION (Value-Based)

**Rule**: Court can only try suits within its monetary limits set by State Government.

#### Court Hierarchy and Pecuniary Limits:

```
Supreme Court:
- Article 131-132: Special original jurisdiction
- Appeals: No monetary limit

High Court (Original Side):
- Varies by state notification
- Example (Bombay): Rs. 2 lakhs and above
- Some High Courts: No original civil jurisdiction (only appellate)

District Court:
- Varies by state
- Typical: Up to Rs. 3 crores
- No upper limit if state notification says so

Subordinate Courts:
Civil Judge (Senior Division): Rs. 5 lakhs to Rs. 50 lakhs (varies by state)
Civil Judge (Junior Division): Up to Rs. 5 lakhs (varies by state)

Small Causes Court (Section 15 Provincial Small Cause Courts Act 1887):
- Urban areas only
- Typically: Up to Rs. 20,000-50,000
- Speedy procedure, no appeal on facts
```

#### Determination of "Value of Suit" (Sections 7-11 Court Fees Act 1870):

```
**Immovable Property**:
Market value at suit institution

**Movable Property**:
Market value at suit institution

**Debt/Money Claim**:
Principal amount + interest due (not future interest)

**Declaration of Title**:
Market value of property

**Injunction**:
Value of property affected

**Specific Performance**:
Amount of consideration

**Partition**:
Share of plaintiff (not whole property)

**Multiple Reliefs**:
Value of relief which gives jurisdiction (not aggregate)

Example:
Suit for Rs. 10 lakhs + specific performance of land worth Rs. 50 lakhs
→ Value = Rs. 50 lakhs (higher relief)
```

### II. TERRITORIAL JURISDICTION (Section 16-20)

**General Rule - Section 20**: 

```
"Every suit shall be instituted in a court within the local limits of whose 
jurisdiction:
(a) The defendant, or each of defendants where more than one, at time of 
    commencement of suit, actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on 
    business, or personally works for gain; OR
(b) Any defendants, where more than one, at time of commencement of suit 
    actually and voluntarily resides, or carries on business, or personally 
    works for gain, PROVIDED leave of court obtained; OR
(c) Cause of action, wholly or in part, arises."
```

#### A. Defendant's Residence/Business (Section 20(a))

**Single Defendant**:
```
File where defendant:
- Actually resides (not mere temporary stay)
- Voluntarily resides (not under arrest/detention)
- Carries on business (has office/regular business activity)
- Personally works for gain (employment/profession)

Example:
Defendant company: Registered office in Mumbai, branch in Delhi
→ Plaintiff can sue in Mumbai (registered office) OR Delhi (branch - carries on business)
```

**Multiple Defendants** (Section 20(a)):
```
If suing multiple defendants:
- Court must have jurisdiction over ALL defendants (conjunctive), OR
- Court must have jurisdiction over at least ONE defendant + get leave (Section 20(b))

Example:
Defendant 1: Resides in Mumbai
Defendant 2: Resides in Delhi
Defendant 3: Resides in Kolkata

Option 1: Find common jurisdiction (where all carry on business - unlikely)
Option 2: Sue in Mumbai (D1's residence) + get leave to add D2 and D3 (Section 20(b))
```

#### B. Cause of Action (Section 20(c))

**Definition**: 
```
"Cause of action" = Bundle of facts which give plaintiff right to sue

Must include:
1. Facts showing plaintiff's right
2. Facts showing defendant's infringement of that right
3. Facts showing consequent damage

Accrues when: All facts necessary for suit come into existence
```

**"Wholly or in Part" Arises**:
```
If cause of action arises in multiple places → Plaintiff can choose ANY place 
where any part of cause of action arose

Example (Debt Recovery):
Contract signed: Mumbai
Goods delivered: Delhi
Payment due: Bangalore
Default occurred: Bangalore

Cause of action arose in: Mumbai (contract) + Delhi (delivery) + Bangalore (default)
→ Plaintiff can file in ANY of these three places
```

**Continuing Cause of Action**:
```
If wrong is continuous (e.g., nuisance, defamation on website):
→ Cause of action arises wherever the wrong continues
→ Plaintiff can sue in any jurisdiction where wrong persists

Example:
Defamatory content on website accessible nationwide
→ Cause of action arises wherever website accessed
→ Plaintiff can sue in ANY jurisdiction in India (BUT recent SC judgments limiting this)
```

#### C. Special Territorial Jurisdiction Rules

**1. Immovable Property (Section 16)**:
```
Section 16: Suits for recovery of immovable property, partition, foreclosure, 
sale, rent, profits, etc. → MUST be filed where property is situated

Exception: No exception. Mandatory jurisdiction.

Example:
Land located: Mumbai
Plaintiff resides: Delhi
Defendant resides: Bangalore

→ Suit MUST be filed in Mumbai (where property situated)
✗ Cannot file in Delhi or Bangalore
```

**2. Compensation for Wrongs to Immovable Property (Section 16 Explanation)**:
```
If suit is ONLY for compensation (not recovery/partition):
→ Can file where property situated OR where defendant resides

Example:
Trespass on land in Mumbai; Defendant in Delhi
Relief: Only damages (not injunction/possession)

→ Can file in Mumbai OR Delhi
```

**3. Suit Against Vessel (Section 17)**:
```
Admiralty jurisdiction:
File where voyage began/ended OR where defendant resides
```

**4. Negotiable Instruments (Section 20 + NI Act Section 142)**:
```
Section 138 NI Act dishonor cases:
Can file where:
- Cheque presented for payment (bank branch), OR
- Payee/holder resides, OR
- Cause of action arose

Note: Restricted by Bridhi Chand v. Surajmull (not unlimited jurisdiction)
```

**5. Compensation for Wrongs to Person or Movables (Section 20)**:
```
File where:
- Wrong committed (tort occurred), OR
- Defendant resides, OR
- Plaintiff resides (if defendant carries on business there)
```

### III. SUBJECT MATTER JURISDICTION

**Rule**: Courts have jurisdiction over types of cases assigned by statute/constitution.

#### Categories:

```
Civil Courts (General):
- All civil matters NOT exclusively assigned elsewhere
- Section 9 CPC: Courts have jurisdiction to try all civil suits EXCEPT expressly/impliedly barred

Tribunals/Special Courts (Exclusive):
- Family Courts: Matrimonial disputes, guardianship
- Consumer Forums: Consumer complaints
- Debt Recovery Tribunals: Bank debt recovery
- NCLT: Company law matters
- Labour Courts: Industrial disputes
- Rent Control Courts: Landlord-tenant (in states with Rent Control Acts)

Rule: If matter assigned exclusively to tribunal → Civil court CANNOT try
```

**Section 9 CPC - Courts to Try All Civil Suits**:
```
"The Courts shall (subject to the provisions herein contained) have 
jurisdiction to try all suits of a civil nature excepting suits of which 
their cognizance is either expressly or impliedly barred."

Civil Nature Test:
✓ Civil: Private rights (property, contract, tort)
✗ Not Civil: Criminal acts, revenue matters, political questions

Expressly Barred:
- Statute says "no civil court shall have jurisdiction"
- Example: Rent Control Act bars civil court for rent disputes

Impliedly Barred:
- Comprehensive code with dispute resolution mechanism
- Example: Industrial Disputes Act creates labour tribunals → Civil courts impliedly barred
```

## Transfer of Cases

### Section 24 - Transfer from Court Lacking Jurisdiction

```
If court finds it lacks jurisdiction → Transfer to competent court

Procedure:
1. Court determines lack of jurisdiction (pecuniary/territorial/subject matter)
2. Passes order transferring to competent court
3. Evidence recorded in transferring court → Valid in receiving court

Effect:
- Suit deemed filed in receiving court from original institution date (for limitation)
- Filing fees paid in first court → Adjusted
```

### Section 25 - Transfer from Supreme Court or High Court

```
Supreme Court/High Court can transfer any case:
FROM: Any court subordinate to it
TO: Any other subordinate court

Grounds (Discretionary):
1. Convenience of parties
2. Ends of justice
3. Important questions of law involved
4. Multiplicity of proceedings

Procedure:
- Application by party OR suo motu by HC/SC
- Notice to opposite party
- Hearing
- Transfer order
```

## Jurisdiction Clauses in Contracts

### Validity and Effect:

```
Parties CAN agree on jurisdiction BY CONTRACT (Section 20 Explanation)

Valid Clause Example:
"All disputes arising from this contract shall be subject to exclusive 
jurisdiction of courts at Mumbai."

Effect:
✓ Creates additional ground for jurisdiction (beyond Section 20)
✓ Can confer jurisdiction where none existed
✗ CANNOT oust jurisdiction of court otherwise competent
✗ CANNOT violate Section 16 (immovable property mandatory jurisdiction)

Case Law - ABC Laminart v. AP Agencies (SC):
"Jurisdiction clause is an additional right; does not take away other 
jurisdictions under Section 20."

Result:
If clause says "Mumbai courts", plaintiff can ALSO sue where Section 20 allows.
To make EXCLUSIVE → Must explicitly say "EXCLUSIVE jurisdiction" + defendant must not object.
```

## Jurisdiction Objections

### When to Raise (Order VII Rule 10, 11):

```
Defendant must raise jurisdiction objection:
EARLIEST OPPORTUNITY (ideally with written statement)

Consequence of Delay:
If defendant participates in proceedings without objection → WAIVER of territorial 
jurisdiction objection

Exception:
- Pecuniary jurisdiction: CANNOT be waived (goes to court's competence)
- Subject matter jurisdiction: CANNOT be waived (goes to court's existence of power)
- Territorial jurisdiction: CAN be waived by conduct
```

### Procedure to Challenge:

```
1. File APPLICATION under Order VII Rule 10 (before written statement)
   OR raise in WRITTEN STATEMENT (Order VIII Rule 1)

2. Court decides jurisdiction AS PRELIMINARY ISSUE

3. Options:
   - Dismiss suit (if lacks jurisdiction + not transferable)
   - Transfer suit (Section 24 if competent court identifiable)
   - Return plaint for presentation to proper court (Order VII Rule 10)

4. Appeal:
   - Against rejection of jurisdiction challenge → Appeal/revision available
   - Against transfer order → Usually no appeal (but revision under Section 115 possible)
```

## Common Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Loan Default

```
Facts:
- Loan agreement signed: Bangalore
- Lender: Mumbai
- Borrower: Delhi
- Payment place: Bangalore
- Default: Borrower stopped paying

Cause of Action Arose:
- Bangalore (contract signed + payment place + default occurred)
- Mumbai (lender's loss suffered there)

Jurisdictions Available (Section 20):
1. Delhi (defendant resides) - Section 20(a)
2. Bangalore (cause of action arose) - Section 20(c)
3. Mumbai (if lender proves part of cause of action arose - arguable)

Cannot File:
- Any other city (no jurisdictional connection)
```

### Scenario 2: Breach of Contract for Sale of Goods

```
Facts:
- Seller: Kolkata
- Buyer: Chennai
- Contract signed: Mumbai (neutral place)
- Goods to be delivered: Chennai
- Payment: Bank transfer to Kolkata
- Breach: Goods not delivered

Cause of Action:
- Mumbai (contract signed)
- Chennai (delivery place - breach occurred here)
- Kolkata (seller's place - from where goods should have been dispatched)

Seller Suing Buyer (for non-payment):
- Kolkata (seller resides) - Section 20(a)
- Chennai (buyer resides) - Section 20(a)
- Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata (cause of action) - Section 20(c)

Buyer Suing Seller (for non-delivery):
- Chennai (buyer resides) - Section 20(a)
- Kolkata (seller resides) - Section 20(a)
- Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata (cause of action) - Section 20(c)
```

### Scenario 3: Partition of Ancestral Property

```
Facts:
- Ancestral house: Jaipur
- Co-owner 1: Jaipur
- Co-owner 2: Pune
- Co-owner 3: Bangalore

Mandatory Jurisdiction (Section 16):
MUST file in Jaipur (where immovable property situated)

Irrelevant:
- Where co-owners reside
- Where family originally from
- Where cause of action arose

Exception: NONE. Section 16 is absolute for immovable property suits.
```

### Scenario 4: Defamation (Tort)

```
Facts:
- Defamatory article published: In Delhi newspaper
- Article written: Mumbai
- Plaintiff: Lives in Bangalore
- Defendant: Lives in Delhi
- Article circulated: All India

Traditional Rule (Section 20):
- Delhi (defendant resides + wrong committed)
- Mumbai (cause of action partly arose)
- Bangalore (plaintiff resides + wrong suffered there)

Recent Restriction (SC in Banyan Tree Holdings):
For online defamation/media:
Plaintiff should sue where:
- Defendant resides, OR
- Plaintiff resides AND suffers "significant" reputational harm there

Not: Every place where accessible (prevents forum shopping)
```

### Scenario 5: Specific Performance of Contract for Land Sale

```
Facts:
- Land: Mumbai
- Seller: Delhi
- Buyer: Bangalore
- Contract signed: Mumbai
- Breach: Seller refuses to convey

Applicable Sections:
- Section 16: Immovable property suit → MUST file where property situated
- Relief: Specific performance (relates to immovable property)

Mandatory Jurisdiction:
Mumbai (where land situated)

Cannot File:
- Delhi (seller's residence) - Section 16 overrides Section 20
- Bangalore (buyer's residence)
```

### Scenario 6: Multiple Defendants in Different States

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff: Sues D1 (Mumbai) + D2 (Delhi) + D3 (Kolkata)
- Contract: Joint liability of all three

Options (Section 20):

Option 1: Section 20(a)
Find court where ALL defendants reside/carry business → Difficult if individual defendants

Option 2: Section 20(b)
File where ANY ONE defendant resides + get LEAVE to add others
Example: File in Mumbai (D1's residence) + seek leave to add D2, D3

Option 3: Section 20(c)
File where cause of action arose (if common cause of action against all)

Practical:
Option 2 most common (file where one defendant + get leave)
Court grants leave if:
- Causes of action connected
- Convenient for all parties
- Prevents multiplicity of suits
```

## Jurisdiction Over Corporations

### Registered Office (Section 20 + Companies Act):

```
Company "resides" where:
1. Registered office (Section 12 Companies Act 2013)
2. Subordinate office where "carries on business"

"Carries on business":
- Has office/branch
- Regular business activity (not one-off transaction)
- Employees/agents present
- Continuous operations

Example:
ABC Ltd: Registered office Mumbai
Branches: Delhi, Bangalore, Chennai

Plaintiff can sue ABC Ltd in:
- Mumbai (registered office) - Section 20(a)
- Delhi, Bangalore, Chennai (carries on business) - Section 20(a)
- Anywhere cause of action arose - Section 20(c)
```

### Service on Company (Order XXIX Rule 2):

```
Service on company valid if served at:
- Registered office, OR
- Principal place of business, OR
- Secretary/director/managing agent
```

## Jurisdiction Over Government (Section 79-82, Order XXVII)

### Union of India:

```
Defendant: Union of India
Jurisdiction: Where cause of action arose (Section 79 + Order XXVII Rule 3)

Service:
- Secretary of concerned department (Central), OR
- Section 80 notice mandatory (2 months prior)
```

### State Government:

```
Defendant: State Government
Jurisdiction: Where cause of action arose (within that state)

Service:
- Chief Secretary OR Secretary of concerned department
- Section 80 notice mandatory (2 months prior)
```

## Summary Chart

```
PECUNIARY JURISDICTION (Value):
Court Fees Act 1870 (Sections 7-11) → Determine value
State notification → Check court's monetary limit
File in LOWEST competent court (Section 15)

TERRITORIAL JURISDICTION (Place):
Section 16: Immovable property → MANDATORY (where property situated)
Section 20(a): Defendant's residence/business
Section 20(c): Cause of action (wholly or in part)
Jurisdiction clause: Additional (not exclusive unless explicit)

SUBJECT MATTER JURISDICTION (Type):
Section 9 CPC: Civil courts try all civil suits UNLESS barred
Expressly barred: Statute says so
Impliedly barred: Tribunal with exclusive jurisdiction

TRANSFER:
Section 24: Transfer if lacking jurisdiction
Section 25: HC/SC transfer for convenience/justice

OBJECTIONS:
Raise earliest (with written statement)
Pecuniary/subject matter: Cannot waive
Territorial: Can waive by conduct
```

## Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Filing in Higher Court Directly
```
❌ Wrong: Suit worth Rs. 10 lakhs filed directly in High Court
✓ Correct: File in District/Civil Court (lowest competent - Section 15)

Exception: High Court's original jurisdiction if state notification permits
```

### Mistake 2: Ignoring Section 16 Mandatory Jurisdiction
```
❌ Wrong: Partition suit for land in Jaipur filed in Delhi (where all parties reside)
✓ Correct: MUST file in Jaipur (Section 16 absolute)

No exception even if all parties agree to Delhi jurisdiction.
```

### Mistake 3: Assuming Jurisdiction Clause is Exclusive
```
❌ Wrong: Contract says "Mumbai jurisdiction" → Plaintiff thinks can ONLY sue in Mumbai
✓ Correct: Mumbai is ADDITIONAL jurisdiction; can also sue under Section 20

To make exclusive: Must say "EXCLUSIVE jurisdiction" + defendant must not object
```

### Mistake 4: Valuing Suit Incorrectly
```
❌ Wrong: Suit for Rs. 5 lakhs + Rs. 10 lakhs land → Value Rs. 15 lakhs (added)
✓ Correct: Value = Rs. 10 lakhs (highest relief; not aggregate)

Court Fees Act Section 7-11: Value is of relief sought (not cumulative)
```

### Mistake 5: Not Raising Jurisdiction Objection Timely
```
❌ Wrong: Defendant files written statement, appears in court, then raises jurisdiction
✓ Correct: Raise jurisdiction in written statement OR preliminary application (earliest)

Delay → Waiver of territorial jurisdiction (not pecuniary/subject matter)
```

### Mistake 6: Thinking Online Defamation Gives Nationwide Jurisdiction
```
❌ Wrong: Defamatory post on website → Sue anywhere in India
✓ Correct: Sue where defendant resides OR where significant harm to reputation (SC restriction)

Banyan Tree Holdings: Cannot sue in every jurisdiction where accessible
```

### Mistake 7: Not Getting Leave for Multiple Defendants (Section 20(b))
```
❌ Wrong: File suit against D1 (Mumbai) + D2 (Delhi) in Mumbai without leave
✓ Correct: File in Mumbai + seek LEAVE to add D2 (Section 20(b))

Court must grant leave (otherwise suit against D2 liable to be dismissed)
```

## Case Law

### 1. RSA Industries v. Abhishek Khanna (SC 2020)
```
Facts: Jurisdiction clause in contract; defendant raised jurisdiction objection
Held: 
- Jurisdiction clause gives ADDITIONAL forum (not exclusive)
- To be exclusive: Must explicitly state + parties' intent clear
- Defendant must object timely; silence → Submission to jurisdiction
```

### 2. ABC Laminart v. AP Agencies (SC 1989)
```
Facts: Contract had "Bombay jurisdiction" clause; suit filed in Bombay
Held:
- Jurisdiction clause DOES NOT oust other courts' jurisdiction under Section 20
- Plaintiff can sue where Section 20 allows EVEN IF jurisdiction clause exists
- Clause creates CONCURRENT jurisdiction (not exclusive)
```

### 3. Swastik Gases v. Indian Oil Corporation (SC 2013)
```
Facts: Multiple defendants in different states; suit filed where one defendant resides
Held:
- Section 20(b): Can sue where ONE defendant resides + get leave for others
- Leave should be granted if:
  * Causes of action connected
  * Prevents multiplicity
  * Not oppressive to other defendants
```

### 4. Kusum Ingots v. Union of India (SC 2004)
```
Facts: Continuing cause of action in multiple jurisdictions
Held:
- "Cause of action" = Bundle of facts giving right to sue
- "Wholly or in part" → Plaintiff can choose ANY place where any part arose
- BUT should not be purely for convenience/forum shopping
```

### 5. Banyan Tree Holdings v. A Murali Krishna Reddy (SC 2009)
```
Facts: Defamation on website; plaintiff sued in his own jurisdiction
Held:
- For online content: Jurisdiction where plaintiff resides + suffers SIGNIFICANT harm
- Not every place where content accessible (prevents unlimited jurisdiction)
- Must show real and substantial connection to forum
```

### 6. New Moga Transport Co. v. United India Insurance (SC 2004)
```
Facts: Jurisdiction clause in insurance policy; accident occurred elsewhere
Held:
- Jurisdiction clause valid but NOT exclusive unless so stated
- Can sue where accident occurred (Section 20(c) - cause of action)
- Clause gives ADDITIONAL option (not ONLY option)
```

### 7. Hakam Singh v. Gammon (SC 1971)
```
Facts: Whether suit for compensation for wrong to immovable property falls under Section 16
Held:
- Section 16: Recovery, partition, foreclosure, rent → MUST file where property situated
- ONLY compensation: Can file where property situated OR where defendant resides (Explanation)
- If mixed relief (compensation + injunction) → Section 16 mandatory
```

# Protocol: Plaint and Written Statement (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-CPC-PLAINT-WRITTEN-STATEMENT  
**Domain**: Civil Procedure  
**Subdomain**: Pleadings and Initiation  
**Version**: 1.0.0  
**Last Updated**: 2025-12-06

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on drafting plaints and written statements under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908, covering mandatory requirements, common defects, and strategic considerations.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "How to draft a plaint?"
- Need to file a civil suit
- Plaint rejected (Order VII Rule 11)
- Drafting written statement
- Raising defenses and counterclaims
- Plaint/written statement amendment needed

## Core Principles

### 1. Plaint = Foundation of Suit

```
Plaint (Order VII):
- Written statement of claim
- Plaintiff's case in chief
- ALL material facts (not evidence, not law)
- Determines reliefs plaintiff entitled to seek

Rule: "Court decides suit based on pleadings"
→ If fact not pleaded → Cannot lead evidence on it
→ If relief not claimed → Court cannot grant it
```

### 2. Written Statement = Defendant's Defense

```
Written Statement (Order VIII):
- Defendant's response to plaint
- Admit/deny each allegation
- Raise defenses
- Counterclaim/set-off (if applicable)

Rule: "What is not denied is deemed admitted" (Order VIII Rule 3)
→ Defendant must specifically traverse each allegation
→ General denial insufficient
```

## PLAINT (Order VII)

### Mandatory Contents (Order VII Rule 1)

```
Every plaint shall contain:

1. Name of Court (Rule 1(a))
2. Name, Description, Residence of Plaintiff (Rule 1(b))
3. Name, Description, Residence of Defendant (Rule 1(c))
   - If defendant's residence unknown: State so
4. Facts Constituting Cause of Action (Rule 1(d))
   - WHEN cause of action arose
   - WHERE cause of action arose (jurisdiction)
5. Facts Showing Court Has Jurisdiction (Rule 1(e))
   - Pecuniary jurisdiction
   - Territorial jurisdiction (Section 20 CPC)
6. Relief Claimed (Rule 1(f))
   - Specific relief sought
   - Alternative relief (if applicable)
   - Interest, if claimed
7. Valuation of Suit (Rule 1(g))
   - For court fees (Court Fees Act 1870)
   - For pecuniary jurisdiction
8. Verification (Rule 1(i))
   - By plaintiff
   - Date and signature

Optional but Recommended:
- Statement of material facts (Rule 1(h))
- Documents relied upon (Rule 14)
```

### Detailed Requirements

#### A. Parties (Rule 1(b), (c))

```
Plaintiff Description:
- Full name: [First name] [Middle name] [Last name]
- Age: [If minor, next friend required]
- Occupation: [Relevant if affects standing]
- Residence: Complete address with pin code

Defendant Description:
- Full name (as accurately known)
- Age (if known)
- Occupation
- Residence: Complete address
  * If unknown: State "Defendant's address unknown" (then personal service waived - substituted service allowed)

Multiple Plaintiffs/Defendants:
- Number each: Plaintiff No. 1, 2, 3...
- Describe each separately
```

**Example**:
```
THE HON'BLE COURT OF CIVIL JUDGE (SENIOR DIVISION), DELHI

SUIT NO. ____ OF 2025

Plaintiff:
Ramesh Kumar, aged 45 years, S/o Late Suresh Kumar, Businessman, residing 
at House No. 123, Sector 45, Noida, Uttar Pradesh - 201301

Versus

Defendant:
Suresh Enterprises Private Limited, a company incorporated under the Companies 
Act 2013, having its registered office at 456 MG Road, Bangalore, Karnataka - 560001
```

#### B. Cause of Action (Rule 1(d))

```
Must state:
1. Material facts (not evidence)
2. Chronological narrative
3. WHEN each fact occurred (dates)
4. WHERE each fact occurred (places - for jurisdiction)

Material Facts vs Evidence vs Law:

Material Facts (MUST plead):
- Plaintiff and defendant entered contract on DD/MM/YYYY
- Consideration: Rs. X
- Defendant breached by failing to deliver goods
- Plaintiff suffered loss Rs. Y

Evidence (DO NOT plead):
- "I have a copy of the contract" → NO (produce at trial)
- "Witness ABC will testify" → NO (evidence stage)

Law (Generally NOT pleaded, court knows law):
- "Under Section 73 Indian Contract Act, damages recoverable" → NO
- Exception: Foreign law must be pleaded and proved
```

**Example**:
```
CAUSE OF ACTION:

1. On 15/01/2024, at Mumbai, the Plaintiff and Defendant entered into a 
   written agreement for sale of 1000 widgets at Rs. 500 per widget, total 
   consideration Rs. 5,00,000/-.

2. The Defendant agreed to deliver the widgets to the Plaintiff's warehouse 
   in Delhi by 28/02/2024.

3. The Plaintiff paid advance of Rs. 1,00,000/- vide cheque no. 123456 dated 
   20/01/2024, which was encashed by the Defendant on 25/01/2024.

4. The Defendant failed to deliver any widgets by 28/02/2024 or thereafter.

5. The Plaintiff issued legal notice dated 05/03/2024 calling upon the Defendant 
   to perform the contract, which was received by the Defendant on 10/03/2024.

6. The Defendant replied vide letter dated 15/03/2024 refusing to perform the 
   contract without lawful excuse.

7. The Plaintiff has suffered loss and damage amounting to Rs. 2,00,000/- being 
   the difference between contract price and market price, and Rs. 1,00,000/- 
   advance paid.

8. Cause of action arose at Mumbai (where contract entered), Delhi (where goods 
   to be delivered), and continues.
```

#### C. Jurisdiction (Rule 1(e))

```
MUST specifically state facts conferring jurisdiction:

Pecuniary:
"The suit value is Rs. ____, which is within the pecuniary jurisdiction of 
this Hon'ble Court as per [State notification/Court's jurisdiction limit]."

Territorial (Section 20):
"The Defendant resides/carries on business at [place] within the territorial 
jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court."
OR
"Cause of action arose wholly/in part at [place] within the territorial 
jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court, as evidenced by the following facts: [...]"

If Section 16 (immovable property):
"The suit immovable property is situated at [place] within the territorial 
jurisdiction of this Hon'ble Court."
```

#### D. Relief Claimed (Rule 1(f))

```
Specific Relief:
State exactly what plaintiff wants court to grant

Types:
1. Money Decree:
   "a) Decree for Rs. ____ with interest @ ___% p.a. from DD/MM/YYYY till realization"

2. Specific Performance:
   "a) Decree for specific performance of the contract dated DD/MM/YYYY directing 
   the Defendant to execute sale deed in favor of Plaintiff for the suit property"

3. Injunction:
   "a) Perpetual injunction restraining the Defendant from [specific act]"

4. Declaration:
   "a) Declaration that the Plaintiff is the absolute owner of the suit property"

5. Possession:
   "a) Decree for possession of the suit property"

6. Partition:
   "a) Decree for partition of the suit property and separate possession of 
   Plaintiff's 1/3rd share"

Alternative Relief:
"b) In the alternative, decree for damages amounting to Rs. ____"

General Relief:
"c) Costs of the suit"
"d) Any other relief the Hon'ble Court deems fit"

Interest:
If claiming interest, MUST specify:
- Rate (percentage)
- From date (typically breach date or suit institution date)
- Till realization (pendente lite + future)
```

**Example**:
```
PRAYER:

In light of the above facts, the Plaintiff humbly prays that this Hon'ble Court 
be pleased to:

a) Decree in favor of the Plaintiff and against the Defendant for recovery of 
   Rs. 3,00,000/- (Rupees Three Lakhs only) being the advance paid (Rs. 1,00,000/-) 
   and damages suffered (Rs. 2,00,000/-);

b) Award interest @ 18% per annum on the decretal amount from the date of suit 
   till realization;

c) Award costs of the suit to the Plaintiff; and

d) Pass such other orders as this Hon'ble Court may deem fit and proper in the 
   circumstances of the case.

AND FOR THIS ACT OF KINDNESS, THE PLAINTIFF SHALL DUTY BOUND FOREVER PRAY.
```

#### E. Valuation (Rule 1(g) + Court Fees Act 1870)

```
Purpose:
1. Court fees calculation
2. Pecuniary jurisdiction determination

Valuation Rules (Court Fees Act Sections 7-11):

Money Suit:
Amount claimed (principal + interest due, NOT future interest)

Immovable Property:
Market value at suit institution

Specific Performance:
Consideration amount in contract

Injunction:
Value of property/right protected

Partition:
Plaintiff's share value (NOT whole property value)

Declaration:
Market value of property (if property involved)

Multiple Reliefs:
Value of relief giving jurisdiction (NOT aggregate)

Example:
Suit for Rs. 5 lakhs (money) + specific performance of land worth Rs. 20 lakhs
→ Valuation: Rs. 20 lakhs (higher relief)
→ Court fees on Rs. 20 lakhs
```

#### F. Verification (Rule 1(i) + Rule 15)

```
Verification Clause:

"VERIFICATION

I, [Name of Plaintiff], the Plaintiff above named, do hereby verify that the 
contents of paragraphs 1 to ___ of the above plaint are true to my personal 
knowledge, and the contents of paragraphs ___ to ___ are based on legal advice 
and I believe them to be true."

Place: [City]
Date: [DD/MM/YYYY]

                                                         [Signature of Plaintiff]

Note:
- Verification by plaintiff (not advocate)
- Personal knowledge vs legal advice distinction
- Date and place mandatory
- False verification → Prosecution for perjury
```

#### G. Documents (Order VII Rule 14)

```
Rule 14: Documents relied upon must be annexed or produced with plaint

Categories:
1. Documents in plaintiff's possession → ANNEX as exhibits
2. Documents in defendant's possession → MENTION in plaint, seek discovery later
3. Documents in third party's possession → MENTION, seek commission to produce

Annexure:
"Annex P-1: Copy of Sale Agreement dated 15/01/2024"
"Annex P-2: Copy of Cheque No. 123456 dated 20/01/2024"
"Annex P-3: Copy of Legal Notice dated 05/03/2024"

Certified Copies:
- Public documents: Certified copies (if originals with government)
- Private documents: Original or certified copy
```

### Rejection of Plaint (Order VII Rule 11)

```
Court SHALL reject plaint if:

(a) Suit appears from plaint to be BARRED BY LAW
    Example: Limitation expired, jurisdiction clause violated

(b) RELIEF CLAIMED IS UNDERVALUED and plaintiff fails to correct within time
    Example: Court fees short-paid due to undervaluation

(c) Plaint not VERIFIED as required
    Example: Verification missing or defective

(d) Facts do not disclose CAUSE OF ACTION
    Example: Plaint shows no legal right violated

(e) Suit appears from plaint to be BARRED by res judicata
    Example: Same matter already decided between parties

(f) Suit appears from plaint to be BARRED by limitation (if clear from plaint)
    Example: Plaint shows suit filed after 3 years limitation

(g) Suit appears from plaint to be BARRED by agreement (arbitration clause)
    Example: Mandatory arbitration clause exists

Rejection Effect:
- Plaint returned to plaintiff
- Can file fresh suit (limitation saved if filed within prescribed time)
- Appeal: Against rejection under Order VII Rule 11 → Appeal under Order 43 Rule 1(a)
```

### Return of Plaint (Order VII Rule 10)

```
Court MAY return plaint for presentation to proper court if:
- Suit filed in court lacking jurisdiction
- Proper court identifiable

Difference from Rejection:
- Rejection (Rule 11): Plaint defective, cannot be cured by re-filing in same court
- Return (Rule 10): Jurisdiction lacking, can file in proper court

Effect:
- Limitation saved if re-filed in proper court within time prescribed (Section 14 Limitation Act)
```

## WRITTEN STATEMENT (Order VIII)

### Mandatory Contents (Order VIII Rule 1)

```
Defendant shall, within 30 days from service of summons (extendable up to 90 days 
from service), file written statement containing:

1. Admission or Denial of Each Allegation (Rule 3)
   - Specific traversal required
   - General denial insufficient

2. Affirmative Defenses (Rule 2)
   - New facts in defendant's favor
   - Example: Payment made, limitation, res judicata

3. Counterclaim (Rule 6A) - Optional
   - Defendant's own claim against plaintiff

4. Set-Off (Rule 6) - Optional
   - Defendant's claim for debt/money against plaintiff's claim

5. Verification (Rule 5)
   - By defendant
   - Similar to plaint verification
```

### Detailed Requirements

#### A. Specific Traversal (Order VIII Rule 3)

```
Rule 3: "Every allegation of fact in the plaint, if not denied specifically or 
by necessary implication, or stated to be not admitted, shall be taken to be 
admitted."

Effect: "WHAT IS NOT DENIED IS DEEMED ADMITTED"

Modes of Denial:

1. Specific Denial:
   Plaint: "Plaintiff paid Rs. 1 lakh on 15/01/2024"
   Written Statement: "Contents of para ___ are denied. Plaintiff did not pay 
   any amount on 15/01/2024 or at all."

2. Denial with Alternative Fact:
   Plaint: "Defendant breached contract by not delivering goods"
   Written Statement: "Contents of para ___ are denied. The contract was 
   frustrated due to force majeure event (pandemic) beyond defendant's control."

3. Denial Due to Lack of Knowledge:
   Plaint: "Defendant has 10 branches across India"
   Written Statement: "Defendant has no knowledge of the contents of para ___ 
   and therefore denies the same."

4. Not Admitted (Requiring Plaintiff to Prove):
   Plaint: "Market value of land is Rs. 50 lakhs"
   Written Statement: "Contents of para ___ are not admitted and plaintiff is 
   put to strict proof thereof."

General Denial (INSUFFICIENT):
❌ "All contents of plaint are denied" → INVALID
✓ "Contents of para 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 are denied. Para 4 and 6 are admitted."
```

#### B. Affirmative Defenses (Order VIII Rule 2)

```
Defendant must plead new facts showing:
- Plaintiff's claim defeated/reduced
- Defendant has valid excuse

Types:

1. Limitation (Section 3 Limitation Act 1963):
   "The suit is barred by limitation under Article ___ of the Limitation Act 
   1963, as the cause of action arose on DD/MM/YYYY and the suit was filed on 
   DD/MM/YYYY, after expiry of ___ years prescribed period."

2. Res Judicata (Section 11 CPC):
   "The suit is barred by res judicata, as the same matter was decided in Suit 
   No. ____ by [Court] vide judgment dated DD/MM/YYYY."

3. Payment/Satisfaction:
   "The defendant has already paid Rs. ___ vide cheque no. ____ dated DD/MM/YYYY, 
   which extinguishes plaintiff's claim."

4. Waiver/Acquiescence:
   "The plaintiff waived his right by continuing to perform the contract after 
   becoming aware of the alleged breach."

5. Estoppel:
   "The plaintiff is estopped from claiming [relief] as he represented to the 
   defendant on DD/MM/YYYY that [representation], and the defendant acted on 
   such representation to his detriment."

6. Accord and Satisfaction:
   "The parties mutually agreed on DD/MM/YYYY to settle all disputes by payment 
   of Rs. ____, which was paid and accepted in full and final settlement."

7. Force Majeure/Frustration:
   "The contract became impossible to perform due to [event] on DD/MM/YYYY, 
   frustrating the contract under Section 56 Indian Contract Act."

8. Jurisdiction Objection:
   "This Hon'ble Court lacks territorial jurisdiction as the defendant does not 
   reside/carry business within the jurisdiction, nor did any part of cause of 
   action arise here."
```

#### C. Counterclaim (Order VIII Rule 6A)

```
Counterclaim = Defendant's independent claim against plaintiff arising out of 
same/connected transaction

Conditions:
1. Must arise out of SAME transaction OR connected transaction
2. Must be within court's jurisdiction (pecuniary + territorial)

Procedure:
- Include in written statement as separate section "COUNTERCLAIM"
- Treat as separate suit (plaintiff becomes defendant to counterclaim)
- Plaintiff files reply to counterclaim (within 30 days)

Effect:
- Both claim and counterclaim tried together
- Can result in mutual set-off
- If counterclaim exceeds claim → Defendant gets decree for difference

Example:
Plaintiff's Claim: Rs. 5 lakhs for goods sold
Defendant's Counterclaim: Rs. 7 lakhs for defective goods causing loss

Result: If both proved → Decree for defendant for Rs. 2 lakhs (7 - 5)
```

#### D. Set-Off (Order VIII Rule 6)

```
Set-Off = Defendant's claim for ASCERTAINED SUM against plaintiff's claim

Conditions:
1. Ascertained sum (not unliquidated damages)
2. Legally recoverable from plaintiff
3. Defendant entitled at suit institution

Types:

Legal Set-Off (Rule 6):
- Ascertained sum
- Recoverable as debt
- Example: Plaintiff claims Rs. 5 lakhs; Defendant has decree against plaintiff for Rs. 2 lakhs
  → Set-off Rs. 2 lakhs → Decree for Rs. 3 lakhs to plaintiff

Equitable Set-Off (English law origin, recognized in India):
- Claims arising from same transaction
- Inequitable to allow plaintiff to recover without set-off
- Example: Sale contract - Plaintiff claims price; Defendant claims damages for defect in same goods

Procedure:
- Plead in written statement
- Give notice of set-off (details of claim)
- Court adjusts decretal amount
```

#### E. Verification (Order VIII Rule 5)

```
Verification Clause (similar to plaint):

"VERIFICATION

I, [Name of Defendant], the Defendant above named, do hereby verify that the 
contents of paragraphs 1 to ___ of the above written statement are true to my 
personal knowledge, and the contents of paragraphs ___ to ___ are based on 
legal advice and I believe them to be true."

Place: [City]
Date: [DD/MM/YYYY]

                                                       [Signature of Defendant]
```

### Time Limit for Written Statement (Order VIII Rule 1)

```
Original Time: 30 days from service of summons

Extension: Court MAY extend up to 90 days from service (with recording reasons)

Post-90 Days: Court SHALL NOT allow written statement (MANDATORY)
(Amendment 2002 - Section 26, CPC Amendment Act 2002)

Recent SC Judgment - Salem Advocate Bar Association (2005):
- Strict adherence to 90-day outer limit
- Extension beyond 90 days ONLY in EXCEPTIONAL circumstances
- Delay defeats entire purpose of CPC amendments for speedy disposal

Consequences of Non-Filing:
- Defendant loses right to file written statement
- Plaintiff's allegations deemed admitted (Order VIII Rule 10)
- Ex parte decree against defendant (Order IX Rule 6)
```

### Defendant's Failure to Appear (Order IX)

```
Rule 6: If defendant does not appear on hearing day AND has not filed written 
statement:
→ Plaintiff may prove claim
→ Court passes EX PARTE decree

Rule 7: If defendant appears but does not file written statement:
→ Allegations deemed admitted (Order VIII Rule 10)
→ Plaintiff needs minimal proof
→ Decree likely

Rule 8: If defendant files written statement but does not appear on hearing:
→ Court may proceed with trial
→ Plaintiff proves case
→ Decree passed

Setting Aside Ex Parte Decree (Rule 13):
Defendant can apply to set aside if:
- Summons not duly served, OR
- Prevented by sufficient cause from appearing

Time: Within 30 days from knowledge of decree
Procedure: Application + affidavit showing cause
Court may set aside and restore suit (if satisfied)
```

## Amendments (Order VI Rule 17)

### Plaint/Written Statement Amendment

```
Rule 17: Court may allow amendment at any stage to determine real questions in controversy

Conditions:

Before Trial Commences:
- Liberal approach
- Amendment allowed unless:
  * Changes cause of action completely
  * Introduces new parties beyond limitation
  * Defeats justice

After Trial Commences:
- Restrictive approach
- Amendment allowed ONLY if:
  * Necessary for determining real controversy
  * No prejudice to other party (OR compensate by costs/adjournment)
  * Not changing cause of action fundamentally

After Decree:
- Very narrow
- Correction of clerical/arithmetical errors only
- NOT substantive amendment

Limitation Considerations (Section 14 Limitation Act):
If amendment introduces new claim beyond limitation:
→ Amendment DENIED (unless relates back to original institution date)
```

**Case Law - Pirgonda Hongonda Patil v. Kalgonda Shidgonda Patil (SC 1957)**:
```
"The object of the provision is that the court should try the merits of the case 
that come before it and, therefore, amendments should be allowed to facilitate a 
decision on merits."

BUT NOT if:
- Opposite party prejudiced beyond compensation by costs
- Fundamentally alters case
- Introduces time-barred claim
```

## Common Defects and Remedies

### Defects in Plaint

```
Defect 1: Cause of Action Not Disclosed
Consequence: Rejection under Order VII Rule 11(d)
Remedy: Amend plaint to add material facts showing legal right violated

Defect 2: Jurisdiction Not Shown
Consequence: Rejection/Return under Order VII Rule 10-11
Remedy: Add specific averments showing pecuniary + territorial jurisdiction

Defect 3: Relief Undervalued
Consequence: Rejection under Order VII Rule 11(b) if court fees short
Remedy: Correct valuation + pay additional court fees within time given by court

Defect 4: Verification Defective
Consequence: Rejection under Order VII Rule 11(c)
Remedy: File corrected verification (if court allows)

Defect 5: Suit Barred by Limitation (Clear from Plaint)
Consequence: Rejection under Order VII Rule 11(f)
Remedy: Plead Section 5 Limitation Act (condonation) + show sufficient cause for delay

Defect 6: Documents Not Annexed
Consequence: Plaintiff cannot rely on documents not annexed/mentioned (Order XIII Rule 1)
Remedy: Seek leave to produce additional documents (with explanation for non-production)
```

### Defects in Written Statement

```
Defect 1: General Denial (Not Specific)
Consequence: Allegations deemed admitted (Order VIII Rule 3)
Remedy: Amend written statement to specifically traverse each allegation

Defect 2: Affirmative Defense Not Pleaded
Consequence: Cannot lead evidence on defense not pleaded (Order XIV Rule 2)
Remedy: Seek amendment to add defense (if trial not commenced)

Defect 3: Filed Beyond 90 Days
Consequence: Court SHALL NOT allow (Order VIII Rule 1 proviso)
Remedy: Show exceptional circumstances OR accept consequences (deemed admission)

Defect 4: Counterclaim Beyond Jurisdiction
Consequence: Counterclaim rejected/tried separately
Remedy: Withdraw counterclaim from written statement, file independent suit

Defect 5: Verification Missing
Consequence: Written statement liable to be struck off
Remedy: File verification immediately
```

## Strategic Considerations

### Drafting Plaint - Best Practices

```
1. Chronological Narrative:
   - Facts in date order
   - Clear timeline
   - Cause of action easily identifiable

2. Precision in Parties:
   - Correct legal names (especially companies)
   - Accurate addresses (for service)
   - Capacity clearly stated

3. Jurisdiction Emphasis:
   - Detailed averments on territorial jurisdiction
   - Multiple grounds (Section 20(a) + (c))
   - Anticipate jurisdiction challenge

4. Relief Clarity:
   - Specific relief sought
   - Alternative relief (if primary may fail)
   - Interest clearly claimed (rate + period)

5. Documents:
   - Annex all key documents
   - Mention documents in defendant's possession (for discovery)
   - Mark exhibits consistently (P-1, P-2...)

6. Valuation:
   - Accurate (not undervalued)
   - Court fees correctly calculated
   - Jurisdiction confirmed

7. Anticipate Defenses:
   - Address potential defenses in plaint itself
   - Example: If limitation issue, plead Section 5 condonation grounds
```

### Drafting Written Statement - Best Practices

```
1. Specific Traversal:
   - Admit what is true
   - Deny specifically what is false
   - Put to proof what is uncertain

2. Affirmative Defenses Early:
   - Jurisdiction objection (first defense)
   - Limitation (if applicable)
   - Res judicata, waiver, estoppel

3. Counterclaim Strategy:
   - If strong counterclaim → Include (deters plaintiff)
   - If exceeds plaintiff's claim → Negotiation leverage
   - If weak → Avoid (plaintiff gets discovery of defendant's case)

4. Documents:
   - Annex documents proving defense
   - Example: Payment receipts, settlement agreements, notices

5. Verification Careful:
   - True to knowledge vs legal advice distinction
   - No false verification (perjury risk)

6. Timeline Adherence:
   - File within 30 days (get extension if needed)
   - NEVER exceed 90 days (fatal)
```

## Case Law

### 1. Urmila Devi v. Yashpal Singh (SC 2022)
```
Facts: Written statement filed beyond 90 days
Held:
- 90-day limit MANDATORY (Order VIII Rule 1 proviso post-2002 amendment)
- Extension beyond 90 days ONLY in EXCEPTIONAL circumstances
- Mere negligence/busy schedule NOT sufficient cause
```

### 2. Virendra Kashinath v. Vinayak N. Joshi (SC 2019)
```
Facts: Plaint amendment sought to introduce new cause of action after limitation
Held:
- Amendment cannot introduce time-barred claim
- Unless amendment "relates back" to original institution date
- New cause of action beyond limitation → Amendment denied
```

### 3. Rajesh Kumar Aggarwal v. K.K. Modi (SC 2006)
```
Facts: Plaint rejected under Order VII Rule 11(d) for not disclosing cause of action
Held:
- Cause of action = Bundle of facts entitling plaintiff to relief
- Court sees plaint ALONE (not documents/evidence)
- If plaint shows no legal right violated → Rejection proper
```

### 4. Devi Prasad v. Delhi Administration (SC 1973)
```
Facts: Verification defective (advocate verified, not party)
Held:
- Verification by party MANDATORY (not advocate)
- Defective verification → Plaint liable to rejection (Order VII Rule 11(c))
- Can be cured if court allows
```

### 5. Sangram Singh v. Election Tribunal (SC 1955)
```
Facts: General denial in written statement
Held:
- Order VIII Rule 3: What not denied deemed admitted
- General denial INSUFFICIENT
- Specific traversal of each allegation REQUIRED
```

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on special civil procedures under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908, including summary suits (Order XXXVII), interim injunctions (Order XXXIX), receivers (Order XL), and interpleader suits (Order XXXV).

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "What is summary suit procedure?"
- Need urgent interim injunction
- Temporary injunction required
- Appointment of receiver needed
- Multiple claimants to same property (interpleader)
- Speedy recovery for negotiable instruments

## Core Framework

### Special Procedures vs Regular Suits

```
REGULAR SUIT (Ordinary Procedure):
- Full trial procedure (Order XIV-XVIII)
- Evidence examination
- Timeline: 1-3 years (average)

SPECIAL PROCEDURES:
- Expedited procedures
- Limited scope
- Faster resolution
- Specific reliefs/situations

Types:
1. Summary Suit (Order XXXVII) - Speedy money recovery
2. Temporary Injunction (Order XXXIX) - Prevent imminent harm
3. Receiver (Order XL) - Protect disputed property
4. Interpleader (Order XXXV) - Resolve competing claims
```

## SUMMARY SUIT (Order XXXVII)

### Purpose and Scope (Rule 1)

```
Rule 1: Summary Suit for Recovery of Debt

Applicable To:
- Claims based on NEGOTIABLE INSTRUMENTS (promissory notes, bills of exchange, cheques)
- Debt/liquidated demand in writing
- Contracts (if expressly payable at certain time)

Geographical Limit:
- Only in areas where Order XXXVII applies (High Courts with original jurisdiction: Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Delhi - specific notifications)

NOT Applicable To:
- Unliquidated claims
- Damages for breach of contract (unless liquidated)
- Regular money suits
- Areas where Order XXXVII not in force

Advantage:
- SPEEDY disposal (within 6 months typical)
- Defendant must obtain LEAVE to defend (not automatic right)
```

### Procedure for Summary Suit (Rule 2-6)

```
Step 1: PLAINT Filed (Rule 2)
Similar to regular plaint + special endorsement:
"This suit is filed under Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908"

Accompanied by:
- Affidavit verifying debt
- Documents (promissory note, bill of exchange, contract)

Step 2: SUMMONS Issued (Rule 3)
Special summons:
- Requires defendant to appear on specified date
- States: "If you wish to defend the suit, you must apply for LEAVE TO DEFEND within 30 days"

Contents:
- Claim details
- Documents on which claim based
- Consequence of non-appearance (summary judgment)

Step 3: Defendant's Options

OPTION A: ADMIT CLAIM (No Response)
- If defendant does not respond within 30 days:
  → Plaintiff entitled to SUMMARY JUDGMENT (immediate decree without trial)
  → Decree passed on affidavit + documents (no evidence needed)

OPTION B: APPLY FOR LEAVE TO DEFEND (Rule 3)
Within 30 days:
- File affidavit showing:
  * Triable defense (not frivolous/vexatious)
  * Grounds to contest claim
- May be UNCONDITIONAL leave OR CONDITIONAL leave (deposit amount in court)

Step 4: Court Decides Leave Application (Rule 3)

Court Examines:
- Is there genuine dispute on merits?
- OR defendant trying to delay?

Options:

(a) REFUSE Leave to Defend:
    - No triable defense shown
    - Defense frivolous/sham
    → Pass SUMMARY DECREE (immediate decree without trial)

(b) GRANT Unconditional Leave to Defend:
    - Triable defense established
    → Convert to REGULAR SUIT (full trial procedure)

(c) GRANT Conditional Leave to Defend (Rule 3 proviso):
    - Defendant shows arguable defense BUT suspicious circumstances
    → Grant leave ON CONDITION: Deposit decree amount/part in court
    → If defendant deposits → Suit proceeds as regular
    → If defendant fails to deposit → Summary decree passed

Step 5: If Leave Granted
- Suit proceeds as REGULAR SUIT (written statement, issues, evidence, trial)
- No longer summary procedure

Step 6: If Leave Refused
- SUMMARY DECREE passed (immediate)
- Defendant can appeal (Order 43 - appealable order)
```

### Grounds for Leave to Defend

```
Defendant Must Show:

✓ TRIABLE DEFENSE (Genuine Dispute):
  - Promissory note forged/fabricated
  - Payment already made (with evidence)
  - Consideration failed
  - Debt time-barred (limitation expired)
  - Debt discharged (release agreement)

✗ NOT TRIABLE DEFENSE:
  - "I need time to verify records" (delaying tactic)
  - "I don't remember signing" (without showing forgery)
  - Vague/general denial without specifics
  - Technical defenses without merit

Standard:
Prima facie defense (not conclusive proof)
→ If defense arguable → Leave granted
→ If defense clearly sham → Leave refused
```

### Summary Judgment (Rule 5)

```
Summary Judgment = Decree without full trial

Passed When:
- Defendant does not apply for leave to defend (within 30 days), OR
- Defendant applies but leave refused

Basis:
- Plaintiff's affidavit + documents
- No oral evidence needed
- No witness examination

Effect:
- Executable decree (like regular decree)
- Defendant can appeal (against refusal of leave + summary decree)

Timeline:
- From plaint to summary decree: 2-4 months (if leave not sought/refused)
- Contrast with regular suit: 1-3 years
```

## TEMPORARY INJUNCTION (Order XXXIX)

### Purpose (Rule 1-2)

```
Temporary Injunction (Interim Injunction):
- Court order restraining party from doing act OR directing party to do act
- PENDING final decision in suit
- Prevents irreparable harm before trial

Types:

1. TEMPORARY INJUNCTION (Rule 1-2):
   - During pendency of suit (before final decree)
   - Preserves status quo
   - Prevents change in situation

2. PERPETUAL INJUNCTION (Sections 36-42 Specific Relief Act):
   - After trial, in final decree
   - Permanent restraint

Distinction:
Temporary = Interim relief (pending suit)
Perpetual = Final relief (in decree after trial)
```

### Conditions for Temporary Injunction (Rule 1-2)

```
Rule 1: Injunction to Restrain Repetition/Continuance of Breach

Rule 2: Injunction to Restrain Waste/Alienation

Court Grants Temporary Injunction If:

1. PRIMA FACIE CASE:
   - Plaintiff shows serious question to be tried
   - NOT conclusive proof; just arguable case
   
   Example: Property title disputed → Prima facie if plaintiff produces sale deed

2. BALANCE OF CONVENIENCE:
   - Greater hardship to plaintiff if injunction NOT granted
   - vs
   - Lesser hardship to defendant if injunction granted
   
   Comparison:
   - If injunction not granted → Plaintiff's property destroyed (irreparable)
   - If injunction granted → Defendant stops construction (can resume if plaintiff loses)
   → Balance favors plaintiff

3. IRREPARABLE INJURY:
   - Injury that cannot be compensated by money (damages inadequate)
   
   Examples:
   ✓ Irreparable: Destruction of ancestral property, trade secret disclosure, goodwill loss
   ✗ NOT Irreparable: Monetary loss (can be compensated)

ALL THREE Must Be Satisfied:
Prima facie + Balance of convenience + Irreparable injury
→ If any one fails → Injunction refused
```

### Procedure for Temporary Injunction (Rule 3)

```
Step 1: File APPLICATION (Interim Application)
With main suit OR during pendency

Contents:
- Grounds (prima facie case, balance, irreparable injury)
- Affidavit supporting application
- Specific relief sought (restraining order)

Step 2: Notice to Defendant (Rule 3)
- Court issues notice to defendant
- Time to file reply (usually 7-15 days)
- OR ex parte application (if urgency - defendant not given prior notice)

Step 3: Hearing
- Arguments by both parties
- Court examines three conditions

Step 4: Order
Options:

(a) GRANT Temporary Injunction:
    - Defendant restrained from [specific act]
    - Conditions: Plaintiff may have to:
      * Furnish security/deposit
      * Give undertaking for damages (if ultimately suit dismissed)
    - Valid till final decree OR earlier vacation

(b) REFUSE Temporary Injunction:
    - Not satisfied with three conditions
    - Plaintiff can seek reconsideration (if circumstances change)

(c) STATUS QUO Order:
    - Both parties maintain existing situation
    - Neither party changes status

Effect of Injunction:
- Defendant must comply (cannot do prohibited act)
- Breach → Contempt of court (Order XXXIX Rule 2A - imprisonment/fine)
```

### Ex Parte Temporary Injunction (Rule 3 proviso)

```
Ex Parte = Without notice to defendant

When Granted:
- EXTREME URGENCY (immediate irreparable harm)
- Delay in notice would defeat purpose

Example:
- Defendant demolishing plaintiff's house tomorrow
- No time to serve notice, hear defendant
→ Court may pass ex parte injunction (immediate restraint)

Procedure:
1. Plaintiff shows extreme urgency
2. Affidavit stating facts + urgency
3. Court may pass ex parte injunction

Duration:
- Very short (7-15 days typically)
- Defendant given notice immediately
- Hearing held within short time
- Court decides: Continue injunction OR vacate

Risk:
- If plaintiff's case weak → Defendant may claim damages for wrongful injunction
- Plaintiff may be required to furnish heavy security
```

### Vacation of Temporary Injunction (Rule 4)

```
Rule 4: Defendant may apply to vacate injunction

Grounds:
- Changed circumstances (facts different from when injunction granted)
- Plaintiff not prosecuting suit diligently
- Plaintiff's case weak (on further examination)

Procedure:
- Defendant files application
- Affidavit showing grounds
- Hearing
- Court decides: Vacate OR continue injunction

Court may also vacate SUO MOTU (on its own) if:
- Plaintiff delays trial unreasonably
- Injunction obtained by misrepresentation/suppression of facts
```

### Damages for Wrongful Injunction (Rule 2A)

```
Rule 2A: Damages if Injunction Wrongfully Obtained

If plaintiff obtains injunction AND ultimately suit dismissed:
→ Defendant may claim damages from plaintiff

Defendant must show:
- Injunction wrongfully obtained (plaintiff's case baseless)
- Defendant suffered loss due to injunction
- Loss quantifiable

Example:
Plaintiff obtained injunction stopping defendant's business
After trial, plaintiff's suit dismissed (no right to injunction)
Defendant lost Rs. 10 lakhs in business revenue
→ Defendant can claim Rs. 10 lakhs damages from plaintiff

Protection:
- Court may require plaintiff to furnish UNDERTAKING for damages before granting injunction
- Ensures defendant compensated if plaintiff fails
```

## RECEIVER (Order XL)

### Purpose (Rule 1)

```
Receiver = Court-appointed person to manage disputed property

When Appointed:
- Property subject to litigation (ownership disputed)
- Risk of waste/destruction/alienation by party in possession
- Preserve property till final decision

Receiver's Role:
- Take possession of property
- Manage property (collect rent, maintain)
- Preserve property value
- Prevent waste
- Act as court's officer (not party's agent)

Property Types:
- Immovable property (land, building)
- Movable property (goods, machinery)
- Business/company (in extreme cases)
- Rent/income from property
```

### Conditions for Appointment (Rule 1)

```
Court Appoints Receiver If:

1. DISPUTED OWNERSHIP:
   - Parties claiming same property
   - Unclear who rightful owner

2. RISK OF WASTE:
   - Party in possession may destroy/damage property
   - Property value diminishing

3. RISK OF ALIENATION:
   - Party may sell/transfer property to third party
   - Defeat other party's claim

4. PRESERVATION NECESSARY:
   - Property requires management (rental property, business)
   - Without receiver, property deteriorates

5. BALANCE OF CONVENIENCE:
   - Appointing receiver beneficial to both parties
   - Neutral management pending decision
```

### Procedure for Receiver (Rule 2-4)

```
Step 1: Application for Receiver
- Party applies (plaintiff or defendant)
- Affidavit showing:
  * Disputed ownership
  * Risk of waste/alienation
  * Necessity of receiver

Step 2: Notice to Opposite Party
- Court issues notice
- Opposite party may:
  * Consent
  * Object (no necessity, willing to furnish security)

Step 3: Hearing
- Court examines necessity
- Balances hardship

Step 4: Appointment Order
If satisfied:
- Court appoints receiver (advocate, chartered accountant, or court officer)
- Defines receiver's powers:
  * Take possession
  * Collect rent/income
  * Maintain property
  * File periodic reports to court

Step 5: Receiver's Oath (Rule 3)
- Receiver takes oath to discharge duties faithfully
- Acts as court's officer (impartial)

Step 6: Remuneration (Rule 4)
- Receiver entitled to remuneration (paid from property income/court-fixed)
- Expenses reimbursed
```

### Powers and Duties of Receiver (Rule 1)

```
Powers:
- Take possession of property
- Collect rent/income
- Initiate/defend suits related to property (with court permission)
- Sell property (only with court permission)
- Engage staff for property management

Duties:
- Preserve property
- File periodic accounts (income, expenses)
- Seek court direction for major decisions
- Act impartially (not favor any party)
- Hand over property to rightful owner after decree

Accountability:
- Receiver accountable to court
- Any misconduct → Removal + damages
```

### Discharge of Receiver (Rule 4)

```
Receiver Discharged When:
- Final decree passed (court decides rightful owner)
- Property handed over to decree-holder
- Parties reach settlement
- Court finds receiver no longer necessary

Final Account:
- Receiver files final account (income, expenses, balance)
- Court approves account
- Surplus paid to decree-holder
- Receiver discharged
```

## INTERPLEADER SUIT (Order XXXV)

### Purpose (Rule 1)

```
Interpleader = When two or more persons claim same property/debt from third party

Scenario:
- A holds property/money
- B claims: "That property is mine"
- C also claims: "No, it's mine"
→ A is STAKEHOLDER (holder of property)
→ B and C are CLAIMANTS

A files INTERPLEADER SUIT:
- Court decides between B and C
- A hands over property to rightful claimant
- A relieved from liability

Example:
- Tenant holds Rs. 1 lakh rent
- Landlord claims: "Pay me rent"
- Mortgagee claims: "Landlord mortgaged property to me; pay me rent"
→ Tenant files interpleader suit
→ Court decides: Landlord vs Mortgagee
→ Tenant pays rent to winner
```

### Conditions for Interpleader (Rule 1)

```
Interpleader Suit Lies If:

1. TWO OR MORE PERSONS claim same property/debt from stakeholder
2. Stakeholder has NO INTEREST in property (mere holder)
3. Stakeholder READY TO HAND OVER to rightful claimant
4. Stakeholder does NOT KNOW who rightful claimant is
5. Claimants' claims INDEPENDENT (not from same right)

NOT Interpleader If:
- Stakeholder has own interest in property
- Stakeholder knows rightful claimant (collusion)
- Only one claimant (regular suit)
```

### Procedure for Interpleader (Rule 1-5)

```
Step 1: Stakeholder Files PLAINT
- Names both/all claimants as defendants
- States: "I hold property; B and C both claim; I have no interest; I am ready to hand over to rightful claimant"
- Deposits property in court OR gives security

Step 2: Court Summons Claimants
- Both claimants summoned
- Required to state their claims

Step 3: Claimants File Written Statements
- Each claimant states basis of claim
- May dispute other claimant's claim

Step 4: Trial Between Claimants
- Stakeholder generally OUT of suit (after depositing property)
- Trial proceeds between claimants (like regular suit)
- Evidence examination

Step 5: Decree
- Court decides: Which claimant entitled to property
- Property/money paid to successful claimant
- Costs: Usually borne by claimants (stakeholder protected)

Stakeholder's Protection:
- If acted bona fide → Not liable for costs
- Protected from suit by either claimant (court's decision final)
```

## COMMON SCENARIOS

### Scenario 1: Summary Suit for Cheque Dishonor

```
Facts:
- Defendant issued cheque for Rs. 10 lakhs (promissory note)
- Cheque bounced
- Plaintiff sues under Order XXXVII (summary suit)

Procedure:
1. Plaint filed with affidavit + cheque copy
2. Summons issued: "Apply for leave to defend within 30 days"
3. Defendant's options:
   
   Option A: No response → Summary decree for Rs. 10 lakhs (within 2-3 months)
   
   Option B: Apply for leave to defend:
   Defendant shows: "Cheque was security for loan; loan never availed"
   Court decides: Triable defense → Grant unconditional leave → Convert to regular suit

   Option C: Apply for leave but weak defense:
   Defendant shows: "Need time to verify accounts"
   Court decides: Not triable defense → Refuse leave → Summary decree

Result: If leave refused → Decree in 3-4 months (vs 2-3 years for regular suit)
```

### Scenario 2: Temporary Injunction to Stop Construction

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff claims ownership of land
- Defendant constructing on same land
- Plaintiff files suit for possession + applies for temporary injunction

Three-Fold Test:

1. Prima Facie Case:
   - Plaintiff produces sale deed (1980)
   - Defendant has no documents
   → Prima facie case established

2. Balance of Convenience:
   - If injunction NOT granted: Defendant builds house (difficult to demolish later)
   - If injunction granted: Defendant stops construction (can resume if wins)
   → Balance favors plaintiff

3. Irreparable Injury:
   - If construction completed, demolition costly/impractical
   - Damages inadequate (plaintiff wants land, not money)
   → Irreparable injury shown

Court Order:
"Defendant restrained from constructing on suit property till final decree. 
Plaintiff to furnish security of Rs. 5 lakhs (for damages if ultimately suit dismissed)."

Effect:
- Defendant must stop construction
- If continues → Contempt (imprisonment/fine)
- Status quo maintained till trial complete
```

### Scenario 3: Receiver for Disputed Rental Property

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff and defendant both claim ownership of building (generating Rs. 2 lakhs/month rent)
- Defendant in possession, collecting rent
- Plaintiff fears defendant will dissipate rent, not maintain building

Application for Receiver:
- Plaintiff applies: "Appoint receiver to collect rent, maintain building"

Court Decision:
- Ownership disputed (trial needed - 1-2 years)
- Risk: Defendant may collect rent, not account for it
- Building needs maintenance (roof leaking)
→ Appoint receiver

Receiver Appointed:
- Advocate Mr. X appointed receiver
- Powers: Collect rent (Rs. 2 lakhs/month), pay for repairs, file monthly accounts

During Suit (2 years):
- Receiver collects Rs. 48 lakhs rent
- Spends Rs. 8 lakhs on repairs
- Balance: Rs. 40 lakhs (deposited in court)

After Decree (Plaintiff Wins):
- Court orders: Pay Rs. 40 lakhs + accrued rent to plaintiff
- Receiver discharged

If Defendant Had Won:
- Rs. 40 lakhs paid to defendant
- Receiver ensured fair management (regardless of winner)
```

### Scenario 4: Interpleader Suit

```
Facts:
- Bank holds fixed deposit (FD) of Rs. 20 lakhs in name of Mr. A (deceased)
- Mr. A's son (B) claims: "I am heir; pay me FD"
- Mr. A's brother (C) claims: "Mr. A executed will leaving FD to me; pay me"
→ Bank does not know who rightful claimant

Interpleader Suit:
1. Bank files interpleader suit naming B and C as defendants
2. Bank deposits Rs. 20 lakhs in court (or gives security)
3. Bank states: "I have no interest; ready to pay rightful claimant"

Trial Between B and C:
- B proves: He is son (succession certificate)
- C proves: Will executed by Mr. A leaving FD to C (will + attesting witnesses)
- Court examines will validity

Decree:
- If will valid → C gets Rs. 20 lakhs
- If will invalid/forged → B gets Rs. 20 lakhs
- Bank protected (paid to winner as per court order)
- Costs: B or C (whoever loses)

Bank's Protection:
- Cannot be sued later by loser (court decided)
- Acted bona fide (no costs on bank)
```

## CASE LAW

### 1. Shiv Kumar Chadha v. Municipal Corporation of Delhi (SC 1993)
```
Facts: Temporary injunction; three-fold test application
Held:
- Prima facie case + Balance of convenience + Irreparable injury → ALL three must be satisfied
- If any one fails → Injunction refused
- Courts must balance competing interests (plaintiff vs defendant hardship)
```

### 2. Mechelec Engineers v. Basic Equipment Corporation (SC 1976)
```
Facts: Summary suit; leave to defend
Held:
- Summary suit procedure for speedy disposal (Order XXXVII)
- Defendant must show TRIABLE defense to get leave to defend
- If defense sham/frivolous → Refuse leave → Summary decree
- Burden on defendant to show defense has substance
```

### 3. Dalpat Kumar v. Prahlad Singh (SC 1992)
```
Facts: Receiver appointed; powers and duties
Held:
- Receiver is court's officer (acts impartially)
- Preserves property pending final decision
- Accountable to court (files periodic reports)
- Any misconduct → Removal + liability for damages
```

### 4. Ramesh B. Desai v. Bipin Vadilal Mehta (SC 2006)
```
Facts: Interpleader suit; stakeholder's protection
Held:
- Interpleader suit protects bona fide stakeholder from competing claims
- Stakeholder must have no interest in property (mere holder)
- Trial between claimants (stakeholder generally out after depositing property)
- Costs usually borne by claimants (stakeholder protected if acted bona fide)
```

# Protocol: Summons and Service (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-CPC-SUMMONS-SERVICE  
**Domain**: Civil Procedure  
**Subdomain**: Pleadings and Initiation  
**Version**: 1.0.0  
**Last Updated**: 2025-12-06

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on issuance of summons, modes of service, proof of service, and consequences of non-service under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "How is summons served in civil suits?"
- Defendant not served properly
- Need to effect service on defendant
- Service by publication required
- Defendant evading service
- Proof of service challenged
- Foreign defendant service needed

## Core Principles

### 1. Summons = Court's Command to Appear

```
Summons (Order V):
- Written notice issued by court
- Commands defendant to appear on specified date
- Accompanied by copy of plaint
- Defendant's first formal notice of suit

Rule: "No decree without service" (except ex parte after proper attempts)
→ Valid service prerequisite for binding decree
→ Irregular service → Decree liable to be set aside
```

### 2. Purpose of Service

```
1. Notice to Defendant:
   - Inform defendant of suit against him
   - Opportunity to defend

2. Jurisdiction:
   - Court's jurisdiction invoked by valid service
   - No valid service → No jurisdiction over defendant's person

3. Natural Justice:
   - Audi alteram partem (hear other side)
   - Defendant's right to be heard
```

## SUMMONS (Order V)

### Contents of Summons (Order V Rule 1)

```
Every summons shall:

1. Be signed by judge or presiding officer

2. Bear seal of court

3. Contain:
   (a) Name of court
   (b) Parties' names and descriptions
   (c) Suit number
   (d) Date of appearance
   (e) Time of appearance
   (f) Command to appear (in H.M. President's name if High Court/Supreme Court)

4. Be accompanied by:
   - Copy of plaint
   - Notice to defendant (Order V Rule 5)

5. Notice to defendant stating:
   - Time to file written statement (30 days)
   - Consequence of non-appearance (ex parte decree)
   - That defendant must appear by advocate or in person
```

**Form of Summons** (Appendix B, Form 1):

```
IN THE COURT OF [NAME OF COURT]

SUMMONS FOR APPEARANCE

Suit No. ____ of 20__

To,
[Name and address of defendant]

WHEREAS [Name of plaintiff], the plaintiff above named, has instituted a suit 
against you in this Court for [brief relief], the particulars whereof are shown 
in the annexed copy of plaint:

YOU ARE HEREBY SUMMONED to appear in this Court in person or by a pleader duly 
instructed and able to answer all material questions relating to the suit, on 
the ____ day of ____, 20__ at __ o'clock in the forenoon and to answer the 
claim, and take notice that, in default of your appearance on the said day, the 
suit will be heard and determined in your absence.

Given under my hand and the seal of the Court, this ____ day of ____, 20__.

                                                                 [Signature]
                                                                 Judge/Officer

Seal of Court
```

### When Summons Issued (Order V Rule 1)

```
Timeline:

1. Plaint Filed → Registered as suit → Suit number assigned

2. Court examines plaint:
   - If plaint in order → Issue summons
   - If defective → Order VII Rule 11 (reject) OR give time to correct

3. Summons prepared:
   - Signed by judge
   - Sealed
   - Copy of plaint annexed
   - Notice to defendant (Order V Rule 5) attached

4. Summons issued:
   - Delivered to process server OR registered post
   - Service attempted

Standard Practice:
- Summons issued within 7-15 days of plaint filing (varies by court)
- First date of appearance: Usually 30-60 days from issue (to allow service time)
```

## MODES OF SERVICE (Order V Rules 9-20)

### 1. Personal Service (Order V Rule 9, 10) - PRIMARY MODE

```
Rule 9: "Whenever it is practicable, service shall be made on the defendant in 
person by delivering or tendering to him the summons"

Procedure:

1. Process Server (Court employee):
   - Locates defendant
   - Identifies defendant personally
   - Delivers summons to defendant
   - Obtains signature on duplicate/acknowledgment

2. If Defendant Refuses to Accept:
   Rule 10: If defendant refuses to receive summons or refuses to sign 
   acknowledgment:
   → Affix original summons to outer door of house where defendant ordinarily resides
   → Service deemed effected

3. Proof of Service:
   - Affidavit by process server
   - OR defendant's signature on duplicate summons
   - OR endorsement on summons stating:
     * Date and time of service
     * Manner of service (personal delivery / refusal + affixture)
     * Signature and designation of server
```

**Valid Personal Service Requirements**:
```
1. Delivered to DEFENDANT personally (not family member/servant at this stage)
2. Server identifies defendant correctly
3. Copy of plaint accompanied summons
4. Server's affidavit/endorsement as proof
5. Service within jurisdiction (if outside, commission needed)
```

### 2. Service on Agent/Representative (Order V Rule 13-18)

```
If defendant cannot be personally served → Service on authorized agent

Rule 13: Service on Agent of Defendant

Who qualifies as agent?
- Agent expressly authorized to accept service
- Power of attorney holder (with service acceptance power)
- Manager of defendant's business
- Secretary of company (Order XXIX Rule 2)

Procedure:
- Deliver summons to agent
- Agent's acknowledgment sufficient
- Service deemed as service on defendant
```

### 3. Substituted Service (Order V Rule 20) - If Personal Service Impracticable

```
Rule 20: Where court is satisfied that service CANNOT be made in ordinary way:
→ Court may order service by:
   (a) Affixture on conspicuous part of defendant's house/last known residence
   (b) Publication in newspaper
   (c) Email/SMS to known email/mobile (if court permits)
   (d) Any other mode court deems fit

Conditions for Substituted Service:

1. Personal service attempted multiple times and failed
   - Usually 3-4 attempts required
   - Different times of day
   - Process server's report showing attempts

2. Defendant's whereabouts:
   - Address known but defendant evading service, OR
   - Address known but defendant not available despite attempts, OR
   - Defendant's address unknown (after due inquiry)

3. Court satisfaction:
   - Application by plaintiff with affidavit
   - Process server's report of attempts
   - Court orders substituted service mode

4. Proof:
   - Affidavit of affixture + photograph (if affixture)
   - Newspaper publication copy (if publication)
   - Email delivery report (if email)
```

**Procedure for Obtaining Substituted Service Order**:

```
Step 1: File Application under Order V Rule 20
- Application stating:
  * Personal service attempts made (dates, times)
  * Reason personal service failed
  * Defendant's last known address
  * Proposed mode of substituted service

Step 2: Annex Process Server's Report
- Report showing 3-4 unsuccessful attempts
- Dates and times of attempts
- Reason for failure (defendant not found / evading / address wrong)

Step 3: Court Hearing
- Court may:
  * Order one more attempt at personal service
  * Order substituted service (if satisfied)
  * Specify mode (affixture / publication / email / other)

Step 4: Effect Substituted Service
- As per court order
- Affidavit of compliance filed

Step 5: Service Deemed Complete
- On date specified in court order (usually 7-30 days from affixture/publication)
```

**Service by Publication** (Newspaper):

```
When Ordered:
- Defendant's whereabouts unknown despite diligent inquiry
- Defendant absconding/evading
- Large number of defendants (class suits)

Procedure:
1. Court orders publication in:
   - One English daily (national/state level)
   - One vernacular daily (local language)

2. Advertisement format:
   - Court name, suit number
   - Parties' names
   - Brief nature of claim
   - Command to appear on date
   - Consequence of non-appearance

3. Publication date + notice period:
   - Usually published once or twice (as court directs)
   - Notice period: 30 days from publication (or as court orders)

4. Proof:
   - Original newspaper copy (with publication visible)
   - Affidavit of publication by newspaper

Effect:
- Service deemed effective after notice period
- Defendant deemed to have knowledge of suit
```

### 4. Service by Registered Post (Order V Rule 9A) - ALTERNATIVE MODE

```
Rule 9A: Court may, in addition to personal service, order service by:
- Registered post with acknowledgment due
- Speed post
- Courier

Procedure:
1. Court directs postal service (in addition to or instead of personal service)
2. Copy of summons + plaint sent by registered post to defendant's address
3. Postal acknowledgment obtained
4. Acknowledgment filed in court

Proof of Service:
- Postal acknowledgment card signed by recipient
- OR endorsement by postal authority (if refused/not delivered)

Effect:
- If postal acknowledgment returned signed → Service complete on date of receipt
- If returned unserved (refused/address wrong) → Personal service or substituted service needed
```

### 5. Service on Government (Order XXVII Rule 3, 4)

```
Rule 3: Service on Union of India

Mode:
- Secretary of concerned Central Ministry/Department
- OR authorized officer

Address:
- Ministry's office address (New Delhi)
- Chief Secretary (if state concern)

Procedure:
1. Summons + plaint copy sent by registered post
2. OR delivered through advocate to Secretary's office
3. Acknowledgment obtained

Time to Appear:
- Minimum 60 days (extendable) - Rule 4

Rule 4: Service on State Government

Mode:
- Chief Secretary of State
- OR Secretary of concerned State Department

Time: 60 days minimum
```

### 6. Service on Company (Order XXIX Rule 2)

```
Mode of Service:
- Secretary of company
- OR Director of company
- OR Manager of principal/registered office

Address:
- Registered office (Section 12 Companies Act 2013)
- OR principal place of business

Procedure:
1. Deliver summons to secretary/director/manager
2. Personal service at registered office
3. Affidavit of server

Alternative:
- Registered post to registered office address
```

### 7. Service on Partnership Firm (Order XXX Rule 2)

```
Mode:
- Service on partner personally, OR
- Service at principal place of business of firm

If all partners cannot be served personally:
- Substituted service on firm (affixture at place of business + publication)
```

### 8. Service Outside Jurisdiction (Order V Rule 21-25)

```
Rule 21: Service in Another State (Within India)

Procedure:
1. Letter of Request (Commission):
   - Requesting court sends letter to court in defendant's state
   - Requesting service on defendant

2. Receiving Court:
   - Effects service as if suit in its own court
   - Returns proof of service

Rule 22: Service in Foreign Country

Procedure:
1. Through Central Government (Ministry of External Affairs):
   - Under Hague Service Convention (if applicable)
   - OR through diplomatic channels

2. Direct Service (if country permits):
   - Registered post to defendant's address abroad
   - Courier service

3. Proof:
   - Acknowledgment from foreign authority/defendant
   - Affidavit if served directly

Time:
- Extended time for appearance (usually 90-120 days)
```

## PROOF OF SERVICE (Order V Rule 16-19)

### Modes of Proof

```
1. Acknowledgment by Defendant (Rule 17):
   - Defendant signs duplicate summons
   - OR sends written acknowledgment
   → Best proof

2. Affidavit of Process Server (Rule 16):
   - Server's affidavit stating:
     * Date, time, place of service
     * How served (personal delivery / affixture / publication)
     * Identification of defendant (if personal)
   → Admissible as evidence (but rebuttable)

3. Registered Post Acknowledgment (Rule 19A):
   - Postal acknowledgment card signed by defendant
   → Proof of service

4. Return of Service (Rule 16):
   - Server's endorsement on summons
   - Stating manner and date of service
   → Court record
```

### Burden of Proof

```
Plaintiff's Duty:
- Prove service on defendant before obtaining ex parte decree
- File affidavit/acknowledgment showing service

Court's Duty:
- Satisfy itself that service effected before proceeding ex parte
- May order fresh service if proof inadequate

Defendant's Challenge:
- Can challenge service by showing:
  * No service received
  * Service not in accordance with CPC
  * Summons not accompanied by plaint copy
```

## CONSEQUENCES OF SERVICE/NON-SERVICE

### If Defendant Served and Appears (Order VIII)

```
Defendant's Options:

1. File Written Statement (within 30 days, extendable up to 90 days)
   - Admit/deny allegations
   - Raise defenses
   - Counterclaim/set-off

2. Appear on Hearing Date:
   - Through advocate or in person
   - Participate in proceedings

3. Challenge Jurisdiction:
   - File application under Order VII Rule 10
   - OR raise in written statement
```

### If Defendant Served but Does Not Appear (Order IX Rule 6)

```
Rule 6: Ex Parte Decree

If on hearing date defendant does not appear AND has been duly served:

Procedure:
1. Plaintiff proves service (affidavit + acknowledgment/postal receipt)
2. Court satisfied service effected
3. Plaintiff proves claim (evidence):
   - Examines witness(es)
   - Produces documents
4. Court passes ex parte decree if claim proved

Effect:
- Decree binding on defendant
- Defendant can apply to set aside (Rule 13) within 30 days if:
  * Summons not duly served, OR
  * Prevented by sufficient cause from appearing
```

### If Defendant Not Served (Order V Rule 12)

```
Rule 12: Proof of Service Required Before Ex Parte Decree

If defendant not served:
→ Court CANNOT pass ex parte decree
→ Must order fresh service OR substituted service

Plaintiff's options:
1. Attempt fresh service (personal/postal)
2. Apply for substituted service (Order V Rule 20)
3. If all modes exhausted and defendant not traceable → Suit may be dismissed for non-prosecution
```

## Setting Aside Ex Parte Decree (Order IX Rule 13)

```
Rule 13: Application to Set Aside Ex Parte Decree

Grounds:

1. Summons Not Duly Served:
   - Service not in accordance with CPC
   - No service at all
   - Summons not accompanied by plaint copy

2. Sufficient Cause Prevented Appearance:
   - Illness, accident, unavoidable circumstances
   - Court error in date/time

Time Limit: 30 days from knowledge of ex parte decree

Procedure:
1. File application with affidavit stating:
   - Grounds (non-service OR sufficient cause)
   - Defense on merits (not frivolous)

2. Court may:
   (a) Set aside ex parte decree + restore suit
   (b) Impose costs on defendant (for delay/non-appearance)
   (c) Dismiss application if no good cause shown

Effect of Setting Aside:
- Suit restored to board
- Defendant files written statement
- Trial proceeds as if ex parte not passed
```

## Common Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Defendant Evading Service

```
Facts:
- Process server visited defendant's residence 3 times
- Neighbors confirm defendant lives there
- Defendant not available each time (appears to be evading)

Solution:
Step 1: File application under Order V Rule 20 for substituted service
Step 2: Annex process server's report (3 unsuccessful attempts)
Step 3: Court orders substituted service by:
   - Affixture on outer door of residence
   - Publication in newspaper (if court deems fit)
Step 4: Affidavit of affixture filed (with photograph)
Step 5: Service deemed complete 7-30 days from affixture (as per court order)
```

### Scenario 2: Defendant's Address Unknown

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff knows defendant's name but not current address
- Last known address: Defendant vacated 2 years ago

Solution:
Step 1: Conduct due inquiry:
   - Neighbors at last known address
   - Mutual acquaintances
   - Public records (Aadhaar, PAN, voter ID - if accessible)

Step 2: File application for substituted service under Order V Rule 20:
   - State inquiry efforts made
   - Request service by publication in newspaper

Step 3: Court orders publication in:
   - English daily (national)
   - Vernacular daily (local)

Step 4: Advertisement published with suit details + appearance date

Step 5: Service deemed complete 30 days from publication
```

### Scenario 3: Service on Company

```
Facts:
- Defendant: ABC Private Limited
- Registered office: Mumbai
- No branch in Delhi (where suit filed)

Solution:
Mode of Service: Order XXIX Rule 2

Option 1: Personal Service at Registered Office
- Process server goes to Mumbai registered office
- Serves secretary/director/manager
- Obtains acknowledgment

Option 2: Registered Post
- Court sends summons by registered post to:
  Address: The Secretary, ABC Private Limited, [Registered Office Address], Mumbai
- Postal acknowledgment obtained

Option 3: Letter of Request (Commission)
- Delhi court sends letter of request to Mumbai court
- Mumbai court effects service
- Returns proof to Delhi court

Time: Company gets standard 30 days to file written statement
```

### Scenario 4: Service on Government

```
Facts:
- Suit against Union of India
- Cause of action: Contractual dispute with BSNL (Central PSU)

Solution:
Mode: Order XXVII Rule 3

Service On:
- Secretary, Department of Telecommunications, Government of India
- Address: Sanchar Bhawan, New Delhi

Procedure:
1. Summons + plaint copy sent by registered post
2. OR delivered through advocate to Secretary's office
3. Acknowledgment obtained

Time to Appear:
- Minimum 60 days from service (Rule 4)
- Can be extended on application by government

Note: Section 80 CPC notice (2 months prior) MANDATORY before suing government
```

### Scenario 5: Foreign Defendant

```
Facts:
- Defendant: UK resident
- Contract executed in India
- Defendant now in London

Solution:
Mode: Order V Rule 22

Option 1: Hague Service Convention (India and UK both signatories)
1. Court forwards summons to Central Authority (Ministry of External Affairs)
2. MEA sends to UK Central Authority
3. UK authority effects service
4. Proof of service returned through diplomatic channels

Option 2: Direct Service (if UK law permits)
1. Summons sent by registered post/courier to defendant's UK address
2. Acknowledgment obtained

Time: Extended time for appearance (90-120 days usual)

Jurisdiction Note:
- Ensure court has territorial jurisdiction (cause of action arose in India / defendant carries business in India / jurisdiction clause)
```

### Scenario 6: Service by Email/SMS

```
Facts:
- Personal service failed despite 4 attempts
- Defendant's email ID and mobile number known (from contract documents)

Solution:
Application under Order V Rule 20:
- Request substituted service by email + SMS
- Some courts permitting this mode (modern practice)

Procedure:
1. Court orders service by email to [defendant's email] + SMS to [defendant's mobile]
2. Summons + plaint copy sent via email (with read receipt)
3. SMS notification sent
4. Email delivery report + screenshot filed as proof

Service deemed complete: 7-15 days from email/SMS (as per court order)

Note: Not universally accepted; some courts prefer traditional modes (affixture + publication)
```

## Common Defects in Service

```
Defect 1: Summons Not Accompanied by Plaint Copy
Effect: Service invalid (Order V Rule 1 requirement)
Remedy: Fresh service with plaint copy

Defect 2: Service on Family Member Instead of Defendant
Effect: Not valid personal service (unless family member is authorized agent)
Remedy: Fresh personal service OR service on agent OR substituted service

Defect 3: No Proof of Service Filed
Effect: Court cannot proceed ex parte
Remedy: File affidavit/acknowledgment proving service

Defect 4: Substituted Service Without Court Order
Effect: Irregular service; decree liable to be set aside
Remedy: Obtain court order for substituted service before effecting

Defect 5: Insufficient Attempts Before Substituted Service
Effect: Court may refuse substituted service order
Remedy: Make 3-4 attempts at different times before applying for substituted service

Defect 6: Service Outside Jurisdiction Without Commission
Effect: Service invalid
Remedy: Obtain letter of request (commission) from court for service outside jurisdiction
```

## Case Law

### 1. Karam Chand Thapar v. CIT (SC 1969)
```
Facts: Service by affixture without prior attempts at personal service
Held:
- Substituted service is LAST RESORT
- Personal service must be attempted first (multiple times)
- Court must be satisfied personal service impracticable
- Substituted service without genuine attempts → Invalid
```

### 2. Umeshchandra Banerji v. Kunjilal Biswas (SC 1959)
```
Facts: Summons served on defendant's son (adult) at residence
Held:
- Service on family member NOT valid personal service (unless authorized agent)
- Order V Rule 9: Service shall be on defendant IN PERSON
- If defendant refuses, then affix on outer door
- Service on family member without defendant's refusal → Invalid
```

### 3. Pana Lal v. State of Rajasthan (SC 1975)
```
Facts: Substituted service by affixture on outer door without attempts at personal service
Held:
- Order V Rule 20: Court must be satisfied personal service impracticable
- "Impracticable" = Cannot be done after reasonable efforts
- Affixture without attempts → Irregular service
- Decree based on such service → Liable to be set aside
```

### 4. Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (SC 1979)
```
Facts: Service on defendant through registered post; acknowledgment not returned
Held:
- Registered post service (Order V Rule 19A) valid mode
- But proof of service required (postal acknowledgment)
- If acknowledgment not returned → Service not proved
- Cannot pass ex parte decree without proof
```

### 5. Roshan Lal Sood v. Rajiv Suri (SC 2016)
```
Facts: Service by courier; proof of service challenged
Held:
- Courier service permissible if court so orders (Order V Rule 20)
- Proof of service: Delivery report + signature of recipient
- If proper proof → Service valid
- Modern modes (email, courier) acceptable with court permission
```

### 6. Samir Narain Bhojwani v. Aurora Properties (SC 2018)
```
Facts: Ex parte decree set aside; defendant claimed no service
Held:
- Order IX Rule 13: Defendant can challenge service within 30 days
- Burden on plaintiff to prove service (affidavit + acknowledgment)
- If defendant shows no service received → Court may set aside ex parte decree
- Natural justice requires actual notice to defendant
```

# Protocol: Trial Procedure (India)

**Protocol ID**: IL-CPC-TRIAL-PROCEDURE  
**Domain**: Civil Procedure  
**Subdomain**: Trial and Judgment

## Purpose

This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on civil trial procedure in Indian courts, from framing of issues through judgment, covering the complete trial workflow under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908.

## Activation Triggers

- User asks "What is the procedure for civil trial?"
- Need to understand trial stages
- Framing of issues required
- Order of evidence questions
- Arguments and final hearing procedures
- Trial timeline and stages

## Core Principles

### 1. Trial Objective

```
Objective: Adjudicate dispute based on:
- Pleadings (plaint + written statement)
- Evidence (oral + documentary)
- Arguments (legal submissions)
- Law applicable

Result: Judgment and decree
```

### 2. Trial Stages Overview

```
STAGE 1: PLEADINGS (Order VI-VIII)
→ Plaint filed
→ Summons issued and served
→ Written statement filed
→ Replication (if needed)

STAGE 2: FRAMING OF ISSUES (Order XIV)
→ Court identifies disputed questions of fact and law
→ Issues framed
→ Parties' agreement/objections

STAGE 3: EVIDENCE (Order XVIII)
→ Plaintiff's evidence (examination-in-chief, cross, re-examination)
→ Defendant's evidence (examination-in-chief, cross, re-examination)
→ Rebuttal evidence (if needed)

STAGE 4: ARGUMENTS (Order XVIII Rule 2)
→ Final arguments by both parties
→ Written submissions (if court directs)

STAGE 5: JUDGMENT (Order XX)
→ Court decides issues
→ Reasons given
→ Judgment pronounced

STAGE 6: DECREE (Order XX Rule 6)
→ Formal adjudication
→ Decree drawn up and signed
```

## FRAMING OF ISSUES (Order XIV)

### Purpose of Issues (Order XIV Rule 1)

```
Rule 1: "Issues arise when material proposition of fact or law is affirmed by 
one party and denied by other"

Issue = Disputed question to be decided by court

Types:

1. Issues of Fact:
   - Disputed factual questions
   - Example: "Did defendant pay Rs. 5 lakhs to plaintiff on 15/01/2024?"

2. Issues of Law:
   - Disputed legal questions
   - Example: "Whether suit is barred by limitation?"

3. Mixed Issues (Fact + Law):
   - Example: "Whether defendant executed contract under coercion?"
     (Fact: Was there threat? Law: Does it amount to coercion under Section 15?)
```

### Procedure for Framing Issues (Order XIV Rule 1-5)

```
Step 1: Court Examines Pleadings
- Reads plaint + written statement + replication (if any)
- Identifies material propositions affirmed by one party and denied by other

Step 2: Court Frames Issues
- Formulates questions (disputed points)
- Arranges in logical order

Step 3: Parties' Input (Rule 2)
- Court may hear parties on issues to be framed
- Parties may suggest additional issues
- Court decides final issues

Step 4: Issues Recorded (Rule 1)
- Framed issues entered in case record
- Numbered sequentially (Issue No. 1, 2, 3...)

Step 5: Amendment (Rule 5)
- Court may amend/add issues at any stage if necessary for determining real controversy
```

### Formulation of Issues

```
Issues should be:
- SPECIFIC (not vague or general)
- CONCISE (not lengthy or argumentative)
- DISPUTED (not admitted facts)
- MATERIAL (relevant to decision)

Format:

Issue No. 1: "Whether the plaintiff proves that defendant borrowed Rs. 10 lakhs 
from plaintiff on 15/01/2024?"

Issue No. 2: "Whether defendant proves payment of Rs. 10 lakhs to plaintiff?"

Issue No. 3: "Whether suit is barred by limitation?"

Issue No. 4: "Whether plaintiff is entitled to interest and if so at what rate?"

Issue No. 5: "To what relief, if any, is plaintiff entitled?"

Final Issue (Standard):
"To what relief?" → Determines remedy based on findings on earlier issues
```

### Striking Out Issues (Order XIV Rule 3)

```
Rule 3: Court may strike out issues which:
- Appear not to require determination (admitted facts)
- Not material to decision
- Cannot be tried for legal reasons

Example:
Plaintiff claims: "Defendant owes Rs. 10 lakhs + Rs. 5 lakhs interest"
Defendant admits: "Rs. 10 lakhs owed, but denies interest"

Issues Framed:
1. Whether defendant liable to pay Rs. 10 lakhs? → STRUCK OUT (admitted)
2. Whether defendant liable to pay interest? → RETAINED (disputed)
3. To what relief?
```

## ORDER OF EVIDENCE (Order XVIII)

### General Principle (Order XVIII Rule 1)

```
Rule 1: Right to begin

Party bearing burden of proof has right to begin evidence

Usual Order:
1. Plaintiff begins (proves claim)
2. Defendant follows (proves defense)
3. Plaintiff's rebuttal (if defendant raised new matter)

Exception (Rule 1 proviso):
If defendant admits plaintiff's case substantially but sets up affirmative defense:
→ Defendant may begin (prove defense)
→ Then plaintiff rebuts

Example:
Plaint: "Defendant owes Rs. 10 lakhs"
Written Statement: "Defendant admits owing Rs. 10 lakhs BUT has already paid it"

Here: Defendant begins (proves payment), plaintiff rebuts (shows no payment received)
```

### Plaintiff's Evidence (Order XVIII Rule 4)

```
Procedure:

1. Plaintiff Calls Witnesses:
   - Plaintiff witness (PW) 1, PW2, PW3...
   - Each examined-in-chief, cross-examined, re-examined

2. Plaintiff Produces Documents:
   - Mark documents as Exhibit P-1, P-2, P-3...
   - Prove through witnesses

3. Plaintiff Closes Evidence:
   - After all witnesses examined
   - "Plaintiff's evidence closed"

Content to Cover:
- Prove all material facts alleged in plaint
- Prove cause of action
- Prove documents relied upon
- Anticipate and disprove defendant's defenses (if known)
```

### Defendant's Evidence (Order XVIII Rule 4)

```
Procedure (same as plaintiff):

1. Defendant Calls Witnesses:
   - Defendant witness (DW) 1, DW2, DW3...
   - Each examined-in-chief, cross-examined, re-examined

2. Defendant Produces Documents:
   - Mark as Exhibit D-1, D-2, D-3...
   - Prove through witnesses

3. Defendant Closes Evidence:
   - After all witnesses examined
   - "Defendant's evidence closed"

Content to Cover:
- Disprove plaintiff's case (if denying)
- Prove affirmative defenses (payment, limitation, res judicata, etc.)
- Prove counterclaim (if raised)
```

### Rebuttal Evidence (Order XVIII)

```
Plaintiff's Rebuttal (Sur-Rejoinder):
If defendant raises NEW MATTER in evidence (not in written statement):
→ Plaintiff may lead rebuttal evidence to disprove new matter

Court's Permission Required:
- Not automatic right
- Court decides if new matter arose
- Court allows rebuttal if in interest of justice

Example:
Defendant's Defense (in written statement): "Contract void for uncertainty"
Defendant's Evidence: "Contract also procured by fraud" (NEW MATTER not pleaded)
Plaintiff's Rebuttal: Evidence to show no fraud

Defendant's Rebuttal (Rare):
If plaintiff leads rebuttal on new matter beyond scope
→ Court may allow defendant's sur-rejoinder
```

### Court's Power to Examine Witnesses (Order XVIII Rule 17, Section 165 Evidence Act)

```
Section 165: Court may examine witness at any stage

Court's Examination (Suo Motu):
- To clear doubt on any point
- To elicit facts not brought out by parties
- In interest of justice

When Exercised:
- During witness examination (to clarify ambiguous answer)
- After parties' examination complete (if gap in evidence)
- To recall witness

Effect:
- Court's questions may help one party or other
- Impartiality maintained (court seeking truth, not favoring party)
```

## FINAL ARGUMENTS (Order XVIII Rule 2)

```
Rule 2: After evidence closed, parties address court

Order of Arguments:

1. Plaintiff's Arguments (First):
   - Summarizes evidence
   - Refers to law and precedents
   - Submits plaintiff proved case on preponderance of probabilities
   - Prays for relief

2. Defendant's Arguments (Response):
   - Counters plaintiff's submissions
   - Summarizes defendant's evidence
   - Refers to law and precedents
   - Submits plaintiff failed to prove case OR defendant proved defenses
   - Prays for dismissal (or decree on counterclaim if applicable)

3. Plaintiff's Reply (Brief):
   - Responds to defendant's submissions (limited to reply, not new arguments)

Written Submissions:
- Court may direct parties to file written arguments
- Time limit given (usually 7-15 days)
- Oral arguments limited to highlighting written submissions

Mode:
- Oral arguments (traditional)
- Written submissions (modern practice to save time)
- Hybrid: Written + brief oral highlighting
```

### Content of Arguments

```
Effective Final Argument Covers:

1. Facts:
   - Summary of undisputed facts
   - Disputed facts and evidence led
   - Which version should be believed (credibility assessment)

2. Law:
   - Applicable statutory provisions
   - Case law (precedents) supporting party's position
   - Distinguish opposite party's precedents

3. Issues:
   - Address each framed issue
   - Show how evidence proves/disproves issue
   - Burden of proof discharged/not discharged

4. Relief:
   - Specific relief claimed
   - Why entitled based on facts + law

Structure:
I. Facts (undisputed + disputed with evidence)
II. Law (statutes + case law)
III. Application (facts to law)
IV. Issues (findings on each issue)
V. Relief (prayer)

Length:
- Concise (court time limited)
- Focused on material points (not every detail)
- Respectful to court and opposite party
```

## JUDGMENT (Order XX)

### Judgment Preparation (Order XX Rule 1-5)

```
Rule 1: Court shall pronounce judgment in open court

Timeline:
- Within 30 days from final hearing (if possible)
- If reserved (not immediate), pronounced on date fixed (parties informed)

Judgment Contents (Rule 4):

MANDATORY:

1. Concise Statement of Case:
   - Parties
   - Plaintiff's claim
   - Defendant's defense

2. Points for Determination (Issues Framed):
   - List of issues

3. Decision on Each Issue with Reasons:
   - Issue No. 1: [Decision] - Reasons: [...]
   - Issue No. 2: [Decision] - Reasons: [...]
   - ...

4. Final Order (Conclusion):
   - Suit decreed/dismissed
   - Relief granted (or denied)
   - Costs

Optional but Recommended:
- Summary of evidence (of each witness)
- Analysis of documents
- Reasoning (detailed discussion of law and facts)

Signature and Date:
- Judge signs judgment
- Date of pronouncement
```

### Judgment Format (Order XX Rule 4)

```
Typical Structure:

1. HEADING:
   Court name, suit number, parties

2. INTRODUCTION:
   Brief background and claim

3. PLEADINGS SUMMARY:
   Plaintiff's case (from plaint)
   Defendant's case (from written statement)

4. ISSUES FRAMED:
   List of issues for determination

5. EVIDENCE SUMMARY:
   Plaintiff's evidence (witness-wise + documents)
   Defendant's evidence (witness-wise + documents)

6. FINDINGS ON ISSUES:
   Issue No. 1: [Findings with reasons]
   Issue No. 2: [Findings with reasons]
   ...

7. CONCLUSION:
   Final decision (suit decreed/dismissed)

8. DECREE:
   Specific relief granted/denied
   Costs

9. SIGNATURE AND DATE:
   Judge's signature
   Date of pronouncement
```

**Sample Judgment Excerpt**:

```
IN THE COURT OF CIVIL JUDGE (SENIOR DIVISION), DELHI

Suit No. 123/2023

Ramesh Kumar ............. Plaintiff
Versus
Suresh Enterprises Pvt. Ltd. ............. Defendant

JUDGMENT

This is a suit for recovery of Rs. 10,00,000/- (Rupees Ten Lakhs only) being 
the amount allegedly owed by the defendant to the plaintiff pursuant to a 
contract dated 15/01/2024.

PLAINTIFF'S CASE:
The plaintiff claims that he entered into a contract with the defendant for 
supply of 1000 widgets at Rs. 10,000/- each, total Rs. 1,00,00,000/-. Plaintiff 
supplied the widgets but defendant failed to pay...

DEFENDANT'S CASE:
The defendant admits the contract but contends that payment of Rs. 10,00,000/- 
was made vide cheque no. 123456 dated 20/03/2024, which was encashed by the 
plaintiff...

ISSUES:
1. Whether plaintiff proves that defendant owes Rs. 10,00,000/-?
2. Whether defendant proves payment?
3. To what relief is plaintiff entitled?

EVIDENCE:
Plaintiff examined 2 witnesses (PW1 - plaintiff himself, PW2 - delivery person).
Plaintiff produced 3 documents (Exhibit P-1: Contract, P-2: Delivery receipts, 
P-3: Demand notice).

Defendant examined 1 witness (DW1 - defendant's accountant).
Defendant produced 2 documents (Exhibit D-1: Cheque copy, D-2: Bank statement).

FINDINGS:

Issue No. 1: Whether plaintiff proves that defendant owes Rs. 10,00,000/-?

[Analysis of plaintiff's evidence, contract, delivery receipts...]
FINDING: Answered in the AFFIRMATIVE. Plaintiff has proved delivery of goods 
worth Rs. 10,00,000/-.

Issue No. 2: Whether defendant proves payment?

[Analysis of defendant's evidence, cheque, bank statement...]
[Finding that cheque was for different transaction, not this contract]
FINDING: Answered in the NEGATIVE. Defendant has failed to prove payment of 
Rs. 10,00,000/-.

Issue No. 3: To what relief is plaintiff entitled?

In view of my findings on Issue No. 1 and 2, plaintiff is entitled to recovery 
of Rs. 10,00,000/- with interest @ 9% per annum from date of suit till realization.

CONCLUSION:
The suit is DECREED in favor of the plaintiff and against the defendant.

DECREE:
The defendant is directed to pay Rs. 10,00,000/- (Rupees Ten Lakhs) to the 
plaintiff with interest @ 9% per annum from 01/03/2023 (date of suit) till 
realization.

The defendant shall pay costs of the suit to the plaintiff.

Announced in open court.

Date: 15/11/2024                                           [Signature]
                                                    Civil Judge (Sr. Div.)
```

### Judgment Pronouncement (Order XX Rule 1-3)

```
Rule 1: Judgment Pronounced in Open Court

Modes:

1. Immediate Pronouncement:
   - After final arguments complete
   - Judge delivers judgment orally
   - Judgment typed/written same day or within few days

2. Reserved Judgment:
   - Judge reserves judgment for detailed consideration
   - Date of pronouncement fixed (usually within 30 days)
   - Parties informed of date
   - On pronouncement date, judgment read in court

Presence of Parties:
- Parties/advocates informed of pronouncement date
- Presence not mandatory (judgment pronounced whether parties present or not)
- Copy of judgment given to parties (on application)

Operative Date:
- Judgment takes effect from date of pronouncement
- Limitation for appeal runs from pronouncement date
```

## DECREE (Order XX Rule 6-7)

```
Rule 6: Decree to Follow Judgment

Decree = Formal expression of adjudication

Difference: Judgment vs Decree:
- Judgment: Reasons, findings, decision (narrative)
- Decree: Formal order, executable (operational)

Decree Contents (Rule 6):

1. Number of suit
2. Names of parties (with full description)
3. Particulars of claim
4. Clear statement of relief granted/refused
5. Costs
6. Date of pronouncement
7. Signature of judge

Decree must be:
- Drawn up within 15 days of judgment (Rule 7 - if practicable)
- Signed by judge
- Sealed
```

### Types of Decrees

```
1. Preliminary Decree (Rule 2):
   - Declares rights but does not finally dispose suit
   - Example: Partition suit - preliminary decree declares shares; final decree 
     after actual partition

2. Final Decree (Rule 2):
   - Finally disposes suit (nothing remains except execution)

3. Partly Preliminary, Partly Final (Rule 2):
   - Example: Suit for account - decree for account (preliminary); after account, 
     decree for balance (final)

4. Consent Decree (Order XXIII Rule 3):
   - Based on compromise between parties
   - Court records compromise and passes decree accordingly

5. Ex Parte Decree (Order IX Rule 6):
   - When defendant does not appear despite service
   - Plaintiff proves claim
   - Decree passed in defendant's absence

6. Decree on Admission (Order XII):
   - When defendant admits plaintiff's claim
   - Court passes decree without trial
```

## COSTS (Section 35, Order XX Rule 2)

```
Section 35: Court's Discretion on Costs

General Rule:
"Costs follow the event" → Winning party entitled to costs from losing party

Court's Discretion:
- Award costs to successful party
- Deny costs (even to successful party)
- Apportion costs (if partial success)
- Order one party to pay costs (regardless of result)

Types of Costs:

1. Court Fees Paid:
   - Amount plaintiff paid as court fees

2. Advocate Fees:
   - Reasonable fee for advocate services
   - NOT actual fee (court assesses reasonable amount)

3. Witness Expenses:
   - Batta paid to witnesses

4. Other Expenses:
   - Process fees, typing, photocopying, etc.

Assessment:
- Summary assessment (court estimates reasonable amount)
- OR detailed assessment (advocate files bill of costs, court scrutinizes)

Denial of Costs:
- Vexatious litigation
- Unreasonable conduct by successful party
- Trivial/technical success (no real relief)

Costs in Defendant's Favor (if suit dismissed):
- Defendant entitled to costs for defending frivolous suit
- Compensation for time and expense
```

## CASE MANAGEMENT AND TIMELINE

### Timeline Targets (CPC Amendment 1999, 2002 - Commercial Courts Act 2015)

```
Commercial Courts Act 2015 (for commercial suits):
- Pleadings: 120 days from summons service
- Trial: Commence within 6 months
- Judgment: Within 18 months from plaint filing

General Civil Suits (Non-Commercial):
- No strict statutory timeline (but court aims for disposal within 1-3 years)

Order VIII Rule 1: Written statement within 90 days (MANDATORY)
Order XVII Rule 1: Day-to-day trial (in practice, dates given with adjournments)
Order XX Rule 1: Judgment within 30 days of final hearing (if practicable)
```

### Adjournments (Order XVII)

```
Rule 1: Court may adjourn hearing

Grounds for Adjournment:
- Illness of party/advocate
- Non-service of summons/documents
- Non-availability of witness (valid reason)
- Settlement negotiations

Restrictions:
- Not granted as matter of course
- Must show sufficient cause
- Costs may be imposed for adjournment
- Repeated adjournments discouraged

Commercial Courts:
- Maximum 3 adjournments to each party (Rule 1 proviso - Commercial Courts Act)
- Strict adherence to timelines
```

## Common Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Framing Issues - Contract Dispute

```
Plaint Claims:
- Contract dated 15/01/2024 for supply of goods
- Plaintiff supplied goods worth Rs. 10 lakhs
- Defendant failed to pay

Written Statement:
- Admits contract and supply
- Claims payment made vide cheque on 20/03/2024
- Alternatively, suit barred by limitation

Issues Framed:

Issue No. 1: Whether plaintiff proves that defendant owes Rs. 10 lakhs?
(Fact: Did defendant pay or not?)

Issue No. 2: Whether suit is barred by limitation?
(Law: Limitation period applicable and whether expired)

Issue No. 3: To what relief is plaintiff entitled?
(Final issue - depends on findings on Issues 1 and 2)
```

### Scenario 2: Order of Evidence - Affirmative Defense

```
Plaint: Plaintiff sues for Rs. 10 lakhs (contract breach)
Written Statement: Defendant admits contract BUT claims suit barred by limitation

Order of Evidence (Order XVIII Rule 1 proviso):
1. Defendant begins (proves limitation defense)
   - Limitation is affirmative defense
   - Defendant bears burden (Section 106 Evidence Act)
2. Plaintiff rebuts (shows suit within limitation OR Section 5 condonation)

Contrast with Normal:
If defendant simply denied liability (no affirmative defense):
→ Plaintiff would begin (prove contract + breach + damages)
→ Defendant would rebut (deny contract/breach)
```

### Scenario 3: Rebuttal Evidence

```
Facts:
- Plaintiff sues for breach of contract
- Defendant's Written Statement: "No breach; plaintiff failed to perform"
- Defendant's Evidence: "Plaintiff also committed fraud" (NEW - not in written statement)

Plaintiff's Application:
- Request permission for rebuttal evidence
- Show fraud allegation is new matter

Court's Decision:
- Allows rebuttal (in interest of justice)
- Plaintiff leads evidence to disprove fraud

Effect:
- If new matter not allowed to be rebutted → Prejudice to plaintiff
- Rebuttal ensures fair trial
```

### Scenario 4: Judgment on Admitted Facts

```
Plaint:
- Plaintiff lent Rs. 10 lakhs to defendant
- Demand made; no payment

Written Statement:
- Admits Rs. 10 lakhs borrowed
- Admits no payment yet
- Requests time to pay (financial hardship)

Issues Framed:
Issue No. 1: Whether defendant admits owing Rs. 10 lakhs? → YES (admitted)
Issue No. 2: To what relief?

Evidence:
- No evidence needed on Issue 1 (admitted)
- Plaintiff may still lead minimal evidence for formality

Judgment:
- Suit decreed for Rs. 10 lakhs
- Court may give time to pay (installments) based on defendant's financial status
```

## Case Law

### 1. Sangram Singh v. Election Tribunal (SC 1955)
```
Facts: Issues framed; whether issues binding
Held:
- Issues framed are not conclusive or binding
- Court may add/amend issues at any stage if necessary for determining real controversy
- Issues are guide for trial, not straitjacket
```

### 2. Roop Kumar v. Mohan Thedani (SC 2003)
```
Facts: Suit dismissed for non-prosecution (plaintiff failed to lead evidence on dates)
Held:
- Plaintiff must prosecute suit diligently
- Repeated adjournments without valid cause → Dismissal for default (Order XVII Rule 2)
- Costs imposed on plaintiff for wasting court time
```

### 3. Kaushalya Devi Massand v. Roopkishore Khore (SC 1990)
```
Facts: Decree passed without hearing defendant's evidence
Held:
- Both parties must be given opportunity to lead evidence (natural justice)
- Decree passed without hearing defendant's evidence → Violation of Order XVIII
- Decree set aside, matter remanded for fresh trial
```

### 4. Ajit Singh v. Gopal Singh (SC 1993)
```
Facts: Judgment did not give reasons for findings on issues
Held:
- Order XX Rule 4: Judgment MUST contain reasons for decision on each issue
- Judgment without reasons → Invalid
- Remanded for fresh judgment with reasons
```


## Common Mistakes

```
Mistake 1: Not Addressing All Framed Issues in Judgment
❌ Judgment addresses Issue 1 and 3, but skips Issue 2
✓ Judgment must contain finding on EACH framed issue (Order XX Rule 4)

Mistake 2: Leading Evidence on Facts Not Pleaded
❌ Plaint does not allege fraud; plaintiff leads evidence on fraud at trial
✓ Only facts pleaded can be proved (unless amendment allowed)

Mistake 3: Not Closing Evidence Formally
❌ After last witness, move to arguments without formal closure
✓ "Plaintiff's/Defendant's evidence closed" → Prevents later addition of witnesses

Mistake 4: Final Arguments Introducing New Facts/Documents
❌ In arguments, refer to document not marked as exhibit or fact not in evidence
✓ Arguments based ONLY on pleadings + evidence on record

Mistake 5: Decree Not Matching Judgment
❌ Judgment decrees suit for Rs. 10 lakhs; decree drawn for Rs. 8 lakhs
✓ Decree must exactly reflect judgment's conclusion

Mistake 6: Not Recording Reasons for Findings
❌ Judgment says "Issue 1 answered in affirmative" without reasons
✓ MUST give reasons for each finding (Order XX Rule 4)

Mistake 7: Not Giving Opportunity to Both Parties
❌ Court allows plaintiff's evidence but refuses to hear defendant's evidence
✓ Both parties must get fair opportunity (natural justice)
```
