# Indian Intellectual Property Law: Copyright Protection

## Purpose
This protocol provides comprehensive guidance on the Copyright Act 1957 and Copyright Rules 2013, covering copyrightable works, ownership, duration, moral rights, exceptions and limitations (fair dealing), assignment and licensing, infringement remedies, digital copyright issues (ISP liability, technological protection measures), and practical considerations for copyright protection and enforcement in India.

## Activation Triggers
Use this protocol when:
- Client wants to protect original literary, artistic, musical, or cinematographic work
- Copyright infringement dispute (unauthorized copying, reproduction, distribution)
- Copyright ownership determination (work for hire, joint authorship)
- Assignment or exclusive license of copyright
- Fair dealing/fair use defense analysis
- Moral rights violation (attribution, integrity)
- Digital copyright issues (online piracy, ISP liability, takedown notices)
- Duration of copyright calculation
- Copyright registration application
- International copyright protection (Berne Convention, TRIPS)
- Software copyright protection
- Database copyright protection
- Compulsory licensing under Section 31, 31A, 31D

## Core Principles

### 1. Section 13: What is Protected by Copyright

**"Copyright Subsists in Original Literary, Dramatic, Musical and Artistic Works, Cinematograph Films and Sound Recordings"**

**Categories of Copyrightable Works (Section 13):**

**A. Literary Works (Section 2(o)):**
- Books, pamphlets, poems, articles
- Computer programs (explicitly included)
- Databases (if original selection/arrangement)
- Lyrics, scripts
- Compilation (if original)

**NOT Literary Works:**
- Ideas, concepts, themes (only expression protected)
- Facts (but arrangement may be)
- Titles (usually too short)

**B. Dramatic Works (Section 2(h)):**
- Plays, scripts for films/television
- Choreographic works (if fixed in writing/notation)
- Pantomimes

**C. Musical Works (Section 2(p)):**
- Musical compositions (melody, harmony)
- Songs (music only, not lyrics - lyrics = literary work)

**D. Artistic Works (Section 2(c)):**
- Paintings, drawings, sculptures
- Photographs
- Architectural works (buildings, models)
- Works of artistic craftsmanship

**E. Cinematograph Films (Section 2(f)):**
- Movies, films, video recordings
- Any visual recording (including documentaries, advertisements)

**F. Sound Recordings (Section 2(xx)):**
- Audio recordings (music, speech, sounds)
- Distinct from musical/literary work recorded

**Example:**
- Song has 3 copyrights:
  1. Lyrics (literary work - lyricist owns)
  2. Music (musical work - composer owns)
  3. Sound recording (sound recording - producer owns)

### 2. Section 13: Requirement of ORIGINALITY

**"Copyright Subsists ONLY in ORIGINAL Works"**

**Originality Test (Indian Law):**

**NOT Novelty/Uniqueness Required:**
- Work need not be novel or unique
- Multiple people can create similar works independently

**Sufficient Originality:**
- Work originated from author (not copied)
- Minimum degree of skill, judgment, labor
- Author's own intellectual creation

**Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak (SC 2008):**
- "Originality in copyright refers to original expression of thought"
- Minimum creativity required (not mere mechanical work)
- Sweat of brow NOT sufficient (departed from earlier law)

**Example (Original):**
- Photograph of common scene (Taj Mahal) → Original (photographer's skill, angle, lighting)
- Compilation of data (if selection/arrangement shows judgment) → Original

**Example (NOT Original):**
- Exact copy of existing work → Not original
- Trivial alteration → Not original (derivative, needs permission)
- Mere mechanical reproduction (photocopy) → Not original

### 3. Section 14: Meaning of Copyright (Exclusive Rights)

**Copyright = Bundle of Exclusive Rights**

**For Literary/Dramatic/Musical/Artistic Works:**

1. **Reproduction** - Right to make copies
2. **Issuance of Copies to Public** - Distribution right
3. **Performance in Public** - Public performance right
4. **Communication to Public** - Broadcast, transmission
5. **Adaptation** - Translation, conversion, modification
6. **Commercial Rental** - Rent copies (for computer programs, sound recordings)

**For Cinematograph Films:**
- Same as above + Right to make copies of film

**For Sound Recordings:**
- Reproduction, issuance, communication to public, commercial rental

**"Exclusive" Means:**
- ONLY copyright owner can do OR authorize
- Others cannot without permission (license)
- Violation = Infringement

## Ownership of Copyright

### Section 17: First Ownership of Copyright

**General Rule: Author is First Owner**

**Author = Creator of Work**
- Writer (literary work)
- Composer (musical work)
- Artist (artistic work)
- Photographer (photograph)
- Director (cinematograph film)
- Producer (sound recording)

**Exceptions (Section 17):**

**Exception 1: Work for Hire (Section 17(a))**

```
"Where a work is made in the course of employment, the employer is the first 
owner of copyright (in absence of agreement to contrary)"
```

**Conditions:**
1. Work made in course of employment
2. Under contract of service (not independent contractor)

**Example:**
- Software engineer employed by Company A
- Engineer writes software during employment
- Copyright: Company A (employer)

**Freelancer/Independent Contractor:**
- NOT employee
- Freelancer retains copyright (unless assigned)

**Example:**
- Company B hires freelance graphic designer for logo
- No employment relationship
- Copyright: Designer (unless contract assigns to Company)

**Exception 2: Government Work (Section 17(c))**
- Work made by/under direction of Government
- Copyright: Government

**Exception 3: Photographs/Paintings/Portraits Commissioned (Section 17(b), (c))**
- Photograph taken OR painting drawn under commission
- Copyright: Person who commissioned (paid for)

**Exception 4: Journalism (Section 17(b))**
- Article in newspaper/magazine/periodical
- Copyright: Proprietor of publication (absent agreement)
- Author retains right to publish in book form

**Exception 5: Works Made in Course of Employment by Public Undertakings (Section 17(cc))**
- Copyright vests in public undertaking

**Joint Ownership (Section 2(z)):**
- Work produced by collaboration
- Contribution of each author inseparable
- Both/all are joint authors
- Joint ownership (all must consent for exploitation)

## Duration of Copyright

### Section 22-29: Term of Protection

**A. Literary, Dramatic, Musical, Artistic Works (Section 22):**

**Lifetime of Author + 60 Years**
- Counted from end of calendar year of author's death

**Example:**
- Author dies on 15th March 2025
- Copyright expires: 31st December 2085 (60 years after 2025)

**B. Anonymous/Pseudonymous Works (Section 23):**
- 60 years from end of calendar year of first publication

**C. Photographs, Cinematograph Films, Sound Recordings (Section 25-27):**
- **Photographs:** 60 years from year of publication
- **Cinematograph Films:** 60 years from year of publication
- **Sound Recordings:** 60 years from year of publication

**D. Government Works (Section 28):**
- 60 years from year of publication

**E. Works of International Organizations (Section 28A):**
- 60 years from year of publication

**After Copyright Expires:**
- Work enters PUBLIC DOMAIN
- Anyone can use freely (no permission, no payment)

## Rights of Copyright Owner

### Economic Rights (Section 14)

**1. Reproduction Right**
- Make copies (print, photocopy, scan, digitize)

**2. Adaptation Right**
- Translate, convert format
- Dramatization (novel to film)
- Version for children

**3. Public Performance Right**
- Perform play, recite poem, screen film

**4. Communication to Public**
- Broadcast on TV/radio
- Internet streaming, upload

**5. Distribution Right**
- Sell, rent, lend copies

**Example:**
- Publishing house wants to publish book
- Needs reproduction + distribution rights
- Must obtain license from author

### Moral Rights (Section 57)

**Section 57:**
```
"Independently of author's copyright, author has right:
(a) to claim authorship (paternity right), AND
(b) to restrain/claim damages for distortion, mutilation, modification, or 
    other action prejudicial to honor or reputation (integrity right)"
```

**Moral Right 1: PATERNITY (Attribution)**
- Right to be identified as author
- Name must appear on copies, credits

**Violation Example:**
- Publisher publishes book without author's name → Violation

**Moral Right 2: INTEGRITY**
- Right to object to distortion/mutilation/modification
- Work must not be altered prejudicially

**Violation Example:**
- Film producer cuts scenes from director's film, distorting message → Violation

**Characteristics of Moral Rights:**
- INALIENABLE (cannot be transferred/sold)
- Personal to author (not transferable, even if copyright assigned)
- Persist even after economic rights assigned
- But can be WAIVED

**Duration of Moral Rights:**
- Same as copyright duration (lifetime + 60 years)
- After author's death: Legal representatives can enforce

## Assignment and Licensing

### Section 18-19A: Transfer of Copyright

**Two Methods to Transfer Rights:**

### A. Assignment (Section 18)

**Assignment = Transfer of Ownership of Copyright**

**Requirements:**
1. **In Writing** - Signed by assignor (copyright owner)
2. **Specific Rights** - Identify rights assigned (reproduction, distribution, etc.)
3. **Territorial Extent** - India, worldwide, specific countries
4. **Period** - Duration (5 years, perpetual, etc.)
5. **Royalty** - If applicable (amount, percentage)

**Section 19 - Assignment Not Valid Unless:**
- In writing
- Signed by assignor
- Specifies rights, territory, period
- If no period specified: Assignment deemed for 5 YEARS only

**Example - Assignment Clause:**

```markdown
The Author hereby assigns to the Publisher the entire copyright in the Work, 
including the exclusive rights of reproduction, distribution, adaptation, 
and communication to public, throughout the world, for the full term of 
copyright, in consideration of royalty of 10% of net sales.
```

**Types of Assignment:**

**1. Complete Assignment:**
- All rights transferred
- Assignee becomes owner

**2. Partial Assignment:**
- Specific rights only (e.g., print rights, not film rights)
- Assignor retains other rights

**3. Exclusive Assignment:**
- Only assignee can exercise rights
- Even assignor cannot exercise

**4. Non-Exclusive Assignment:**
- Assignee can exercise rights
- Assignor can also license to others

### B. License (Section 30)

**License = Permission to Use (Ownership NOT Transferred)**

**Exclusive License (Section 2(j)):**
- Only licensee can exercise rights
- Copyright owner cannot exercise OR grant to others
- Must be in writing (Section 30)

**Non-Exclusive License:**
- Licensee can use
- Copyright owner can also use AND license to multiple others
- Can be oral OR written (but written preferable)

**Implied License:**
- Arises from conduct/circumstances
- Example: Hiring photographer for wedding → Implied license to use photos

**Section 30 - Exclusive License Requirements:**
- Must be in writing
- Signed by copyright owner OR authorized agent

**Difference:**

| Aspect | Assignment | License |
|--------|------------|---------|
| **Ownership** | Transferred | Retained by licensor |
| **Exclusivity** | Can be exclusive/non-exclusive | Can be exclusive/non-exclusive |
| **Formality** | MUST be in writing | Exclusive: Must be in writing; Non-exclusive: Can be oral |
| **Rights** | All or specified rights | Permission to use |
| **Standing to Sue** | Assignee can sue | Exclusive licensee can sue (Section 55) |

## Exceptions and Limitations (Fair Dealing)

### Section 52: Acts Not Constituting Infringement

**Fair Dealing Provisions (Section 52(1)):**

**1. Private or Personal Use (Section 52(1)(a))**
- Making single copy for private use
- Research, criticism, review

**Conditions:**
- Must be fair dealing
- No commercial purpose
- One copy only

**Example (Fair):**
- Photocopy chapter from library book for study → Fair dealing
- Photocopy entire textbook to avoid buying → NOT fair dealing (excessive)

**2. Criticism or Review (Section 52(1)(a))**
- Using work to critique/review
- Must acknowledge source

**Example:**
- Book reviewer quotes paragraphs in review → Fair dealing
- Film critic shows clips in review → Fair dealing

**3. Reporting Current Events (Section 52(1)(b))**
- Newspaper/magazine/broadcast reporting news

**Example:**
- News report using photograph from original source (with attribution) → Fair

**4. Educational Use (Section 52(1)(i))**
- Reproduction by teacher/pupil during instruction
- In course of educational activities

**Conditions:**
- Not for commercial purpose
- Extent reasonable

**5. Library/Research (Section 52(1)(n), (o))**
- Library making copies for research/private study
- One article from periodical OR reasonable portion of work

**6. Judicial Proceedings (Section 52(1)(q))**
- Reproduction for judicial proceedings
- Report of judicial proceedings

**Fair Use Test (Derived from Case Law):**

**Four Factors:**

1. **Purpose and Character of Use**
   - Commercial vs educational/research?
   - Transformative use?

2. **Nature of Copyrighted Work**
   - Factual or creative?
   - Published or unpublished?

3. **Amount and Substantiality**
   - How much used? (percentage)
   - Qualitatively important?

4. **Effect on Market**
   - Substitute for original?
   - Harm commercial value?

**Example Analysis:**

**Scenario:** Student photocopies 3 chapters (out of 20) from textbook for exam preparation

Factor 1: Educational purpose ✓  
Factor 2: Published textbook (factual) ✓  
Factor 3: 15% of work (reasonable) ✓  
Factor 4: No market harm (temporary use) ✓

**Likely:** Fair dealing

**Scenario:** Business photocopies entire technical manual for all 50 employees to avoid buying copies

Factor 1: Commercial purpose ✗  
Factor 2: Published work  
Factor 3: 100% (entire work) ✗  
Factor 4: Substitutes for purchasing → Market harm ✗

**Likely:** NOT fair dealing (infringement)

## Copyright Infringement

### Section 51: Infringement Defined

**Infringement = Doing, Without License, Act that Copyright Owner Has Exclusive Right to Do**

**Direct Infringement:**
- Reproducing work without permission
- Distributing copies
- Public performance without permission
- Communicating to public (e.g., uploading to internet)

**Indirect Infringement (Section 51(a)(ii)):**
- Permitting place to be used for infringing performance
- Selling, distributing infringing copies
- Importing infringing copies

**Example:**
- A copies B's novel, publishes 1000 copies, sells them
- Infringements: Reproduction (copying) + Distribution (selling)

**Knowledge Requirement (Commercial Dealings):**
- For selling/distributing: Must know OR have reason to believe copies are infringing
- Section 51(b)

**Innocent Infringer Defense:**
- If defendant did not know and had no reasonable grounds to know work was protected
- Damages may be reduced (but injunction still granted)

### Remedies for Infringement (Section 55)

**Civil Remedies:**

**1. Injunction (Section 55(1)(a))**
- Permanent injunction (restraining future infringement)
- Interim/temporary injunction (pending trial)

**Example:**
- Copyright owner discovers pirate copies circulating
- Files suit for injunction to stop further distribution

**2. Damages (Section 55(1)(a))**
- Compensatory damages (actual loss)
- Account of profits (defendant's profit from infringement)

**Calculation:**
- Plaintiff elects: Damages OR Account of profits (not both)

**Damages Measure:**
- Lost sales (how many copies plaintiff could have sold)
- License fee (what defendant should have paid)

**3. Delivery Up (Section 55(1)(b))**
- Infringing copies delivered to plaintiff for destruction
- Plates, masters used for infringement destroyed

**Example:**
- Pirate CDs seized and delivered to copyright owner for destruction

**4. Conversion Damages (Section 55(1)(b))**
- Damages for conversion (wrongful possession of infringing goods)

**Criminal Remedies (Section 63):**

**Infringement as Criminal Offense:**
- Imprisonment: Minimum 6 months, maximum 3 YEARS
- Fine: Minimum Rs. 50,000, maximum Rs. 2,00,000

**Cognizable Offense:**
- Police can arrest without warrant (for repeat offenders)
- First offense: Non-cognizable (police cannot arrest without warrant)

**Section 64 - Enhanced Penalty for Second Offense:**
- Imprisonment: Minimum 1 year, maximum 3 years
- Fine: Minimum Rs. 1,00,000, maximum Rs. 2,00,000

**Section 65 - Seizure of Infringing Copies:**
- Police can seize infringing copies
- Without warrant (if satisfied infringement)

## Digital Copyright Issues

### Section 65A-65B: Technological Protection Measures and Rights Management Information

**Section 65A - Protection of Technological Measures:**

```
"Prohibition Against Circumvention of Effective Technological Measures"
```

**Technological Protection Measure (TPM):**
- Technology/device/component preventing unauthorized access/use
- Examples: Encryption, password protection, DRM, geo-blocking

**Section 65A:**
- Circumventing TPM = Offense
- Manufacturing/selling circumvention devices = Offense

**Example:**
- DVD encrypted with region code (TPM)
- Breaking encryption to play DVD from another region → Violation of Section 65A

**Penalty:**
- Imprisonment up to 2 years
- Fine up to Rs. 3,00,000

**Section 65B - Protection of Rights Management Information:**

**Rights Management Information (RMI):**
- Information identifying work, author, owner, terms of use
- Copyright notice, author name, watermark

**Section 65B:**
- Removing/altering RMI with intent to conceal infringement = Offense
- Distributing work with RMI removed = Offense

**Example:**
- Photograph with photographer's watermark
- Removing watermark before posting online → Violation of Section 65B

**Penalty:**
- Imprisonment up to 2 years
- Fine up to Rs. 3,00,000

### Section 52(1)(c) - Internet Service Provider Safe Harbor

**ISP Liability for User Infringement:**

**General Rule:**
- ISP may be liable for hosting infringing content uploaded by users

**Exception - Safe Harbor (Section 52(1)(c)):**

ISP NOT liable if:
1. ISP is mere intermediary (hosting/transmitting)
2. ISP has NO actual knowledge of infringement
3. On obtaining knowledge, ISP expeditiously removes/disables access
4. ISP does not financially benefit directly from infringement

**Notice and Takedown:**
- Copyright owner sends notice to ISP (informing of infringement)
- ISP must promptly remove content OR disable access
- If ISP complies: No liability

**Example:**
- User uploads pirated movie to video-sharing platform
- Platform (ISP) unaware initially
- Copyright owner sends takedown notice
- Platform removes video within 24 hours
- Platform NOT liable (safe harbor protection)

**Failure to Takedown:**
- If ISP ignores notice and does not remove content
- Safe harbor lost
- ISP liable for infringement (contributory infringement)

## Compulsory Licensing (Section 31, 31A, 31D)

### Section 31 - Compulsory License for Published Works

**After 3 Years from Publication:**

Any person can apply to Copyright Board for compulsory license if:
1. Work published in India OR outside India
2. 3 years elapsed since publication
3. Copies not available to public at reasonable price OR work withheld from public

**Copyright Board May Grant License:**
- If satisfied work not available OR withheld
- Applicant must pay royalty (fixed by Board)
- License NON-EXCLUSIVE

**Example:**
- Academic textbook published 5 years ago
- Out of print, not available in market
- Educational publisher applies for compulsory license
- Board grants license, fixes royalty at 5%

### Section 31A - Compulsory License for Benefit of Disabled

**For Works in Accessible Format:**

Any person can apply for compulsory license to produce work in format accessible to disabled persons

**Formats:**
- Braille
- Audio books
- Large print
- Digitized formats

**Conditions:**
- Work not already available in accessible format
- License for non-commercial purposes
- Royalty fixed by Board (may be nominal)

### Section 31D - Statutory License for Broadcasting

**Cover Versions of Sound Recordings:**

Once sound recording of musical work published:
- Any person can make new sound recording (cover version)
- By giving notice to copyright owner + paying royalty

**Royalty Rate:**
- As per Copyright Board OR as agreed

**Conditions:**
- Original recording must be published in India
- Cover version for same musical work
- Cannot alter lyrics/music fundamentally
- Notice given to owner
- Royalty paid

**Example:**
- Original song by Singer A released by Music Label B
- Singer C wants to record cover version
- C gives notice to B + pays royalty
- C can record and release cover (statutory license)

## Registration of Copyright (Section 45)

**Copyright Registration is OPTIONAL**

**Copyright Arises Automatically:**
- Upon creation and fixation
- Registration NOT required for protection

**Benefits of Registration:**

1. **Prima Facie Evidence (Section 48)**
   - Certificate of registration = Prima facie evidence of:
     - Ownership
     - Particulars in certificate

2. **Evidential Value:**
   - Shifts burden of proof to challenger
   - Useful in infringement suits

3. **Public Notice:**
   - Entry in Copyright Register
   - Third parties can search

4. **Statutory Damages:**
   - Easier to claim statutory damages if registered

**Registration Procedure:**

1. **Application** - Form XIV (literary/dramatic/musical/artistic) or Form XV (films/sound recordings)
2. **Documents** - Copies of work, proof of ownership, applicant's identity
3. **Fee** - Prescribed fee (varies by work category)
4. **Diary Number** - Application given diary number
5. **Examination** - Copyright Office examines (30-60 days)
6. **Objections** - If any discrepancies, objections issued
7. **Reply** - Applicant replies within 30 days
8. **Registration** - If satisfied, registration granted
9. **Certificate** - Certificate of registration issued
10. **Extract in Copyright Journal** - Published in Copyright Office Journal

**Timeline:**
- Usually 6-12 months (if no objections)

**Grounds for Refusal:**
- Work not original
- Work not copyrightable
- Ownership disputed
- Previous registration exists

## International Copyright Protection

### Berne Convention for Protection of Literary and Artistic Works

**India is Member of Berne Convention**

**Key Principles:**

**1. National Treatment:**
- Works of foreign authors (from Berne member countries) protected in India
- Indian authors' works protected in all Berne member countries
- Same rights as nationals

**Example:**
- US author's book automatically protected in India (no formality)
- Indian author's book automatically protected in US, UK, France, etc.

**2. Automatic Protection:**
- No formality required (no registration)
- Copyright arises on creation

**3. Minimum Term:**
- Berne Convention minimum: Lifetime + 50 years
- India provides: Lifetime + 60 years (exceeds minimum)

**4. Moral Rights:**
- Paternity and integrity rights recognized

### TRIPS Agreement (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights)

**India is WTO Member → TRIPS Obligations**

**TRIPS Requires:**
- Copyright protection for computer programs (literary works)
- Copyright protection for databases (if original)
- Rental rights for computer programs, cinematograph films
- Term: Minimum 50 years for most works (India provides 60)

**TRIPS Article 9:**
- Incorporates Berne Convention substantive provisions

## Common Scenarios and Solutions

### Scenario 1: Employee Creates Software - Who Owns Copyright?

**Facts:**
- Software engineer employed by Company A
- Engineer develops software application during office hours
- Using company resources

**Legal Analysis:**

**Section 17(a) - Work for Hire:**
- Software created in course of employment
- Under contract of service

**Ownership:**
- Company A owns copyright (employer)
- Engineer has NO copyright (absent contrary agreement)

**If Freelancer:**
- Not employee (independent contractor)
- Freelancer owns copyright (unless assignment)

**Recommendation:**
- Companies should have employment agreements with express copyright assignment clauses

---

### Scenario 2: Fair Dealing Defense - Student Photocopies Textbook

**Facts:**
- Student photocopies 5 chapters (out of 10) from textbook
- For exam preparation
- Publisher sues for infringement

**Student's Defense:**

**Section 52(1)(a) - Fair Dealing for Private Study:**
- Private or personal use exception

**Fair Dealing Test:**
1. Purpose: Educational ✓
2. Nature: Published textbook
3. Amount: 50% (substantial) ✗
4. Market Effect: Substitutes for purchase ✗

**Likely Outcome:**
- 50% is excessive
- Defeats publisher's market
- NOT fair dealing (infringement)

**If Only 1-2 Chapters (10-20%):**
- Likely fair dealing ✓

---

### Scenario 3: Photographer Hired for Wedding - Photo Ownership

**Facts:**
- Bride and groom hire Photographer X for wedding
- Photographer takes 500 photos
- Delivers album to couple
- Later, photographer uses photos in advertising
- Couple objects

**Legal Analysis:**

**Section 17(b) - Commissioned Photographs:**
```
"In case of photograph taken under commission, the person who commissioned 
the photograph is the first owner of copyright"
```

**Ownership:**
- Bride and groom (commissioners) own copyright
- Photographer does NOT own copyright

**Photographer's Use in Advertising:**
- Without couple's permission → Infringement

**Couple's Remedies:**
- Injunction to stop advertising use
- Damages

**If Photographer Wants to Use:**
- Must obtain written permission from couple
- OR contract at outset to retain copyright

**Modern Practice:**
- Photography contracts often reserve photographer's right to use for portfolio/advertising
- Express clause: "Photographer retains right to use photos for advertising, subject to privacy"

---

### Scenario 4: Copyright Infringement on Social Media - ISP Liability

**Facts:**
- User uploads pirated movie to video-sharing platform
- Copyright owner discovers infringement
- Sends takedown notice to platform
- Platform takes 15 days to remove video
- Meanwhile, video viewed 100,000 times

**Legal Analysis:**

**ISP Liability:**

**Section 52(1)(c) - Safe Harbor:**
- Platform is intermediary
- Conditions for safe harbor:
  - No actual knowledge (initially) ✓
  - On obtaining knowledge, expeditiously removes ✓ (15 days reasonable?)
  - No financial benefit directly from this video ✓

**Expeditiously Removed?**
- 15 days may be excessive (should be 24-48 hours)
- If unreasonable delay → Safe harbor questionable

**Outcome:**
- If court finds 15 days unreasonable → Platform liable (contributory infringement)
- If 15 days reasonable (considering manual review) → Platform NOT liable

**Best Practice for Platforms:**
- Automated notice-and-takedown system
- Remove within 24 hours of notice
- Counter-notice procedure (if user disputes)

---

### Scenario 5: Moral Rights Violation - Film Editing

**Facts:**
- Director A makes film, assigns copyright to Producer B
- Producer edits film heavily, removing key scenes
- Director's artistic vision distorted
- Director sues for moral rights violation

**Legal Analysis:**

**Section 57 - Moral Rights:**

**Right of Integrity:**
- Director has right to object to distortion/mutilation/modification prejudicial to honor/reputation

**Assignment ≠ Moral Rights Waived:**
- Economic rights assigned to Producer (copyright)
- Moral rights remain with Director (inalienable)

**Director's Remedies:**
- Injunction to restrain release of edited version
- Damages for violation of integrity right
- Mandatory injunction to restore original version

**Producer's Defense:**
- Edits necessary for commercial viability
- Director consented (if written waiver exists)
- Changes not prejudicial to honor/reputation

**Waiver of Moral Rights:**
- Director could have waived moral rights in writing
- Common in film industry contracts
- But must be express and clear

### Scenario 6: Compulsory License - Out-of-Print Textbook

**Facts:**
- Classic textbook published in 1995
- Author deceased (2005)
- Copyright held by Legal Heirs
- Book out of print for 8 years
- Educational publisher wants to reprint

**Legal Analysis:**

**Section 31 - Compulsory License:**

**Conditions Satisfied:**
1. Work published (1995) ✓
2. 3+ years since publication ✓
3. Copies not available to public OR withheld ✓

**Procedure:**
1. Publisher applies to Copyright Board
2. Notice to copyright owner (legal heirs)
3. Hearing
4. If Board satisfied work not available
5. Grant compulsory license
6. Fix royalty (e.g., 5-10%)

**Terms of License:**
- Non-exclusive (heirs can also publish)
- Territory (India only OR worldwide - as determined by Board)
- Period (5-10 years)
- Royalty payable to heirs

**Heirs' Position:**
- Can oppose (argue work available OR in process of reprint)
- If unsuccessfully oppose → Receive royalty

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Assuming Ideas are Protected
**Consequence:** No copyright protection for ideas/concepts
**Solution:** Understand only expression protected, not ideas

### 2. Not Obtaining Written Assignment
**Consequence:** Ownership disputes; assignment invalid (Section 19)
**Solution:** Always execute written assignment/license agreement

### 3. Assuming Registration Required for Protection
**Consequence:** Delay in enforcing rights
**Solution:** Copyright arises automatically; registration optional but advisable

### 4. Work for Hire - Employee Retains Copyright
**Consequence:** Employer does not own work created by employee
**Solution:** Section 17(a) - Employer owns (but have express clause in employment contract)

### 5. Excessive Fair Dealing Claim
**Consequence:** Infringement liability despite fair use defense
**Solution:** Limit copying to reasonable portion; consider four factors

### 6. Ignoring Moral Rights in Assignment
**Consequence:** Author can still claim integrity/attribution despite assignment
**Solution:** Obtain written waiver of moral rights (if necessary)

### 7. ISP Not Acting on Takedown Notice
**Consequence:** Loss of safe harbor; contributory infringement liability
**Solution:** Promptly remove content on receiving valid notice (24-48 hours)

### 8. Failing to Specify Rights in License
**Consequence:** Ambiguity, disputes over scope
**Solution:** Clearly specify: Rights, territory, period, exclusivity, royalty

### 9. Circumventing TPMs
**Consequence:** Criminal liability under Section 65A
**Solution:** Respect technological protection measures; obtain authorization

### 10. Not Conducting Copyright Search Before Use
**Consequence:** Unknowing infringement
**Solution:** Search Copyright Register; verify ownership; obtain permission

# Indian IP Law: Industrial Designs Protection

## Purpose
Comprehensive guidance on Designs Act 2000 covering design registrability, application procedure, term, infringement, piracy remedies, and design protection strategy for aesthetic/functional product features.

## Activation Triggers
Use when: Product design protection needed | Design registration application | Design infringement/piracy | Copyright vs design overlap | Functionality vs aesthetics distinction | Design search and clearance | International design filing (Hague Agreement) | Design portfolio strategy | Prior art assessment.

## Core Principles

### Section 2(d): "Design" Defined

**"Design means only the features of shape, configuration, pattern, ornament or composition of lines or colors applied to any article whether in two dimensional or three dimensional or in both forms, by any industrial process or means, whether manual, mechanical or chemical, separate or combined, which in the finished article appeal to and are judged solely by the eye"**

**Essential Elements:**

1. **FEATURES** - Shape, configuration, pattern, ornament, composition of lines/colors
2. **APPLIED TO ARTICLE** - Must be applied to specific article (not abstract design)
3. **INDUSTRIAL PROCESS** - Produced by industrial means (capable of mass production)
4. **JUDGED BY EYE** - Visual/aesthetic appeal
5. **NOVELTY/ORIGINALITY** - New or original (Section 4)

**What is Protected:**
- Appearance, aesthetic features
- NOT functionality (functional features = patent subject)

**Examples:**

**Registrable Designs:**
- Shape of mobile phone ✓
- Pattern on textile ✓
- Ornamental design on furniture ✓
- Configuration of shoe ✓

**NOT Registrable:**
- Mechanical function of hinge ✗ (functional - patent)
- Chemical formula ✗ (not visual)
- Construction method ✗ (process - patent)

### Section 2(e): "Article" Defined

**"Article means any article of manufacture and includes any part of an article capable of being made and sold separately"**

**Examples:**
- Furniture, clothing, toys, utensils, vehicles, packaging

**Parts of Articles:**
- Car headlight design (separate article) ✓
- Bottle cap design ✓

### Section 4: Novelty and Originality Requirement

**Design is Registrable if:**

**(a) NEW** - Not previously published or used in any country

**Absolute Novelty Standard:**
- Prior publication/use anywhere in world destroys novelty
- NO grace period (unlike patents in some countries)

**(b) ORIGINAL** - Not previously known/used in India

**Distinction:**

| Aspect | Novelty | Originality |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| **Scope** | Worldwide | India |
| **Test** | Not published anywhere | Not known/used in India |

**Example:**
- Design published in US magazine → Not novel (worldwide publication)
- Design used privately in India (not published) → May be original

**Section 5: What is NOT Registrable:**

**(a) Not Novel/Original** - Previously published/disclosed

**(b) Comprises/Contains Scandalous/Obscene Matter**

**(c) Section 23 Emblems and Names (Improper Use) Act** - National emblems, flags

**(d) Mere Mechanical Device** - Functional features only (no aesthetic appeal)

**(e) Trade Mark/Property Mark** - Cannot register trademark as design

**Example:**
- Design consisting solely of company logo → Not registrable (trademark, not design)

---

## Design Registration Procedure

### Phase 1: Design Search

**Prior Art Search:**
- Indian Design Database (official)
- International design databases (WIPO, EUIPO)
- Product catalogs, magazines
- Internet search (images)

**Objective:**
- Assess novelty/originality
- Identify similar designs
- Evaluate registration prospects

### Phase 2: Application Filing

**Form 1: Application for Registration of Design**

**Information Required:**

1. **Applicant Details** - Name, address, nationality
2. **Article** - Description (e.g., "Table lamp")
3. **Class of Article** - Locarno Classification (32 classes)
4. **Design Representation** - Drawings/photographs showing design from all views (front, back, side, top, perspective)
5. **Statement of Novelty** - What is new/original
6. **Priority Claim** - If claiming foreign priority (6 months under Paris Convention)

**Locarno Classification:**
- International classification for industrial designs
- 32 classes (by type of article)

Examples:
- Class 06: Furnishing (furniture, mirrors, etc.)
- Class 09: Packages and containers
- Class 12: Means of transport

**Multiple Articles/Classes:**
- Single application can cover multiple designs for articles in SAME class
- Different classes → Separate applications

**Fee:**
- Application: Rs. 1,000 (individual/startup) | Rs. 4,000 (SME) | Rs. 8,000 (large entity)

**Filing Receipt:**
- Application number issued
- Priority date: Filing date

### Phase 3: Examination

**Formalities Check:**
- Application complete? (Form, fees, representations)
- Correct class?
- Prohibited matter?

**Substantive Examination (Novelty/Originality):**
- Prior art search by examiner
- Assess novelty

**Examination Report:**
- Objections raised (if any)
- Timeline: 3-6 months from filing

**Response:**
- Deadline: 6 MONTHS (non-extendable)
- Amend representations, statement of novelty
- Argue novelty/originality

**Refusal:**
- If not satisfied → Application refused
- No hearing in design examination (unlike patents/trademarks)

### Phase 4: Registration

**If No Objections OR Objections Resolved:**
- Design registered
- Certificate of Registration issued
- Published in Design Journal

**Rights Effective:**
- From date of registration (filing date)

**Term:**
- **10 YEARS** from registration
- **Extension:** Additional 5 YEARS (total 15 years max)
- Extension fee: Rs. 2,000 (individual/startup) | Rs. 8,000 (SME) | Rs. 16,000 (large entity)

**No Opposition Procedure:**
- Unlike patents/trademarks, no pre-grant or post-grant opposition
- Design directly registered after examination
- Challenges only via cancellation petition (Section 19)

---

## Rights and Infringement

### Section 11: Rights of Registered Proprietor

**Exclusive Rights:**

**(1) Apply Design to Article** - Only proprietor can apply design to article for which registered

**(2) Import/Sell/Publish Articles Bearing Design** - Prevent others from importing, selling, publishing articles with design

**Example:**
- Design for mobile phone casing registered
- Third party manufactures identical phone casing → Infringement

**Section 11(2) - Copyright in Registered Design:**

**Important Dual Protection Restriction:**

```
"Once design registered, NO copyright in features protected by design registration"
```

**Explanation:**
- Design and copyright mutually exclusive (for same features)
- If design registered → Cannot claim copyright for those features
- Prevents double protection

**Example:**
- Designer creates ornamental pattern for textile
- Registers design → Copyright in pattern extinguished (for that use)
- BUT if pattern also used in artwork (not industrial process) → Copyright may subsist

### Section 22: Piracy of Registered Design (Infringement)

**Piracy = Infringement**

**Occurs When:**

**(a) Fraudulent/Obvious Imitation of Registered Design Applied to Article in Same Class**
- Defendant's design is fraudulent OR obvious imitation
- Applied to article in same class

**Test for Imitation:**
- Compare designs (overall impression)
- Would informed user/buyer confuse?
- Exact copy not required (substantial similarity sufficient)

**Example:**
- Registered design: Ornamental vase with floral pattern
- Defendant's vase: Same shape + similar floral pattern (minor color change)
- Obvious imitation → Piracy

**(b) Importing Such Article for Sale**
- Importing infringing article → Piracy (even if not manufactured in India)

**Defense - Innocent Infringer:**
- Defendant did not know design registered
- No damages (but injunction granted)

**Section 22(2) - Prior Continuous Use Defense:**
- If defendant used design before plaintiff's registration
- Can continue use (prior user rights)

### Section 22A: Piracy as Criminal Offense

**Criminal Liability:**
- Piracy (knowing infringement) = Criminal offense
- **Imprisonment:** Up to 3 years
- **Fine:** Up to Rs. 50,000

**Conditions:**
- Intentional/knowing piracy
- First offense: Summary trial

**Example:**
- Manufacturer knowingly copies registered design
- Sells large quantities
- Criminal prosecution (in addition to civil suit)

### Remedies (Section 22(1))

**Civil Remedies:**

1. **Injunction** - Restrain further piracy
2. **Damages** - Compensatory damages OR
3. **Account of Profits** - Defendant's profits from piracy
4. **Delivery Up** - Infringing articles/tools destroyed

**Damages Calculation:**
- Proprietor's lost sales
- Reasonable royalty defendant should have paid

---

## Cancellation of Design (Section 19)

**Any Person Aggrieved Can Apply to Cancel Design:**

**Grounds:**

**(a) Previously Published** - Design not novel (prior publication before registration)

**(b) Not New/Original** - Design not new or original at registration date

**(c) Not Registrable** - Falls under Section 5 (scandalous, mere mechanical device, etc.)

**(d) Not a Design** - Does not meet Section 2(d) definition

**(e) Proprietor Not Entitled** - Applicant was not entitled (design belongs to applicant)

**Procedure:**
- Application to Controller
- Notice to proprietor
- Evidence by both sides
- Hearing
- Decision: Cancel OR Maintain

**Effect of Cancellation:**
- Design removed from register
- Treated as if never registered
- Prior actions for infringement may fail

---

## Design vs Copyright Overlap

### Section 15: Copyright in Design

**Complex Interaction:**

**Before Registration:**
- Design eligible for copyright protection (as artistic work)
- Copyright Act applies

**After Registration:**
- Design features protected by design registration → NO copyright (Section 11(2))
- Other uses (not industrial process) → Copyright may subsist

**Example:**
- Designer creates jewelry design
- Design registered → Design Act protection (industrial jewelry)
- Same design used in painting → Copyright protection (artistic work, not industrial)

**Section 15(2) - Limitation:**

If article to which design applied reproduced more than 50 times by industrial process → NO copyright in design (only design protection)

**Rationale:**
- Prevent overlap of design and copyright
- Industrial designs = Design Act
- Artistic works (not industrially reproduced >50 times) = Copyright

**Strategy:**
- For mass-produced products → Register design
- For limited art pieces → Rely on copyright

---

## International Design Protection

### Hague Agreement (Joined by India in 2019)

**Single Application to WIPO:**
- Designate multiple countries (EU, US, Japan, South Korea, etc.)
- Centralized filing, renewal, management

**Benefits:**
- Cost-effective (vs filing in each country separately)
- Simplified procedure
- One language, one currency

**Procedure:**
1. File international application with WIPO
2. WIPO examines formalities
3. Publishes in International Designs Bulletin
4. Each designated country examines (6-12 months)
5. Grant OR refuse in each country

**Term:**
- 5 years (renewable every 5 years, up to 15 years total)

### Paris Convention (6-Month Priority)

**File in India → 6 Months to File Abroad:**
- Claim India filing date as priority date
- Useful for securing worldwide protection

## Design Strategy

### 1. Selection - Protect Key Visual Features

**What to Protect:**
- Unique shape, pattern, ornamentation
- Product packaging design
- User interface (if visual features)

**What NOT to Protect:**
- Functional mechanisms (patent)
- Brand names/logos (trademark)

### 2. Timing - File BEFORE Public Disclosure

**NO Grace Period:**
- Any publication/sale before filing destroys novelty
- File design application BEFORE product launch

**Example:**
- Product displayed at trade show → Public disclosure
- File design application BEFORE trade show

### 3. Multiple Applications for Variations

**File Separate Applications:**
- Different design variations
- Different color combinations
- Different classes

**Example:**
- Furniture design in Class 06
- Textile pattern in Class 05 (separately)

### 4. Monitor and Enforce

**Monitor:**
- Market for copycat products
- Trade fairs, online marketplaces

**Enforce:**
- Send cease and desist
- File infringement suit
- Criminal complaint (if blatant piracy)

### Common Mistakes

**1. Public Disclosure Before Filing** → Destroys novelty

**2. Claiming Functional Features** → Not registrable (functional = patent)

**3. Not Filing in All Relevant Classes** → Limited protection

**4. Not Extending After 10 Years** → Design lapses

**5. Confusing Design with Trademark** → Trademark for brand, design for appearance

**6. Not Monitoring Market** → Copycat products flourish unchecked

**7. Delay in Filing Infringement Suit** → Damages increase, injunction delayed

**8. Expecting Automatic Copyright Protection After Registration** → Section 11(2) extinguishes copyright for industrial use

**9. Not Representing Design from All Views** → Incomplete protection

**10. Filing in Wrong Class** → May be refused OR limited protection

# Indian IP Law: Geographical Indications Protection

## Purpose
Comprehensive guidance on Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act 1999, covering GI registration, protection of regional products, authorized users, infringement remedies, and GI strategy for traditional/regional products.

## Activation Triggers
Use when: Regional/traditional product requires GI protection | GI registration application | Unauthorized use of GI | Authorized user registration | GI infringement action | Agricultural/handicraft product protection | International GI recognition | GI vs trademark conflict.

## Core Principles

### Section 2(1)(e): "Geographical Indication" Defined

**"Geographical indication, in relation to goods, means an indication which identifies such goods as agricultural goods, natural goods or manufactured goods as originating, or manufactured in the territory of a country, or a region or locality in that territory, where a given quality, reputation or other characteristic of such goods is essentially attributable to its geographical origin"**

**Essential Elements:**

1. **INDICATION** - Name or sign identifying geographical origin
2. **GOODS** - Agricultural, natural, or manufactured goods
3. **ORIGINATING IN** - Territory, region, locality
4. **QUALITY/REPUTATION/CHARACTERISTIC** - Attributable to geographical origin

**Examples:**

**Registered Indian GIs:**
- **Darjeeling Tea** (West Bengal) - Unique flavor due to climate, altitude
- **Basmati Rice** (Punjab, Haryana) - Distinct aroma, grain length
- **Kanchipuram Silk Sarees** (Tamil Nadu) - Traditional weaving techniques
- **Alphonso Mango** (Maharashtra) - Unique taste, texture
- **Mysore Sandalwood Oil** (Karnataka) - Specific fragrance
- **Chanderi Fabric** (Madhya Pradesh) - Traditional craft

**What Makes a GI:**
- Link between product and geography (climate, soil, tradition)
- Distinctive quality/reputation due to origin

**NOT GI:**
- Generic names (e.g., "Table" - no geographical link)
- Brand names (e.g., "Tata" - company name, not geographical)

### Section 2(1)(f): Who Can Be Registered as "Authorized User"

**Authorized User = Producer/Trader Authorized to Use GI**

**Criteria:**
- Person producing goods in geographical area
- Dealing/trading in goods originating in area
- Quality, reputation, characteristics attributable to geography

**Examples:**
- Tea estate in Darjeeling → Authorized user for "Darjeeling Tea"
- Weaver in Kanchipuram → Authorized user for "Kanchipuram Silk"

---

## GI Registration Procedure

### Phase 1: Application

**Who Can Apply (Section 11):**

**(a) Association of Persons** - Producers' association, traders' association (preferred)

**(b) Producer** - Individual producer from geographical area

**(c) Organization/Authority** - Government organization, local authority

**Example:**
- Darjeeling Planters' Association applied for "Darjeeling Tea" GI
- Association of producers (not individual tea estate)

**Application Form: GI-1**

**Information Required:**

1. **Geographical Indication** - Name/sign (e.g., "Darjeeling Tea")
2. **Goods** - Description, class
3. **Geographical Area** - Territory, region, locality (map, boundaries)
4. **Quality/Reputation/Characteristics** - What makes goods distinctive
5. **Link to Geography** - How geography contributes to quality (climate, soil, tradition, skill)
6. **Inspection Body** - Who will inspect/certify authorized users
7. **Applicant Details** - Association/producer/organization

**Fee:**
- Rs. 5,000 (application)

**Supporting Documents:**
- Statement of case (detailed description)
- Map showing geographical area
- Evidence of quality, reputation (historical use, awards, recognitions)
- Details of inspection structure

### Phase 2: Examination

**Formalities Check:**
- Application complete?
- Correct class?
- Fee paid?

**Substantive Examination:**

**Examiner Assesses:**

1. **Is it a GI?** - Meets Section 2(1)(e) definition?
2. **Link to Geography?** - Quality/reputation attributable to geography?
3. **Geographical Area Defined?** - Clear boundaries?
4. **Not Generic?** - Not common name for goods

**Examination Report:**
- Objections raised (if any)
- Timeline: 6-12 months

**Response:**
- Applicant responds to objections
- Amend statement, provide evidence

### Phase 3: Publication and Opposition

**Acceptance:**
- GI application accepted
- Published in GI Journal

**Opposition Period: 3 MONTHS**

**Who Can Oppose:** Any person (Section 13)

**Grounds:**
- Not a GI (Section 9 - absolute grounds)
- Conflicts with existing GI/trademark (Section 10 - relative grounds)
- Area not correctly defined
- Link to geography not established

**Opposition Procedure:**
- Notice of opposition
- Counter-statement
- Evidence
- Hearing
- Decision

**Timeline:** 1-2 years if opposed

### Phase 4: Registration

**If No Opposition OR Opposition Rejected:**
- GI registered
- Certificate issued
- Published in GI Journal

**Term:**
- **10 YEARS** (renewable indefinitely every 10 years)

**Renewal:**
- File renewal application before expiry
- Fee: Rs. 5,000

---

## Authorized User Registration

### Separate Application Required

**GI Registration ≠ Right to Use**

**Two-Step Process:**

**Step 1:** GI registered by association/organization

**Step 2:** Individual producers apply to become "Authorized Users"

**Authorized User Application: GI-2**

**Who Can Apply:**
- Producer in geographical area
- Trader dealing in goods from area
- Must meet quality standards

**Procedure:**
1. Apply to Registrar (GI-2 form)
2. Provide evidence:
   - Located in geographical area
   - Producing/dealing in goods
   - Compliance with quality standards
3. Inspection (by inspection body designated in GI registration)
4. If satisfied → Authorized User Certificate issued

**Fee:** Rs. 500

**Validity:** 5 years (renewable)

**Registered Authorized Users:**
- Can use GI on products
- Entitled to legal protection
- Must comply with quality standards

**Monitoring:**
- Inspection body monitors authorized users
- Ensures quality maintained
- Can revoke authorization if standards not met

---

## Rights and Protection

### Section 21: Rights of Registered Proprietor and Authorized Users

**Proprietor (Association/Organization That Registered GI):**
- Authorizes users
- Monitors quality
- Enforces rights

**Authorized Users:**
- Use GI on goods
- Prevent unauthorized use by others
- Sue for infringement

### Section 22: Infringement

**Infringement Occurs When:**

**(a) Use of GI by Unauthorized Person** - Person not registered as authorized user uses GI

Example:
- Tea producer in Assam labels tea as "Darjeeling Tea" → Infringement (not from Darjeeling)

**(b) Use of GI for Goods Not Originating from Geographical Area**

Example:
- Silk saree from Varanasi labeled "Kanchipuram Silk" → Infringement

**(c) False Representation of Origin**

Example:
- Product labeled "Made in Darjeeling" when actually made elsewhere → Infringement

**(d) Imitation/Translation of GI**

Example:
- Using "Darjiling" OR "Darjeeling-style" for non-Darjeeling tea → Infringement

**Section 22(3) - No Innocent Infringer Defense:**
- Unlike design/patent, knowledge not required
- Strict liability for unauthorized use

### Remedies (Section 28)

**Civil Remedies:**

1. **Injunction** - Restrain unauthorized use
2. **Damages/Account of Profits**
3. **Delivery Up** - Infringing goods/labels destroyed

**Criminal Remedies (Section 39):**
- **Imprisonment:** Up to 3 years
- **Fine:** Rs. 50,000 to Rs. 2,00,000

**Cognizable Offense:**
- Police can arrest (with warrant)

**Enhanced Penalty for Repeat Offense (Section 40):**
- Imprisonment: 1-3 years (mandatory minimum 1 year)
- Fine: Rs. 1,00,000 to Rs. 2,00,000

---

## GI vs Trademark

### Section 10: Conflicts with Trademarks

**GI Cannot Be Registered if:**

**(a) Identical/Similar to Registered Trademark (Prior Rights)**
- Trademark registered before GI application → Trademark prevails

**Example:**
- "Champagne" trademark registered in India (hypothetically) before GI application
- GI application for "Champagne" from Champagne, France → May be refused

**(b) Well-Known Trademark**
- Conflict with well-known mark → GI refused

**Section 24: Trademark Cannot Be Registered if Conflicts with GI**

**After GI Registered:**
- Trademark application for name identical/similar to GI → Refused

**Example:**
- "Darjeeling Tea" registered as GI
- Trademark application for "Darjeeling Tea" for tea products → Refused

**Exception - Bona Fide Use of Own Name:**
- If person/business using own name (which is same as GI) in good faith
- Can continue use (not infringement)

**Example:**
- "Darjeeling Tea Shop" owned by person named Darjeeling (before GI registration)
- Can continue use (bona fide use of own name)

---

## International GI Protection

### TRIPS Agreement (Article 22-24)

**All WTO Members Must:**
- Protect geographical indications
- Prevent misleading use
- Prevent unfair competition

**Enhanced Protection for Wines and Spirits (Article 23):**
- Absolute protection (even if no confusion)

**Example:**
- "Champagne" (France) protected globally for wine/spirits
- Cannot use "Champagne" for sparkling wine from other regions (even with qualifiers like "California Champagne")

### Bilateral Agreements

**India Has Bilateral GI Agreements With:**
- European Union
- UK
- ASEAN countries

**Protection:**
- Indian GIs protected abroad
- Foreign GIs protected in India

**Examples:**
- Scotch Whisky (UK) protected in India
- Darjeeling Tea (India) protected in EU

---

## GI Strategy

### 1. Identification - What Products Qualify?

**Assess:**
- Product's link to geography (climate, soil, tradition)
- Distinct quality/reputation due to origin
- Historical evidence of association
- Producer community (can form association)

**Examples:**

**Strong GI Candidates:**
- Agricultural products with terroir (climate/soil influence): Tea, coffee, rice, spices
- Traditional crafts: Textiles, pottery, handicrafts
- Foodstuffs: Cheese, wine, processed foods with traditional methods

### 2. Application - Association vs Individual

**Preferred: Association Application**
- Represents all producers
- Establishes quality standards
- Monitors compliance
- Stronger enforcement

**Individual Application:**
- If no association exists
- Faster (sometimes)
- Limited control over quality standards

### 3. Quality Standards and Inspection

**Critical for GI Success:**
- Define clear quality standards
- Establish inspection body
- Regular monitoring of authorized users
- Revoke authorization if standards not met

**Example:**
- Darjeeling Tea GI: Quality standards for plucking, processing, tea characteristics
- Inspection by designated body
- Ensures "Darjeeling Tea" maintains reputation

### 4. Enforcement and Monitoring

**Market Surveillance:**
- Monitor for unauthorized use
- Online marketplaces, retail stores

**Enforcement Actions:**
- Send cease and desist letters
- File infringement suits
- Criminal complaints for blatant misuse

### 5. International Registration

**Madrid Protocol (for GIs?):**
- NOT applicable (Madrid only for trademarks)

**Bilateral Agreements:**
- Seek protection in export markets
- Apply for GI registration in target countries

**Example:**
- Basmati Rice: Register GI in EU, US, Middle East (major export markets)
- Prevent misuse of "Basmati" label

### Common Mistakes

**1. Confusing GI with Trademark** → GI = Geographical origin; Trademark = Brand

**2. Individual Producer Applying (Instead of Association)** → Weaker control over quality

**3. Poorly Defined Geographical Area** → Disputes, enforcement difficulties

**4. Not Establishing Quality Standards** → GI loses value, reputation

**5. Not Monitoring Authorized Users** → Quality degradation, misuse

**6. Not Enforcing Rights** → Unauthorized use flourishes, GI diluted

**7. Not Seeking International Protection** → Misuse abroad, lost export revenue

**8. Assuming GI Registration = Automatic Right to Use** → Must apply as authorized user separately

**9. Not Renewing GI/Authorized User Registration** → Rights lapse

**10. Generic Use** → Allowing GI to become generic term (e.g., "Basmati" used generically for aromatic rice)

# Indian IP Law: Enforcement and Remedies

## Purpose
Comprehensive guidance on IP enforcement procedures (civil and criminal) across patents, copyrights, trademarks, designs, including infringement suits, remedies (injunctions, damages, account of profits), criminal prosecution, border measures (customs seizure), and practical enforcement strategies.

## Activation Triggers
Use when: IP infringement discovered | Civil suit for infringement | Criminal complaint filing | Interim injunction application | Anton Piller order (search/seizure) | John Doe order (against unknown infringers) | Customs recordation (border measures) | Domain name dispute | Online piracy enforcement | Enforcement strategy planning.

## Core Principles

### Civil vs Criminal Remedies

| Aspect | Civil Remedies | Criminal Remedies |
|--------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Purpose** | Compensate plaintiff, restrain infringement | Punish infringer, deter future violations |
| **Initiated By** | IP owner (plaintiff) | IP owner (complaint) OR Police (suo motu for cognizable offenses) |
| **Forum** | Civil Court (District Court/High Court) | Criminal Court (Magistrate Court) |
| **Remedies** | Injunction, Damages, Account of Profits, Delivery Up | Imprisonment, Fine, Seizure of infringing goods |
| **Standard of Proof** | Preponderance of probabilities (balance of probabilities) | Beyond reasonable doubt |
| **Availability** | All IP types | Copyright (strong), Trademarks (moderate), Patents/Designs (civil only for some) |

**Strategic Choice:**
- **Civil Suit:** For ongoing business competitor, seek injunction + damages
- **Criminal Complaint:** For blatant piracy/counterfeiting, seek deterrence

**Can File Both:** Simultaneously OR sequentially (civil + criminal)

---

## Civil Enforcement Procedures

### Phase 1: Pre-Litigation

**Step 1: Cease and Desist Letter**

**Purpose:**
- Put infringer on notice
- Attempt settlement before litigation
- Establish knowledge (for damages calculation)

**Contents:**
- IP rights owned by sender
- Evidence of infringement
- Demand to cease infringement
- Deadline for response (7-15 days)
- Consequences of non-compliance (litigation)

**Infringer's Possible Responses:**
1. **Comply:** Stop infringement, settle
2. **Deny:** Dispute infringement, challenge IP validity
3. **Ignore:** No response → Proceed to litigation

**Step 2: Evidence Collection**

**Critical for Successful Suit:**

**Documentary Evidence:**
- IP registration certificates
- Assignment/license agreements
- Infringing products/materials (purchase proof)
- Advertisements, websites (screenshots with timestamp)
- Sales invoices (defendant's sales)

**Expert Evidence:**
- Technical experts (for patents)
- Valuation experts (for damages)
- Survey evidence (for trademark confusion)

**Investigative Measures:**
- Market survey (purchase infringing goods)
- Online monitoring (e-commerce, social media)
- Test purchases (notarized purchase invoices)

**Step 3: Assess Forum (Jurisdiction)**

**Jurisdiction for IP Suits:**

**Patents:**
- High Court (commercial division) OR District Court (where infringement occurred/plaintiff resides)
- Section 104 Patents Act

**Copyrights:**
- District Court having jurisdiction (where plaintiff resides OR infringement occurred)
- Section 62 Copyright Act

**Trademarks:**
- District Court having jurisdiction (where plaintiff resides/carries business OR defendant resides/carries business OR infringement occurred)
- Section 134 Trade Marks Act

**Designs:**
- District Court having jurisdiction (where proprietor OR defendant resides/carries business)
- Section 22 Designs Act

**Commercial Courts Act 2015:**
- For specified value (Rs. 3 lakhs+): Commercial Division of High Court OR Commercial Courts
- Expedited procedure (strict timelines)

### Phase 2: Filing Suit

**Plaint Contents (Order VII CPC):**

1. **Jurisdiction Facts** - Establish court's jurisdiction
2. **Plaintiff's Title** - Ownership/exclusive license
3. **IP Details** - Registration number, date, class
4. **Infringing Acts** - Detailed description (who, what, when, where)
5. **Damages/Account of Profits** - Quantification (if claiming)
6. **Prayer** - Injunction (interim + permanent), Damages, Delivery Up, Costs

**Documents to File:**
- Plaint + Affidavit verifying plaint
- IP registration certificate (certified copy)
- Evidence of infringement (samples, invoices, screenshots)
- Valuation certificate (for court fees)

**Court Fees:**
- Based on valuation OR ad valorem (for injunction - notional value)
- Varies by state (0.5% to 2% of valuation)

### Phase 3: Interim Injunction (Order XXXIX CPC)

**Purpose:** Prevent ongoing infringement pending trial (may take 2-5 years)

**Application:** IA under Order XXXIX Rule 1&2 CPC

**Test for Interim Injunction:**

**Three-Fold Test (ALL Must Be Satisfied):**

1. **Prima Facie Case** - Plaintiff shows likelihood of success on merits
2. **Balance of Convenience** - Irreparable injury to plaintiff > hardship to defendant
3. **Irreparable Injury** - Damages inadequate remedy (cannot be compensated by money)

**Example:**
- Plaintiff owns registered trademark "XYZ" for footwear
- Defendant using "XYZ" for footwear (infringing)
- Prima facie case: Registered mark + identical use ✓
- Irreparable injury: Goodwill damage, consumer confusion (cannot be quantified) ✓
- Balance of convenience: Plaintiff's business damaged > Defendant can use different mark ✓
- Interim injunction granted

**Timeline:**
- Hearing usually within 2-4 weeks of filing
- Order within 1-2 months (if expedited)

**Ex-Parte Interim Injunction:**
- In extreme urgency (imminent launch, trade fair)
- Without notice to defendant
- Rare (courts reluctant)

### Phase 4: Anton Piller Order (Search and Seizure)

**Purpose:** Prevent destruction of evidence by defendant

**Requirements:**

1. **Extremely strong prima facie case**
2. **Serious potential/actual damage** to plaintiff
3. **Clear evidence** that defendant has incriminating documents/materials
4. **Real possibility** that defendant will destroy evidence before discovery

**Procedure:**
- Ex-parte application (defendant not notified)
- Court appoints Commissioner
- Commissioner searches defendant's premises (with Court Officer)
- Seize infringing goods, documents, evidence
- Inventory prepared (panchnama)

**Safeguards:**
- Defendant's counsel can be present (if available)
- Search during business hours
- No excessive force
- Female commissioner for search of female/residential premises

**Example:**
- Copyright owner suspects pirate DVD manufacturing unit
- Evidence suggests massive piracy operation
- Risk of evidence destruction if notice given
- Anton Piller order obtained
- Surprise search → Seize pirate DVDs, stampers, invoices

### Phase 5: John Doe Order (Against Unknown Infringers)

**Purpose:** Injunction against unknown/unnamed defendants

**Applicable When:**
- Infringers not identified (anonymous online infringers)
- Large-scale infringement (multiple unknown parties)

**Procedure:**
- Describe class of defendants (e.g., "all persons infringing plaintiff's copyright in Film X")
- Court issues order against "John Doe" defendants
- Advertise order publicly
- Any person infringing falls within injunction scope

**Example:**
- Movie release imminent
- Pirated copies may be distributed online by unknown persons
- John Doe order against "all persons uploading/distributing pirated copies"
- ISPs, hosting platforms put on notice

**Requirements:**
- Strong prima facie case
- Irreparable harm
- Infringement by unidentified persons likely

### Phase 6: Trial

**Stages:**

1. **Written Statement** - Defendant's defense (30-90 days from summons)
2. **Replication** - Plaintiff's reply (if needed)
3. **Framing of Issues** - Court identifies contested questions of fact/law
4. **Evidence** - Plaintiff's evidence → Cross-examination → Defendant's evidence → Cross-examination
5. **Arguments** - Oral/written submissions
6. **Judgment** - Court decides

**Timeline:**
- Normal: 2-5 years (High Court), 3-7 years (District Court)
- Commercial Courts: 1-2 years (expedited - strict timelines under Commercial Courts Act)

### Phase 7: Judgment and Remedies

**Remedies Decreed:**

**1. Permanent Injunction (Section 55 Copyright Act; Section 135 Trade Marks Act):**
- Restrain defendant from infringing permanently
- Failure to comply → Contempt of court

**2. Damages (Compensatory):**

**Calculation Methods:**

**A. Lost Profits:**
- Plaintiff's lost sales due to infringement
- Formula: (Units plaintiff would have sold - Units actually sold) × Profit per unit

**B. Reasonable Royalty:**
- What defendant should have paid for license
- Expert evidence on industry royalty rates

**C. Defendant's Profits (Account of Profits - alternative to damages):**
- Plaintiff elects: Damages OR Account of Profits (not both)
- Account of Profits: Defendant's revenue from infringing goods - Costs
- Discovery: Defendant must disclose sales, costs

**Example:**
- Defendant sold 10,000 infringing units at Rs. 500 each = Rs. 50 lakhs revenue
- Costs: Rs. 20 lakhs
- Profits: Rs. 30 lakhs
- Plaintiff entitled to Rs. 30 lakhs (account of profits)

**3. Delivery Up (Section 58 Copyright Act; Section 135 Trade Marks Act):**
- Infringing goods/materials delivered to plaintiff for destruction
- Plates, molds, masters destroyed

**4. Costs:**
- Litigation costs awarded to successful party
- Typically 20-40% of actual costs

**Punitive/Exemplary Damages:**
- Rare in India (only if malicious/fraudulent infringement)

## Criminal Enforcement

### Copyright - Criminal Provisions (Section 63-70)

**Section 63 - Infringement as Offense:**

**Penalties:**
- **Imprisonment:** Minimum 6 months, maximum 3 YEARS
- **Fine:** Minimum Rs. 50,000, maximum Rs. 2,00,000

**Second Offense (Section 63A):**
- **Imprisonment:** Minimum 1 year, maximum 3 years
- **Fine:** Minimum Rs. 1,00,000, maximum Rs. 2,00,000

**Cognizable Offense (Section 63B):**
- For second/subsequent offense: Police can arrest without warrant
- First offense: Non-cognizable (police cannot arrest without warrant)

**Section 64 - Enhanced Penalty for Second Offense**

**Section 65 - Seizure of Infringing Copies:**
- Police can seize infringing copies without warrant (if satisfied reasonable grounds)

**Procedure:**

1. **Complaint by IP Owner** - To Police OR Magistrate
2. **FIR Registered** - Police investigates
3. **Seizure** - Police seizes infringing goods (Section 65)
4. **Charge Sheet Filed** - After investigation
5. **Trial** - Magistrate Court
6. **Conviction** - Imprisonment + Fine

**Timeline:** 1-3 years (summary trial for first offense)

### Trademarks - Criminal Provisions (Section 103-105, 107-114)

**Section 103 - Penalty for Applying False Trademark:**
- **Imprisonment:** Minimum 6 months, maximum 3 years
- **Fine:** Minimum Rs. 50,000, maximum Rs. 2,00,000

**Section 104 - Penalty for Selling Goods with False Trademark:**
- Same as Section 103

**Section 105 - Enhanced Penalty for Second Offense:**
- **Imprisonment:** Minimum 1 year, maximum 3 years
- **Fine:** Minimum Rs. 1,00,000, maximum Rs. 2,00,000

**Cognizable Offense:**
- Police can arrest with warrant

**Defenses (Section 107-112):**
- Bona fide use of own name
- Innocent infringer (no knowledge, reasonable grounds)

**Procedure:** Similar to copyright criminal prosecution

### Designs - Criminal Provisions (Section 22A)

**Section 22A - Piracy of Design:**
- **Imprisonment:** Up to 3 years
- **Fine:** Up to Rs. 50,000

**First offense:** Summary trial

## Border Measures (Customs Seizure)

### Intellectual Property Rights (Imported Goods) Enforcement Rules 2007

**Purpose:** Prevent import of infringing goods

**Procedure:**

**Step 1: Recordation with Customs**

IP owner records IP with Customs authorities (online application)

**Information:**
- IP details (registration number, certificate)
- Description of genuine goods
- Particulars of authorized importers/distributees
- Infringing goods description (how to identify)

**Step 2: Customs Surveillance**

Customs officers monitor imports

**Step 3: Detention of Suspected Goods**

If Customs suspects goods infringing → Detain goods

**Notice to:**
- Importer
- IP owner

**Step 4: Verification (10 Days)**

IP owner inspects detained goods

**If Infringing:**
- File application for confiscation
- Provide indemnity bond (to protect importer if goods genuine)

**If Not Infringing:**
- Goods released

**Step 5: Adjudication**

Customs adjudicates

**If Infringing:** Goods confiscated, destroyed

**Benefits:**
- Prevents infringing goods entering market
- Cost-effective (no need to sue each importer)
- Deterrent effect

## Online/Digital Enforcement

### ISP Liability and Notice-Takedown (Section 52(1)(c) Copyright Act; IT Act Section 79)

**Section 52(1)(c) Copyright Act - ISP Safe Harbor:**

ISP NOT liable if:
1. Mere intermediary (hosting/transmitting)
2. No actual knowledge of infringement
3. On obtaining knowledge, expeditiously removes/disables access

**Procedure:**

**Step 1: Notice to ISP**

Copyright/trademark owner sends takedown notice to ISP

**Contents:**
- Owner's details, IP rights
- Infringing content URL
- Request to remove/disable access
- Declaration (content infringing, notice in good faith)

**Step 2: ISP Action**

ISP must promptly (24-48 hours) remove/disable access

**Step 3: Counter-Notice (If User Disputes)**

User claims content non-infringing → Counter-notice

**Step 4: Restoration OR Court Proceeding**

IP owner must file court suit within reasonable time OR ISP may restore content

**Failure to Takedown:**
- ISP loses safe harbor protection
- Liable for infringement (contributory infringement)

### Domain Name Disputes

**INDRP (India Domain Name Dispute Resolution Policy) - .IN domains**

**Forum:** .IN Registry (Arbitration)

**Grounds:**
- Domain name identical/confusingly similar to complainant's trademark
- Registrant has no legitimate interest
- Registered/used in bad faith

**Remedy:**
- Transfer domain to complainant OR
- Cancel domain registration

**Timeline:** 2-4 months

**UDRP (Uniform Domain Name Dispute Resolution Policy) - .COM, .NET, etc.**

**Forum:** WIPO, National Arbitration Forum

**Similar procedure to INDRP**

## Enforcement Strategy

### 1. Pre-Enforcement Assessment

**Questions to Consider:**

1. **Strength of IP Rights:**
   - Registered? Valid? Renewable?
   - Prior art issues (patents)?
   - Descriptive mark (trademarks)?

2. **Strength of Infringement Case:**
   - Clear infringement OR colorable imitation?
   - Evidence quality?
   - Defenses likely?

3. **Infringer Profile:**
   - Business competitor OR fly-by-night pirate?
   - Financial status (can pay damages)?
   - Repeat offender?

4. **Commercial Considerations:**
   - Cost of litigation vs potential recovery
   - Business relationship (supplier, distributor)?
   - Publicity impact (Streisand effect)?

### 2. Remedies Selection

**Injunction Focus:** When ongoing business harm, need to stop immediately

**Damages Focus:** When infringement ceased, seek compensation

**Criminal Prosecution:** For blatant piracy/counterfeiting (deter others)

**Multiple Remedies:** Injunction + Damages + Criminal (parallel proceedings)

### 3. Settlement Negotiations

**Often Better than Litigation:**
- Faster resolution
- Cost-effective
- Certainty of outcome
- Preserve business relationships

**Settlement Terms:**
- Cease infringement
- Destroy infringing stock
- Pay compensation (lump sum OR royalty for past use)
- License for future use (if appropriate)
- Confidentiality clause

### Common Mistakes

**1. Delay in Enforcement** → Laches defense, damages limitation

**2. Insufficient Evidence Collection** → Weak case, injunction refused

**3. Not Seeking Interim Injunction** → Infringement continues during trial (years)

**4. Underestimating Defendant's Resources** → Prolonged litigation, high costs

**5. Not Considering Settlement** → Litigation costs exceed recovery

**6. Filing Only Civil OR Criminal (Not Both)** → Leave deterrence/compensation on table

**7. Not Recordating IP with Customs** → Infringing imports enter market unchecked

**8. Ignoring Online Infringement** → Piracy proliferates on internet

**9. Not Monitoring Market** → Late discovery of infringement (damages escalate)

**10. Inadequate Damages Quantification** → Under-compensation

# Indian IP Law: Licensing and Assignment of Intellectual Property

## Purpose
Comprehensive guidance on licensing and assignment of patents, copyrights, trademarks, designs across Indian IP laws, covering exclusive/non-exclusive licenses, technology transfer, royalty structures, termination, compulsory licensing, and IP transaction due diligence.

## Activation Triggers
Use when: IP license negotiation/drafting | IP assignment transaction | Technology transfer agreement | Franchise agreement (trademark license) | Copyright licensing (publishing, software) | Patent licensing (cross-licensing, technology access) | Royalty dispute | License termination | Compulsory license | IP due diligence for M&A | International IP licensing.

## Core Principles

### Assignment vs License - Fundamental Distinction

| Aspect | Assignment | License |
|--------|------------|---------|
| **Ownership Transfer** | YES (transferee becomes owner) | NO (licensor retains ownership) |
| **Duration** | Permanent (or as specified) | Temporary (specified term) |
| **Rights Transferred** | All rights OR specified rights (permanently) | Permission to use (not ownership) |
| **Consideration** | Lump sum OR royalty | Royalty/license fee |
| **Reversion** | Generally NO (unless condition) | YES (at end of term) |
| **Standing to Sue** | Assignee can sue for infringement | Exclusive licensee can sue (usually) |
| **Formality** | Must be in writing (for all IP) | Writing required for exclusive license |

**Example (Assignment):**
- Inventor assigns patent to Company X for Rs. 50 lakhs
- Company X becomes patent owner
- Inventor has NO further rights (unless assignment limited)

**Example (License):**
- Patentee licenses patent to Company Y for 10 years, royalty 5%
- Patentee remains owner
- After 10 years, rights revert to patentee

---

## Patents - Licensing and Assignment

### Section 67-71: Assignment and Licenses

**Section 68 - Form and Effect of Assignments:**

**Requirements:**
1. **In Writing** - Must be documented
2. **Signed by Assignor** OR authorized agent
3. **Registered** - Must be registered with Patent Office (within 6 months)

**If Not Registered:**
- Assignment NOT effective against subsequent assignee/licensee
- Not admissible as evidence

**Section 69 - Effect of Non-Registration:**
- Unregistered assignment void against subsequent registered assignment/license

**Example:**
- 1st March: A assigns patent to B (not registered)
- 1st June: A assigns same patent to C (registered)
- Priority: C > B (C prevails)

**Lesson:** Always register assignment promptly

**Section 70 - Compulsory License Cannot Be Assigned:**
- Compulsory license (Section 84) non-assignable
- Tied to licensee (prevents commercialization of compulsory license)

### Patent License Types

**1. Exclusive License:**
- Only licensee can practice patent
- Patentee cannot grant to others (or use himself - if truly exclusive)

**Requirements:**
- Must be in writing
- Must be registered

**Example:**
- Pharmaceutical company licenses patented drug to Manufacturer A exclusively for India
- No other manufacturer can produce (including patentee)

**2. Non-Exclusive License:**
- Multiple licensees permitted
- Patentee can also practice OR license to others

**Example:**
- Software patentee licenses to Company A, Company B, Company C simultaneously

**3. Sole License:**
- Only licensee and patentee can practice
- Patentee cannot grant to others (but can use himself)

**Cross-Licensing:**
- Two patentees license patents to each other
- Common in technology industries (avoid infringement suits)

**Example:**
- Company A has Patent X
- Company B has Patent Y
- Both needed for Product Z
- Cross-license: A licenses X to B; B licenses Y to A

### Patent Royalty Structures

**1. Lump Sum (Upfront Payment):**
- One-time payment for license
- No ongoing royalty

**Example:** Rs. 1 crore upfront for 10-year license

**2. Running Royalty (Percentage of Sales):**
- Percentage of net sales/revenue
- Typical: 2-10% (varies by industry, exclusivity)

**Example:** 5% of net sales of licensed products

**3. Minimum Guarantee + Running Royalty:**
- Minimum annual payment (guaranteed) + royalty if sales exceed threshold

**Example:** Rs. 10 lakhs minimum per year + 3% royalty

**4. Milestone Payments:**
- Payments on achieving milestones (development stages, regulatory approval, sales targets)

**Example:**
- Rs. 50 lakhs on regulatory approval
- Rs. 1 crore on first Rs. 10 crore sales
- 2% royalty thereafter

**Factors Affecting Royalty Rate:**
- Exclusivity (exclusive = higher royalty)
- Territory (worldwide vs India only)
- Field of use (broad vs narrow)
- Patent strength (strong patent = higher royalty)
- Market size, competitive landscape

## Copyright - Assignment and Licensing

### Section 18-19: Assignment of Copyright

**Section 19 - Mode of Assignment:**

**Requirements (ALL Mandatory):**

1. **In Writing** - Must be written document signed by assignor
2. **Specific Rights** - Identify rights assigned (reproduction, distribution, etc.)
3. **Territorial Extent** - India, worldwide, specific countries
4. **Period/Term** - Duration (5 years, 10 years, full term, etc.)

**Section 19(4) - Default Term if Not Specified: 5 YEARS**

**Example:**
- Author assigns copyright to Publisher
- Agreement silent on duration
- Assignment deemed for 5 years only (copyright reverts to author after 5 years)

**Moral Rights (Section 57):**
- **CANNOT Be Assigned** - Inalienable
- Remain with author (even if copyright assigned)
- Can be waived (in writing)

**Section 18 - First Owner of Copyright in Special Cases:**

**Work Made for Hire (Section 17(a)):**
- Employer owns copyright (not employee)
- But can assign back to employee (if agreed)

**Commissioned Works:**
- Photographer/painter commissioned → Commissioner owns (Section 17(b), (c))
- Unless contract states otherwise

### Copyright License Types

**1. Exclusive License (Section 30):**
- Must be in writing
- Only licensee can exercise rights
- Copyright owner excluded from exercising rights
- Licensee can sue for infringement (Section 55)

**2. Non-Exclusive License:**
- Can be oral OR written (written preferable)
- Multiple licensees permitted
- Copyright owner can also exercise rights

**Example (Publishing):**
- Author licenses book to Publisher A (exclusive print rights - India) for 5 years
- Author licenses to Publisher B (exclusive digital rights - worldwide) for 3 years
- Different rights, different licensees, both exclusive in respective fields

### Copyright Licensing Scenarios

**Software Licensing:**
- End-user license agreements (EULA)
- Site license (organization-wide)
- OEM license (bundled with hardware)

**Publishing:**
- Print rights, digital rights, translation rights, film adaptation rights (separately licensable)

**Music:**
- Mechanical license (reproduction)
- Performance license (public performance)
- Synchronization license (use in film/video)

**Royalty Structures (Copyright):**

**Publishing:**
- 10-15% of net sales (typical for books)
- Higher for established authors

**Software:**
- Per-unit license fee OR subscription fee

**Music:**
- Mechanical royalty: Fixed per copy (or streaming)
- Performance royalty: Based on usage (radio play, concerts)

## Trademarks - Assignment and Licensing

### Section 37-48: Assignment and Transmission

**Section 37 - Trademark Assignment with/without Goodwill:**

**With Goodwill:**
- Trademark + associated business/goodwill transferred
- Assignee can use mark immediately

**Without Goodwill:**
- Only trademark transferred (no business)
- Risky (may confuse consumers)
- Registrar scrutinizes (may refuse if deceptive)

**Restrictions (Section 38):**
- Cannot assign part of mark only (e.g., cannot assign "COCA" retaining "COLA")
- Cannot assign for some goods only (if would cause confusion)

**Registration of Assignment (Section 45):**
- Assignment effective only after registration with Trademark Registry
- Application within 6 months

### Trademark License (Registered User - Section 48-54)

**Old Law: Registered User System (Abolished 2010)**

**New Law (Post-2010):**
- No "registered user" requirement
- Licensor can license freely
- BUT must maintain quality control (to prevent deception)

**Permitted Use (Section 2(1)(r)):**
```
"Permitted use means use of trademark by registered proprietor OR registered 
user OR person authorized by proprietor (subject to control of proprietor)"
```

**Quality Control Requirement:**
- Licensor must control quality of licensed goods/services
- Prevents deceptive use (consumer protection)

**Example:**
- McDonald's licenses trademark to franchisees
- McDonald's must ensure franchisees maintain quality standards (food quality, service, hygiene)
- Failure to control → May lose trademark rights

**Types:**

**1. Exclusive License:**
- Only licensee can use mark in specified territory/field
- Licensor cannot grant to others (or use himself in that field/territory)

**2. Non-Exclusive License:**
- Multiple licensees permitted
- Licensor can also use

**3. Sole License:**
- Only licensee and licensor can use
- No third-party licenses

**Franchise Agreement:**
- Comprehensive trademark license + business system
- Franchisee uses franchisor's trademark + follows operating procedures
- Royalty: Initial fee + ongoing royalty (% of sales)

**Example:**
- Subway franchise
- Franchisee pays upfront fee + 8% ongoing royalty
- Uses SUBWAY trademark, follows prescribed menu, quality standards

## Designs - Assignment and Transmission

### Section 30: Assignment and Transmission

**Similar to Patents/Trademarks:**
- Assignment must be in writing
- Registered with Design Office

**License:**
- Design Act silent on licensing
- But proprietor can grant permission to use design (contractual license)

**Exclusive vs Non-Exclusive:**
- As per agreement
- No statutory formalities (unlike patent exclusive license)

## Technology Transfer and Cross-Border Licensing

### Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) Considerations

**Royalty Payments to Foreign Licensor:**

**Automatic Route (No RBI Approval Required):**
- Lump sum payment: Up to USD 2 million
- Royalty on domestic sales: Up to 5% (without minimum)
- Royalty on exports: Up to 8%
- For franchise: 2% (domestic), 5% (export)

**Government Route (RBI Approval Required):**
- Exceeding above limits
- Sensitive sectors (defense, atomic energy)

**Restrictions:**
- Non-compete clauses (limited to duration of agreement)
- Restrictions on export (prohibited in most cases)
- Mandatory technology transfer clauses (in some sectors)

### International Licensing - Key Clauses

**1. Territory:**
- Define geographical scope (India only, Asia, worldwide)

**2. Field of Use:**
- Limit to specific applications/industries

**Example:** Patent license for medical devices (not industrial uses)

**3. Exclusivity:**
- Exclusive, sole, non-exclusive

**4. Term:**
- Duration (typically 5-10 years for technology)
- Renewal options

**5. Royalty:**
- Rate, payment terms, currency, withholding tax

**6. Improvement Grants:**
- Grant-back of improvements (licensee's improvements to licensed technology)
- Exclusive OR non-exclusive grant-back

**7. Quality Control:**
- For trademarks: Mandatory quality control provisions

**8. Termination:**
- Grounds (breach, insolvency, force majeure)
- Notice period
- Reversion of rights
- Sell-off period (for inventory)

**9. Governing Law and Jurisdiction:**
- Indian law vs foreign law
- Arbitration clause (ICC, SIAC, Delhi arbitration)

**10. Confidentiality:**
- Protection of trade secrets, know-how disclosed during license

---

## Termination of License

### Grounds for Termination

**1. Expiry of Term:**
- License expires on end date
- Rights revert to licensor

**2. Breach:**
- Non-payment of royalty
- Quality control violations (trademarks)
- Unauthorized sublicensing
- Infringement of licensor's other IP

**3. Insolvency:**
- Licensee becomes insolvent
- License may terminate automatically (if clause included)

**4. Mutual Agreement:**
- Parties agree to early termination

**5. Force Majeure:**
- Supervening impossibility (rare)

### Consequences of Termination

**Obligations on Termination:**

**Licensee Must:**
- Cease use of licensed IP
- Return confidential information
- Sell remaining inventory (within agreed period - "sell-off period")
- Pay outstanding royalties

**Licensor Can:**
- Reclaim all rights
- Sue for infringement if licensee continues use

**Survival Clauses:**
- Confidentiality (survives termination)
- Royalty payment obligations (for pre-termination use)
- Indemnification

## IP Due Diligence (M&A, Licensing)

### Due Diligence Checklist

**1. Title Verification:**
- Verify registered owner (search IP registers)
- Confirm no third-party claims
- Review chain of title (assignments, previous licenses)

**2. Validity:**
- Patent: Still in force? (annual fees paid?)
- Trademark: Renewed? Not removed for non-use?
- Copyright: Within term?
- Design: Valid registration?

**3. Encumbrances:**
- Existing licenses (exclusive/non-exclusive)
- Security interests (IP pledged as collateral)
- Litigation (pending/threatened)

**4. Infringement Risks:**
- Freedom-to-operate analysis (does licensed IP infringe third-party rights?)
- Prior art search (for patents)

**5. Geographic Scope:**
- Where is IP protected? (India only OR multiple countries?)
- Filing status in key markets

**6. Restrictions:**
- Non-compete clauses in prior agreements
- Field-of-use restrictions
- Government approvals required (for sensitive sectors)

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**1. Not Registering Assignment/Exclusive License** → Not effective against subsequent transferees

**2. Ambiguous License Scope** → Disputes over territory, field of use, exclusivity

**3. No Quality Control (Trademark License)** → Risk of losing trademark (naked license)

**4. Not Specifying Term (Copyright Assignment)** → Defaults to 5 years (Section 19(4))

**7. Unclear Termination Provisions** → Disputes on termination, sell-off periods

**8. Not Conducting IP Due Diligence** → Acquiring invalid/encumbered IP

**9. Granting Worldwide Exclusive Rights Prematurely** → Limits future expansion opportunities

**10. No Improvement Grant-Back Clause** → Licensee benefits from improvements, licensor does not

# Indian IP Law: IP Portfolio Strategy and Management

## Purpose
Comprehensive guidance on strategic IP management including portfolio development, freedom-to-operate (FTO) analysis, IP due diligence (M&A, licensing), IP search strategies, IP valuation, defensive publications, and integrated IP strategy for businesses and innovators.

## Activation Triggers
Use when: Developing IP strategy for startup/company | M&A due diligence (acquiring/target company) | FTO analysis before product launch | IP portfolio audit | IP valuation (fundraising, licensing) | Competitive intelligence (competitor IP monitoring) | Defensive publication strategy | International IP filing strategy | IP-backed financing.

## Core Principles

### IP Portfolio = Strategic Business Asset

**IP Portfolio Components:**
1. **Patents** - Core technologies, defensive patents, continuation applications
2. **Trademarks** - Brand names, logos, slogans, product configurations
3. **Copyrights** - Software, creative works, documentation
4. **Trade Secrets** - Confidential know-how, processes, customer lists
5. **Designs** - Product aesthetics, packaging designs
6. **Domain Names** - Online brand presence

**Strategic Value:**
- **Competitive Advantage** - Exclude competitors, create barriers to entry
- **Monetization** - Licensing revenue, sale
- **Defensive Shield** - Cross-licensing leverage, deterrent against suits
- **Valuation** - Enhance company valuation (fundraising, M&A)
- **Market Signaling** - Demonstrate innovation leadership

## IP Portfolio Development Strategy

### Phase 1: IP Audit

**Identify All IP Assets:**

**1. Patents:**
- List all filed/granted/abandoned patents
- Status (pending, granted, expired)
- Maintenance fees paid?
- Licensing agreements

**2. Trademarks:**
- Registered marks (all classes, territories)
- Renewal status
- Common law marks (unregistered but used)
- Domain names

**3. Copyrights:**
- Software code, documentation
- Marketing materials, website content
- Ownership (employees, contractors, third-party content)

**4. Trade Secrets:**
- Confidential processes, formulas
- Customer/supplier lists
- Protections in place (NDAs, access controls)

**5. Designs:**
- Product designs (registered/unregistered)

**IP Audit Outputs:**
- Inventory spreadsheet (IP type, filing date, status, owner, territory)
- Gap analysis (missing protection, expired rights)
- Risk assessment (third-party infringement, validity issues)

### Phase 2: Align IP Strategy with Business Goals

**Questions to Answer:**

1. **What is core technology/brand?** - Focus IP protection here
2. **What are growth markets?** - File IP in those territories
3. **Who are competitors?** - Monitor their IP, file defensively
4. **What is revenue model?** - Product sales, licensing, subscription?
5. **What is risk tolerance?** - Aggressive filing OR selective protection?

**IP Strategy Types:**

**A. Offensive Strategy (Innovation Leaders):**
- Broad patent portfolio (exclude competitors)
- Strong trademark protection (brand equity)
- Enforce rigorously (deter infringers)

**Example:** Pharmaceutical companies (large patent portfolios, evergreening strategies)

**B. Defensive Strategy (Cost-Conscious):**
- Selective patenting (core innovations only)
- Defensive publications (prevent competitor patents, avoid patenting)
- Trade secrets (for processes difficult to reverse-engineer)

**Example:** Coca-Cola (formula as trade secret, not patent)

**C. Hybrid Strategy (Most Common):**
- Patent core technologies
- Trademark key brands
- Trade secrets for supporting processes

### Phase 3: Filing and Prosecution

**Patent Filing Strategy:**

**1. Timing:**
- File BEFORE publication/disclosure (no grace period in India)
- Provisional application (12 months to file complete)
- PCT application (30 months to enter national phase)

**2. Continuation Strategy:**
- File continuation applications (additional embodiments, improvements)
- Divisional applications (if multiple inventions in single application)

**3. Geographic Strategy:**
- India (domestic market)
- PCT (international filing)
- Priority countries (US, EU, China for technology; Middle East, Africa for pharmaceuticals)

**Trademark Filing Strategy:**

**1. Classes:**
- File in all relevant classes (current + future expansion)
- Defensive classes (prevent cybersquatting, brand dilution)

**2. Variants:**
- Word mark + logo mark (separately)
- Transliterations (for brand name in different scripts)

**3. International:**
- Madrid Protocol (multi-country filing)
- Key markets based on business strategy

### Phase 4: Maintenance and Monitoring

**Maintenance:**
- Renewal reminders (patents: annual fees; trademarks: 10 years)
- Review portfolio annually (abandon non-core IP to save costs)
- Update licenses/assignments

**Monitoring:**
- **Competitive Intelligence:** Monitor competitor patents, trademarks (alerts for new filings)
- **Infringement Monitoring:** Market surveillance (online, retail)
- **Journal Monitoring:** Oppose conflicting trademark/design applications

## Freedom-to-Operate (FTO) Analysis

**Purpose:** Assess risk of infringing third-party IP before product launch

**When Required:**
- Before launching new product
- Before entering new market
- Before M&A transaction
- Before licensing technology

### FTO Analysis Procedure

**Step 1: Define Product/Technology**

Identify features, components, processes that could be patented

**Step 2: Patent Search**

**Databases:**
- India: Indian Patent Office database
- Global: Google Patents, Espacenet, WIPO, USPTO

**Search Strategy:**
- Keyword search (technology terms)
- Classification search (IPC, CPC codes)
- Assignee search (competitors' patents)

**Search Scope:**
- India (if manufacturing/selling in India)
- Export markets (if exporting)

**Step 3: Identify Relevant Patents**

Screen search results:
- Active patents (not expired, fees paid)
- Covering product features
- Territorial coverage (India)

**Step 4: Claim Analysis**

For each relevant patent:
- Read claims (independent + dependent)
- Map product features to claim elements
- Determine if ALL claim elements present in product (infringement test)

**Step 5: Risk Assessment**

**High Risk:**
- Product reads on multiple claim elements OR all elements
- Patent strong (recently granted, no prior art issues)
- Patentee actively enforcing

**Medium Risk:**
- Product reads on some claim elements (workaround possible)
- Patent validity questionable (prior art exists)

**Low Risk:**
- No patents covering product
- Patents expired OR abandoned

**Step 6: Mitigation Options**

**If High Risk:**

**Option 1: Design Around**
- Modify product to avoid claim elements
- Re-run FTO analysis for modified design

**Option 2: License**
- Negotiate license with patentee
- Pay royalty for use

**Option 3: Challenge Patent**
- File opposition (if patent recently granted)
- File revocation petition (Section 64 Patents Act)
- Risk: Litigation costs, uncertainty

**Option 4: Delay Launch**
- Wait for patent expiry (if close to expiration)

**Option 5: Proceed at Risk**
- If risk low (patent invalid, patentee unlikely to sue)
- Obtain insurance (IP infringement insurance)

**FTO Opinion:**
- Document analysis, conclusion, mitigation recommendations
- Attorney-client privilege (work product)

## IP Due Diligence (M&A, Licensing)

**Phase 1: IP Identification**

**Request from Target:**
- IP register (all patents, trademarks, copyrights, designs)

**Trademarks:**
- Renewed? (10-year term)

**Phase 4: Encumbrances and Restrictions**

**Existing Licenses:**
- Exclusive licenses (limit acquirer's freedom to use)
- Non-exclusive licenses (dilute value)
- Royalty obligations

**Security Interests:**
- IP pledged as collateral (loans secured by IP)

**Phase 5: Infringement Risks**

**Inbound:**
- Target infringing third-party IP? (FTO analysis)

**Outbound:**
- Third parties infringing target's IP?
- Enforcement history (has target enforced rights?)

**Phase 6: Valuation**

Assess IP value using:
- **Cost Approach** - Development cost
- **Market Approach** - Comparable transactions, royalty rates
- **Income Approach** - NPV of future cash flows attributable to IP

**Phase 7: Recommendation**

**Green Light:** IP clean, valuable, properly owned

**Yellow Light:** Issues identified, require mitigation (price adjustment, indemnity, escrow)

**Red Light:** Material IP issues (recommend no-go OR significant price reduction)

## IP Valuation

### Valuation Methods

**1. Cost Approach:**

**Replacement Cost:**
- How much to recreate IP from scratch?
- R&D costs, patent filing costs, marketing costs (trademarks)

**Limitations:**
- Ignores market value, commercial success
- Useful for early-stage IP (no revenue yet)

**2. Market Approach:**

**Comparable Transactions:**
- What did similar IP sell/license for?
- Industry royalty rates (2-10% for patents, 5-15% for trademarks)

**Sources:**
- Licensing databases (RoyaltySource, ktMINE)
- Public M&A transactions

**Limitations:**
- Comparables difficult to find (each IP unique)

**3. Income Approach (Most Common):**

**Discounted Cash Flow (DCF):**

**Formula:**
```
IP Value = Σ (Incremental Cash Flow / (1 + Discount Rate)^t)
```

**Steps:**
1. **Project Cash Flows** - Revenue attributable to IP (product sales, licensing revenue)
2. **Determine Incremental Cash Flow** - Revenue WITH IP - Revenue WITHOUT IP
3. **Discount to Present Value** - Apply discount rate (10-20% depending on risk)
4. **Sum PVs** - Total = IP value

**Example:**
- Patent for drug compound
- Projected revenue: Rs. 100 crores/year for 10 years
- Incremental revenue attributable to patent: 70% (Rs. 70 crores/year)
- Discount rate: 15%
- PV = Rs. 70 crores / 1.15 + Rs. 70 crores / 1.15^2 + ... + Rs. 70 crores / 1.15^10
- IP Value: Rs. 350 crores (approx)

**Relief from Royalty Method:**

**Formula:**
```
IP Value = Σ ((Revenue × Royalty Rate) / (1 + Discount Rate)^t)
```

**What royalty would company pay to license IP?**

**Example:**
- Trademark for brand
- Projected revenue: Rs. 50 crores/year
- Market royalty rate for similar brands: 5%
- Royalty savings: Rs. 2.5 crores/year
- Discount rate: 12%
- PV over 10 years: Rs. 14 crores (approx)

**4. Real Options Approach (For Uncertain IP):**

- Value of optionality (e.g., patent portfolio with multiple potential applications)
- Black-Scholes model adapted for IP

**Use Cases for Valuation:**
- M&A transactions (purchase price allocation)
- Fundraising (company valuation)
- Licensing negotiations (royalty determination)
- IP-backed financing (collateral valuation)
- Tax purposes (transfer pricing, IP amortization)

# Indian Intellectual Property Law: Patents Protection

## Purpose
Comprehensive guidance on Patents Act 1970 and Patents Rules 2003, covering patentability criteria (novelty, inventive step, industrial application), patent application procedure, examination, opposition, term and maintenance, patent rights and infringement, compulsory licensing, and practical patent strategy for inventions in India.

## Activation Triggers
Use when: Client has invention requiring patent protection | Patent infringement dispute | Patentability assessment | Patent application filing | Pre-grant/post-grant opposition | Compulsory license under Section 84 | Patent validity challenge | Prior art search | Freedom-to-operate analysis | Patent portfolio strategy | Pharmaceutical patents (Section 3(d) analysis) | Patent licensing/assignment | International patent filing (PCT, Paris Convention).

## Core Principles

### Section 2(1)(j): "Invention" Defined

**"Invention means a new product or process involving an inventive step and capable of industrial application"**

**Three Essential Requirements (ALL Must Be Satisfied):**

1. **NOVELTY** - New (not anticipated by prior art)
2. **INVENTIVE STEP** - Non-obvious to person skilled in art  
3. **INDUSTRIAL APPLICATION** - Capable of being made/used in industry

### Section 3: What is NOT Patentable

**Excluded Subject Matter:**

**(d) Discovery of Scientific Principle** - Mere discovery (not invention)

**(e) Mere Arrangement/Rearrangement of Known Devices** - No inventive step

**(f) Method of Agriculture/Horticulture** - Growing plants/animals

**(i) Algorithms, Computer Programs Per Se** - Software generally not patentable (unless technical effect beyond computer)

**(j) Presentation of Information** - Mere information display

**(k) Topography of Integrated Circuits** - Protected separately (not under patents)

**(l) Traditional Knowledge** - Indigenous knowledge

**(m) Inventions Contrary to Public Order/Morality** - Harmful inventions

**(n) Plants and Animals (Except Microorganisms)** - Living organisms

**(o) Biological Process for Production of Plants/Animals** - Natural breeding

**(p) Mathematical/Business Methods** - Abstract methods

**Critical - Section 3(d): Pharmaceutical Patents**

```
"Mere discovery of new form of known substance (not resulting in enhancement 
of known efficacy) OR mere discovery of new property/use of known substance 
OR mere use of known process unless such process results in new product or 
employs at least one new reactant, is NOT patentable"
```

**Novartis v. Union of India (SC 2013):**
- Imatinib mesylate (Gleevec) beta crystalline form
- Mere new form of known substance (imatinib)
- No enhanced efficacy
- NOT patentable under Section 3(d)

**Effect:**
- Prevents evergreening (minor modifications to extend patent term)
- Requires substantial improvement in efficacy

---

## Patentability Criteria

### 1. NOVELTY (Section 2(1)(l) - Prior Art)

**Invention is NEW if:**
- Not published in any document anywhere in the world (absolute novelty)
- Not used in India (prior public use)
- Not claimed in earlier patent application with earlier priority date

**Prior Art (Section 2(1)(j)):**
- Everything made available to public before priority date
- Worldwide publications (papers, patents, online)
- Public use in India

**Destroying Novelty:**
- Publication before filing → Destroys novelty
- Public demonstration → Destroys novelty

**Grace Period:**
- NO grace period in India (unlike US)
- Any publication before filing destroys novelty

**Example:**
- Inventor publishes paper describing invention on 1st January
- Files patent on 1st March
- Publication on 1st January = Prior art
- Patent rejected for lack of novelty

**Solution:**
- File patent BEFORE any publication/disclosure

### 2. INVENTIVE STEP (Section 2(1)(ja))

**"Feature that makes invention non-obvious to person skilled in art"**

**Test (Problem-Solution Approach):**

1. Identify closest prior art (most similar to invention)
2. Determine technical problem solved
3. Would skilled person, starting from closest prior art, arrive at claimed invention in obvious manner?

**If YES (Obvious) → NO inventive step**  
**If NO (Non-obvious) → Inventive step present**

**Factors Indicating Inventive Step:**
- Solves long-standing problem
- Unexpected result
- Commercial success (secondary consideration)
- Addresses unmet need

**Example:**
- Prior art discloses drug A for disease X
- Inventor discovers dosage modification improving efficacy 2x with reduced side effects
- Non-obvious solution (specific dosage not suggested in prior art)
- Inventive step present ✓

**Obvious Variation:**
- Prior art suggests dosage range 10-100mg
- Inventor claims 50mg (midpoint)
- Obvious to try → NO inventive step ✗

### 3. INDUSTRIAL APPLICATION (Section 2(1)(ac))

**"Invention capable of being made or used in industry"**

**Requirement:**
- Practical utility
- Reproducible
- Not abstract/theoretical

**Examples:**

**Industrial Application ✓:**
- Pharmaceutical composition (can be manufactured)
- Manufacturing process (can be practiced)
- Mechanical device (can be made)

**NO Industrial Application ✗:**
- Perpetual motion machine (impossible)
- Mathematical theorem (abstract)
- Human diagnostic method on living body (Section 3(i))

---

## Patent Application Procedure

### Phase 1: Pre-Filing

**Step 1: Prior Art Search**
- Search patent databases (Indian, global)
- Search scientific literature
- Identify closest prior art
- Assess patentability

**Step 2: Draft Specification**

**Types of Specification:**

**1. Provisional Specification (Section 9(1)):**
- Brief description (not complete disclosure)
- Filed first to secure priority date
- 12 months to file complete specification

**2. Complete Specification (Section 9(2)):**
- Full disclosure enabling person skilled in art to practice invention
- Claims defining scope of monopoly

**Complete Specification Contents:**

1. **Title** - Concise title
2. **Field of Invention** - Technical field
3. **Background** - Prior art, problem to be solved
4. **Summary** - Brief summary
5. **Detailed Description** - Full disclosure (enabling)
6. **Claims** - Define scope (independent + dependent claims)
7. **Abstract** - 150 words summary
8. **Drawings** - If necessary

**Claim Drafting:**

**Independent Claim:**
- Broadest claim (essential features only)
- Example: "A pharmaceutical composition comprising compound X"

**Dependent Claim:**
- Narrows independent claim (additional features)
- Example: "The composition of claim 1, wherein concentration of X is 10-50mg"

**Claim Strategy:**
- Broad independent claim (maximum protection)
- Narrow dependent claims (fallback if broad claim invalid)

**Step 3: File Application**

**Forms:**
- Form 1: Application for patent
- Form 2: Provisional/complete specification
- Form 3: Statement and undertaking (Section 8 - foreign applications)
- Form 5: Declaration (if applicable)

**Fee:**
- Natural person: Rs. 1,600
- Small entity: Rs. 4,000
- Entity other than small: Rs. 16,000

**Priority Date:**
- Date of filing = Priority date
- If provisional filed → Priority date is provisional filing date
- Complete specification due within 12 months

### Phase 2: Examination

**Request for Examination (Section 11B):**

**Timeline:**
- Must request examination within 48 MONTHS of priority date
- If not requested → Application deemed withdrawn

**Fee:**
- Natural person: Rs. 4,000
- Small entity: Rs. 10,000
- Other: Rs. 20,000

**Examination Process:**

1. **Patent Examiner Reviews Application**
   - Novelty search
   - Inventive step assessment
   - Industrial application check
   - Compliance with formalities

2. **First Examination Report (FER) Issued**
   - Objections raised (if any)
   - Prior art cited
   - Timeline: 12-18 months from request for examination

3. **Response to FER**
   - Applicant has 12 MONTHS to respond (6 months + 6 months extension)
   - Amend claims, argue patentability
   - Submit evidence, affidavits

4. **Hearing (If Required)**
   - If objections not resolved by written response
   - Oral hearing before Controller

5. **Grant OR Refusal**
   - If satisfied: Patent granted
   - If not satisfied: Application refused

**Timeline:**
- Total: 2-5 years from filing to grant (approximately)

### Phase 3: Grant and Publication

**Publication of Application (Section 11A):**
- Application published after 18 months from priority date
- Published in Patent Office Journal
- Public can access

**Grant of Patent:**
- Certificate of patent issued
- Patent No. assigned
- Publication in journal
- Rights effective from date of patent

**Rights on Grant:**
- Exclusive rights to make, use, sell, import patented invention (Section 48)
- 20 years from filing date (non-extendable)

## Patent Rights and Infringement

### Section 48: Rights of Patentee

**Exclusive Rights:**

**For Product Patent:**
- Prevent others from making, using, selling, offering for sale, importing

**For Process Patent:**
- Prevent others from using process
- Prevent others from using, selling, offering for sale, importing product directly obtained by process

**Example:**
- Patent on pharmaceutical compound X
- Third party manufactures compound X without license → Infringement
- Third party imports compound X from abroad → Infringement

### Section 104: Burden of Proof (Process Patents)

**Reverse Burden for Process Patents:**

If patented process for new product:
- Defendant proved to have manufactured same product
- Presumed defendant used patented process (unless proved otherwise)
- Burden on defendant to prove different process used

**Rationale:**
- Difficult for plaintiff to prove defendant's process (trade secret)
- Shifts burden to defendant

### Section 107: Infringement Exceptions

**Acts NOT Constituting Infringement:**

**(a) Private/Non-Commercial Use** - Personal experimentation, research

**(b) Bona Fide Use Before Priority Date** - Prior user rights (if used before patent filed)

**(c) Experimental Purposes** - Research on patented invention

**(d) Regulatory Approval** - Manufacturing for regulatory approval (Bolar provision)

**(e) Transit Through India** - Goods passing through India (not infringement if not sold in India)

**Section 107A(b) - Parallel Import Allowed:**
- Exhaustion of rights
- Patented product sold by patentee abroad → Can be imported into India without infringement

### Remedies for Infringement (Section 108)

**Civil Remedies:**

1. **Injunction** - Restrain infringement
2. **Damages** - Compensatory damages OR account of profits
3. **Delivery Up** - Infringing products/materials destroyed

**Criminal Remedies:**
- NO criminal remedies (unlike copyright)
- Only civil suit for infringement

---

## Opposition to Patents

### Section 25(1): Pre-Grant Opposition

**Who Can Oppose:** Any person (from publication to grant)

**Grounds:**
- Lack of novelty, inventive step, industrial application
- Not patentable under Section 3, 4
- Insufficient disclosure
- Fraud/misrepresentation

**Procedure:**
- File representation (Form 7A)
- Controller considers opposition
- Hearing (if required)
- Decision before grant

**Effect:**
- If opposition successful → Application refused
- If unsuccessful → Patent granted

### Section 25(2): Post-Grant Opposition

**Who Can Oppose:** Any interested person (within 12 months of grant)

**Grounds (Similar to Pre-Grant):**
- Section 25(2)(a)-(k): Novelty, inventive step, Section 3/4 exclusions, etc.

**Procedure:**
- File opposition (Form 7)
- Evidence filed
- Counter-evidence by patentee
- Hearing
- Decision by Controller

**Effect:**
- If opposition successful → Patent revoked
- If unsuccessful → Patent upheld

**Timeline:**
- Decision usually within 18-24 months

---

## Compulsory Licensing (Section 84-92)

### Section 84: Compulsory License (After 3 Years)

**After 3 YEARS from grant**, any interested person can apply for compulsory license on grounds:

**Grounds (Any of Following):**

**(a) Reasonable Requirements of Public Not Satisfied**
- Patented invention not available at reasonable price
- Insufficient quantity
- Not worked in India

**(b) Patented Invention Not Available at Reasonably Affordable Price**

**(c) Patented Invention Not Worked in India**
- Manufacturing not done in India (importation not sufficient)

**Example:**
- Pharmaceutical patent for life-saving drug
- Patentee only imports drug (not manufacturing in India)
- Drug priced at Rs. 1 lakh per month (unaffordable)
- Generic manufacturer applies for compulsory license
- Controller may grant license if:
  - Public requirement not met
  - Price unreasonable
  - Not worked in India

**Natco v. Bayer (First Compulsory License - 2012):**
- Cancer drug (Sorafenib) patented by Bayer
- Price: Rs. 2.8 lakhs per month
- Only 2% of patients could access
- Natco applied for compulsory license
- Controller granted license
- Royalty: 6% to Bayer
- Natco to sell at Rs. 8,800 per month

**Conditions for Grant:**

1. Applicant attempted to obtain voluntary license on reasonable terms (but failed)
2. Applicant capable of working invention
3. Public interest served

**Terms of Compulsory License:**

- Non-exclusive (patentee can also exploit)
- Predominantly for domestic market (not export-oriented, except specific cases)
- Royalty payable to patentee (determined by Controller, usually 4-7%)
- License can be revoked if conditions not met

### Section 92: Compulsory License in National Emergency

**Controller Can Grant Compulsory License Without Application:**

**Grounds:**
- Circumstances of national emergency
- Extreme urgency
- Public non-commercial use

**Example:**
- COVID-19 pandemic
- Government can grant compulsory license for vaccine/drug manufacturing
- No need to negotiate with patentee first

**Section 92A: Compulsory License for Export to Countries with Insufficient Manufacturing Capacity:**

- Pharmaceutical products
- Export to least-developed countries/importing countries with public health crisis
- TRIPS flexibility (Doha Declaration)

## Integration and Strategies

### Patent Portfolio Strategy

**1. File Early, File Often:**
- Secure priority date before publication
- File provisional (if invention developing)
- File complete within 12 months

**2. Broad + Narrow Claims:**
- Broad independent claims (maximum protection)
- Narrow dependent claims (fallback if challenged)

**3. Freedom-to-Operate (FTO) Analysis:**
- Before commercialization, search for patents covering similar technology
- Assess infringement risk
- Obtain licenses OR design around

**4. International Filing:**
- PCT application (international filing under Patent Cooperation Treaty)
- 30 months to enter national phase (India, US, Europe, etc.)
- Paris Convention (12-month priority claim)

### Pharmaceutical Patents - Section 3(d) Strategy

**Challenge:**
- Section 3(d) prevents evergreening
- Minor modifications not patentable

**Strategy for Innovators:**
- Demonstrate enhanced efficacy (not just new form)
- Therapeutic advantage (clinical data)
- Unexpected properties

**Strategy for Generic Manufacturers:**
- Challenge patents under Section 3(d)
- File pre-grant/post-grant opposition
- Prior art search

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

**1. Publishing Before Filing** → Destroys novelty (no grace period in India)

**2. Not Requesting Examination** → Application deemed withdrawn after 48 months

**3. Not Responding to FER Within 12 Months** → Application deemed abandoned

**4. Claiming Software "Per Se"** → Not patentable (need technical effect)

**5. Not Filing Complete Specification Within 12 Months of Provisional** → Priority date lost

**6. Over-Broad Claims** → Challenged for lack of support/enablement

**7. Not Considering Compulsory License Risk** → Especially for essential medicines

**8. Not Working Invention in India** → Vulnerable to compulsory license under Section 84

**9. Ignoring Prior Art During Drafting** → Claims too broad, easily invalidated

**10. Not Maintaining Patent (Annual Fees)** → Patent lapses if fees not paid

# Indian IP Law: Trademarks Protection

## Purpose
Comprehensive guidance on Trade Marks Act 1999 and Trade Marks Rules 2017, covering registrability, application procedure, opposition, well-known marks, infringement, passing off, dilution, and trademark portfolio strategy in India.

## Activation Triggers
Use when: Trademark selection and clearance | Registration application | Opposition proceedings | Infringement dispute | Passing off action | Well-known mark status | Trademark licensing/assignment | Domain name disputes | Geographical indication conflict | Comparative advertising | Parallel import issues | Trademark renewal/restoration.

## Core Principles

### Section 2(1)(zb): "Trade Mark" Defined

**"A mark capable of being represented graphically and which is capable of distinguishing the goods or services of one person from those of others"**

**Essential Elements:**

1. **MARK** - Any sign (word, logo, shape, color, sound, smell - if graphically representable)
2. **GRAPHICALLY REPRESENTABLE** - Can be depicted on paper
3. **DISTINCTIVE** - Distinguishes source of goods/services

**Types of Marks:**

- **Word Marks** - TATA, RELIANCE, GOOGLE
- **Device Marks** - Logos (Apple logo, Nike swoosh)
- **Combined Marks** - Word + logo
- **Shape Marks** - Product shape (Coca-Cola bottle)
- **Color Marks** - Single color OR combination (Cadbury purple)
- **Sound Marks** - Yahoo yodel, Nokia tune
- **Smell Marks** - Fragrance (rare, difficult to represent graphically)

### Section 9: Absolute Grounds for Refusal

**Marks That CANNOT Be Registered:**

**(a) Not Capable of Distinguishing** - Generic, descriptive terms

Example: "SOAP" for soap ✗ | "DOVE" for soap ✓ (arbitrary/fanciful)

**(b) Devoid of Distinctive Character** - Common words/symbols

Example: "BEST QUALITY" for products ✗

**(c) Descriptive Marks** - Describe characteristics

Example: "FAST" for courier service ✗ | "FEDEX" ✓

**(d) Customary in Trade** - Common in industry

Example: "THE" for newspapers ✗

**(e) Simple Geometrical Shapes** - Triangle, circle ✗

**(f) Offensive/Prohibited** - Scandalous, immoral marks

Example: National emblems, religious symbols (without permission)

**Exception - Acquired Distinctiveness (Proviso):**

Even if descriptive/non-distinctive initially, if mark has acquired distinctiveness through use, can be registered

Example:
- "YELLOW PAGES" originally descriptive
- Through extensive use → Acquired distinctiveness
- Registrable ✓

**Evidence of Acquired Distinctiveness:**
- Market share, sales figures
- Advertising expenditure
- Consumer surveys showing recognition
- Duration of use

### Section 11: Relative Grounds for Refusal (Conflict with Prior Marks)

**Cannot Register if:**

**(a) Identical with Earlier Mark for Identical Goods/Services**

Example:
- Prior registration: APPLE for computers
- Application: APPLE for computers → Rejected

**(b) Similar to Earlier Mark for Similar/Identical Goods/Services, Likelihood of Confusion**

Test: Would consumer confuse origin?

**Similarity Assessment:**
- Visual (appearance)
- Phonetic (sound)
- Conceptual (meaning)

Example:
- Prior: TIDE for detergent
- Application: TYDE for detergent → Likely confusing → Rejected

**(c) Identical/Similar to Well-Known Mark (Even for Different Goods/Services)**

Example:
- GOOGLE is well-known for search engines
- Application: GOOGLE for bicycles → Rejected (dilutes well-known mark)

## Trademark Registration Procedure

### Phase 1: Trademark Search and Clearance

**Step 1: Comprehensive Search**

**Databases to Search:**
- Indian TM Registry (official database)
- State emblems, prohibited marks
- Company names (MCA database)
- Domain names
- Common law prior use (unregistered marks)

**Search Strategy:**
- Identical marks in same class
- Phonetically similar marks
- Visually similar marks
- Translation equivalents

**Step 2: Clearance Opinion**

**Risk Assessment:**
- **Low Risk:** No similar marks → Proceed
- **Medium Risk:** Similar marks in different classes → Assess conflict
- **High Risk:** Similar marks in same class → Reconsider OR prepare for opposition

### Phase 2: Filing Application

**Application Form: TM-A**

**Information Required:**

1. **Applicant Details** - Name, address, nationality
2. **Mark** - Representation (JPG for device marks)
3. **Goods/Services** - Description + Nice Classification class
4. **Claimed User Date** - Date of first use (if applicable)
5. **Priority Claim** - If claiming foreign priority (6 months under Paris Convention)

**Nice Classification:**
- 45 classes total
- Classes 1-34: Goods
- Classes 35-45: Services

Example:
- Class 25: Clothing, footwear
- Class 35: Advertising, business management

**Multiple Classes:**
- Can file single application for multiple classes
- Fee per class

**Fee:**
- Physical application: Rs. 4,500 per class (individual/startup) | Rs. 9,000 (others)
- E-filing: Rs. 4,500 per class (individual/startup) | Rs. 9,000 (others)

**Filing Receipt:**
- Application number issued immediately
- Can use "TM" symbol (not ® - reserved for registered marks)

### Phase 3: Examination

**Vienna Codification (For Device Marks):**
- Examiner searches device elements using Vienna codes
- Identifies conflicting logos

**Examination Report:**
- Issued within 12-18 months
- Objections raised (Section 9, 11 grounds)
- Prior conflicting marks cited

**Response to Examination Report:**
- Deadline: 1 MONTH (extendable)
- Options:
  - Amend specification
  - Disclaim non-distinctive elements
  - Argue distinctiveness
  - Evidence of acquired distinctiveness/use
  - Narrow goods/services

**Hearing:**
- If objections not resolved → Hearing before Registrar
- Oral arguments
- Final decision: Grant OR Refusal

### Phase 4: Publication and Opposition

**Acceptance:**
- Mark accepted for registration
- Published in Trade Marks Journal

**Opposition Period: 4 MONTHS (from publication)**

**Who Can Oppose:** Any person (Section 21)

**Grounds:**
- Section 9, 11 grounds (same as examination)
- Prior use/reputation
- Bad faith

**Opposition Procedure:**

1. **Notice of Opposition (TM-O)** - Filed within 4 months
2. **Counter-Statement** - Applicant responds (2 months)
3. **Evidence** - Opponent's evidence (2 months) → Applicant's evidence (2 months) → Opponent's reply (1 month)
4. **Hearing** - Oral arguments
5. **Decision** - Registrar decides (grant OR refuse)

**Timeline:** 2-4 years if opposed

### Phase 5: Registration

**If No Opposition OR Opposition Dismissed:**
- Certificate of Registration issued
- Effective date: Application filing date
- Valid for: 10 YEARS (renewable indefinitely)

**Rights:**
- Exclusive use of mark
- Right to sue for infringement
- Use ® symbol

## Trademark Rights and Infringement

### Section 28: Rights of Registered Proprietor

**Exclusive Rights:**
- Use mark on goods/services
- Prevent unauthorized use by others
- Assign, license mark

**Infringement (Section 29):**

**(1) Identical Mark, Identical Goods/Services**
- Use of identical mark on identical goods → Automatic infringement (no confusion proof needed)

Example: Using "ADIDAS" on shoes (when ADIDAS registered for shoes) → Infringement

**(2) Similar Mark, Similar Goods/Services + Likelihood of Confusion**
- Must prove likelihood of confusion or association

Test:
- Would average consumer confuse source?
- Factors: Visual/phonetic/conceptual similarity, nature of goods, channels of trade

Example:
- ZARA registered for clothing
- ZARO used for clothing → Likely confusion → Infringement

**(3) Well-Known Mark + Unfair Advantage/Detriment**
- Using mark identical/similar to well-known mark
- Takes unfair advantage of reputation OR
- Causes detriment to distinctive character/repute

Example:
- MICROSOFT well-known for software
- MICROSOFT used for bicycles → Dilution of repute → Infringement

**(4) Identical/Similar Mark + Advertisement**
- Advertising goods/services under mark → Infringement (even if no actual sale)

**(8) Applying Mark to Packaging/Material**
- Affixing mark to packaging → Contributory infringement

**Defenses (Section 30):**

**(a) Bona Fide Use of Own Name/Address** - Can use own name/address if not trademark use

Example: "Johnson & Johnson" can be used by person named Johnson for goods

**(b) Descriptive Use** - Bona fide use of descriptive terms

**(c) Comparative Advertising** - Honest comparison identifying competitor's mark (conditions apply)

**(d) Prior Continuous Use** - If used continuously before opponent's registration/application

Example:
- A used mark since 2010
- B registered mark in 2015
- A can continue use (prior user rights)

**(e) Exhaustion of Rights (Parallel Import)** - Genuine goods sold abroad by proprietor → Can be imported (parallel import)

### Passing Off (Common Law)

**Even Without Registration, Can Sue for Passing Off:**

**Elements:**

1. **Goodwill/Reputation** - Plaintiff has goodwill in mark
2. **Misrepresentation** - Defendant's use causes confusion
3. **Damage** - Actual or likely damage to plaintiff's goodwill

**Remedy:** Injunction + Damages

**Example:**
- Local restaurant "Udipi Café" (unregistered, used for 20 years)
- New restaurant opens nearby: "Uduppi Café"
- Customers confused
- Original owner can sue for passing off (even without registration)

### Remedies (Section 135)

**Civil Remedies:**

1. **Injunction** - Restrain infringement
2. **Damages/Account of Profits** - Compensatory damages OR defendant's profits
3. **Delivery Up** - Infringing goods/labels destroyed

**Criminal Remedies (Section 103-105):**

- **Imprisonment:** Up to 3 years
- **Fine:** Rs. 50,000 to Rs. 2,00,000
- For applying false trademark, selling goods bearing false trademark

**Cognizable Offense** - Police can arrest (with warrant)

## Well-Known Trademarks (Section 11(6)-(9))

**Well-Known Mark:**
- Mark with substantial reputation in India
- Protected even without registration
- Protected across all goods/services (not just registered classes)

**Determination:**
- Registrar determines well-known status (on case-by-case basis)
- Factors (Rule 124):
  - Number of actual/potential consumers
  - Geographical extent of use
  - Duration of use
  - Advertising expenditure
  - Market share
  - Registration in other countries

**Examples:**
- COCA-COLA, McDONALD'S, GOOGLE, APPLE, MICROSOFT

**Protection:**

1. **Against Registration** - Can oppose registration of similar mark in any class
2. **Against Use** - Can restrain use even for unrelated goods/services (dilution)

**Example:**
- ROLEX well-known for watches
- Someone applies ROLEX for bicycles
- Even though different goods → Registrar can refuse (Section 11(6))

## Trademark Maintenance and Renewal

### Section 25: Renewal

**Initial Term:** 10 years from registration

**Renewal:** Indefinitely, every 10 years

**Renewal Application:**
- File within 6 months before expiry
- Can file up to 6 months after expiry (with surcharge)
- If not renewed → Removed from register

**Restoration (Section 60):**
- If mark removed for non-renewal
- Can apply for restoration within 1 YEAR of removal
- Show sufficient cause + pay fee

### Section 47: Removal for Non-Use (5 Years)

**Any Person Can Apply to Remove Mark if:**
- Mark not used for continuous period of 5 years + 3 months
- No bona fide use on goods/services for which registered

**Proprietor's Defense:**
- Prove use in 5 years preceding application
- OR special circumstances preventing use

**Use:**
- Must be bona fide commercial use (not token use)
- On goods/services in India (export use counts)
- By proprietor OR registered user

**Effect of Removal:**
- Mark removed from register
- Becomes available for others

## Trademark Portfolio Strategy

### 1. Selection and Clearance

**Trademark Strength Spectrum:**

**Strongest:**
- **Fanciful** - Invented words (KODAK, XEROX)
- **Arbitrary** - Common words unrelated to goods (APPLE for computers)

**Moderate:**
- **Suggestive** - Suggests quality/characteristic (GREYHOUND for fast buses)

**Weak:**
- **Descriptive** - Describes goods (CREAMY for ice cream) - Not registrable (unless acquired distinctiveness)
- **Generic** - Common name for goods (COMPUTER for computers) - NEVER registrable

**Strategy:** Choose fanciful/arbitrary marks for maximum protection

### 2. Filing Strategy

**Classes:**
- File in all relevant classes (present + future use)
- Consider defensive classes (prevent cybersquatting)

**Priority:**
- File early (first-to-file system in India)
- Consider international filing (Madrid Protocol)

### 3. Policing and Enforcement

**Monitor:**
- TM Journal (weekly) for conflicting applications
- Market for infringing use

**Oppose:**
- File opposition against conflicting applications

**Enforce:**
- Send cease and desist letters
- File infringement suits

### 4. International Protection

**Madrid Protocol:**
- Single application to WIPO (World Intellectual Property Organization)
- Designate multiple countries (India joined Madrid Protocol in 2013)
- Cost-effective for multi-country filing

**Paris Convention:**
- 6-month priority claim
- File in India → 6 months to file abroad claiming India priority date

### Common Mistakes

**1. Using Descriptive Terms** → Weak protection OR unregistrable

**2. Not Conducting Search** → Infringement risk, wasted application fee

**3. Not Filing in All Relevant Classes** → Gaps in protection

**4. Not Using Mark After Registration** → Vulnerable to removal for non-use (Section 47)

**5. Delayed Opposition** → Miss 4-month window

**6. Using ® Before Registration** → Offense under Section 139

**7. Not Renewing** → Mark removed (restoration costly)

**8. Generic Use (Trademark Becoming Generic)** → XEROX preventing "xeroxing"; GOOGLE fighting "googling" as verb

**9. Not Monitoring Journal** → Miss conflicting applications

**10. Inconsistent Use** → Using mark differently than registered (weakens enforcement)
